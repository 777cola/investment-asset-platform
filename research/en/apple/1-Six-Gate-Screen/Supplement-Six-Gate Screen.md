## Stage one to stage three: these three stages are actually a plus this year

The first gate is not in dispute. Apple's revenue has just three parts: hardware sales (USD 78.678 billion this quarter, 71.9% of total), services (USD 30.739 billion, 28.1%), and app commission (included in services). All three can be modeled separately, forecast, and cross-checked. In the AI era this is scarce — you can hardly model a model company's cash flow three years out, but you can model the iPhone replacement cycle.

Evidence for the second gate is in this quarter's statements. Global smartphone shipments rose slightly year on year in the first half of 2026, yet Apple lifted its share to 20%, with iPhone revenue up 21.7% and average selling price pushed higher again by Pro models. Services gross margin was 75.6%, contributing USD 23.2 billion of gross profit over the year, close to three-quarters of the product line. A business that combines rising share with structural upgrade is rare in a mature industry.

The third gate is the ecosystem. 2.3 billion active devices and 1.5 billion paid subscriptions: users stay not because of any single feature but because of photos, iMessage, AirPods, Watch and App Store purchase history. Switching once means replacing half your digital life, and that cost is worth more in a downturn. So the third gate scores 9: the real moat is not chip benchmarks but the hassle of switching phones.

The 1 point deducted is on the services side. The App Store commission model is being challenged in both Europe and the US: the EU's Digital Markets Act requires opening up external payments, and US courts are narrowing anti-steering clauses. This item contributes over USD 20 billion of gross profit a year, but its legal foundation is thinner than three years ago. The ecosystem is still the ecosystem, only the legitimacy of collecting rent is being renegotiated.

## Stage four: Cook passes the baton — 7 points, not 9

When Cook took over in 2011, Apple's annual revenue was USD 108 billion; in FY2025 it is USD 416.2 billion, nearly quadrupling in fourteen years. Services went from zero to an annualized USD 123 billion; cumulative shareholder returns exceed USD 800 billion. This is one of the most successful successions in business history, without dispute.

Taking over on September 1 is John Ternus, a hardware engineering background who ran product definition for Mac and iPad, with a deeper understanding of the supply chain than someone from a pure design background. The upside is that the product roadmap will not break; the downside is that he has never done capital allocation, nor dealt with Wall Street for fourteen years. What Apple needs most right now is precisely capital allocation judgment: whether to continue USD 100 billion/year in buybacks, and whether AI investment should shift from expense to capital expenditure.

So 7 points. The points go to "handover certainty" — announced a month in advance, unanimous board approval, Cook staying on as advisor; this is a textbook transition. The deductions go to "unproven" — the new CEO's first three-year plan is not out yet, and any premium should not be built on speculation.

One more historical pattern: Apple's successions (Jobs to Cook 2011, Cook to Ternus 2026) both occurred at turning points in the product cycle. 2011 was the shift from single product to ecosystem; 2026 is the shift from hardware to the AI entry point. The handover itself is not the risk; getting the direction wrong during the handover period is. The first thing to watch after Ternus takes office is the capex structure — how the money is spent on AI compute, in-house modems and foldables; these three determine the gross margin in 2028.

## Gate 5: paying 34x for 10% growth — rejected at this gate

Put three sets of numbers side by side: current price USD 303.42, market cap USD 4.41 trillion, TTM EPS of about USD 8.9 (backed out from the current price and a 34x P/E) → 34x; on the FY2027 consensus EPS of USD 10.0 → 30x; management guides next quarter to revenue +9%–11% and gross margin 47%–48%.Paying 30x for 10% growth means prepaying for 2027 AI monetization.

Where is a reasonable anchor? Apply 28x to FY2027 EPS of USD 10.0 — the midpoint of Apple's valuation over the past five years excluding the two bubble periods of 2021 and 2024 — and fair value is USD 280, 8% below the current price. Sell-side target prices now sit at USD 335–360, implying 33–35x, premised on services growth returning above 15% and AI starting to contribute revenue.Neither premise appeared anywhere in the FY2026Q3 results.

One caveat: 34x is not Apple's problem alone; it is the pricing environment for large-cap US tech as a whole. But others being expensive does not prove it is cheap. The fifth gate asks only one thing — if I buy today and it falls 30%, can I hold on and add. At USD 303, I say no.

## Gate 6: The buy thesis must be reconcilable against quarterly reports

The sixth gate does not ask "is this a good company" but "can my buy thesis be verified every quarter." Apple's bull case now rests on a single argument: Siri AI will become a new paid gateway. It was unveiled at WWDC in June and public beta feedback has been positive, but the company's own language is that "paid value-added services have yet to generate revenue at scale" — which translates to: not yet visible in the financials.

A thesis that cannot be reconciled in the quarterly report is not an investment thesis; it is a hope. So this gate scores 5: not a veto, but "wait for evidence." The verifiable path is clear enough: an "AI subscription" line appearing within services revenue, or management disclosing paying users and ARPU on the earnings call. Until then, any valuation expansion on AI is narrative-driven.

There is also a discipline issue: half of the 34x multiple the market assigns Apple is a "certainty premium." When that certainty itself starts to wobble — services decelerating, Greater China below the consensus, gross margin peaking — the premium contracts faster than profit declines. That is why the stock fell 9% in the three days after earnings — what fell was not the results, but the premium.

## Where we differ from the sell side: they have discounted 2027 AI revenue back to today

Two days after the earnings report, the moves by two major banks are worth comparing: Morgan Stanley cut its target price from a higher level to USD 360 and lowered FY2027 EPS from 10.39 to 10.00; Daiwa instead raised its target to USD 335. The two went in opposite directions, but both used the same multiple—33 to 35 times. The disagreement is not about the company, but about "willingness to pay for revenue not yet on the table."

My reason for not using 33 times is straightforward: 33 times corresponds to a combination of "services returning to above 15% + AI starting to charge," and both of these need at least two quarters to verify. Pricing on assumptions before verification is equivalent to replacing research with belief. If services really do return to 15% in 2027, then 33 times would not be expensive at that time—but I can pay up to buy after the evidence comes out.

This is why the order of the six gates matters: the first four gates answer "is it a good company," and the last two answer "should I act now." Apple scored 35 points on the first four gates (out of 40), and only 8 points on the last two (out of 20). This distribution is itself the conclusion—good company, bad timing.
