Core view

Verdict first: this industry has no growth, only share and average price. Anyone who accepts this premise will not pay 34x for Apple.

Industry landscape: five players share the market, none holding more than 20%

Global smartphone shipments will reach about 1.2 billion units in 2026, up slightly year on year (rough estimate). Share structure: Samsung about 19%, Apple 20%, Xiaomi about 14%, Transsion about 9%, OPPO/vivo combined about 16%, with the rest split among others. China's market is more clearly stratified: the above-RMB 6,000 segment is Apple and Huawei territory, while RMB 2,000–4,000 is the main battleground for Xiaomi, OPPO and vivo.

The defining feature of this landscape is that no one can eat anyone else's lunch. Apple holds the high end, Samsung holds all price tiers plus in-house memory supply, and Chinese vendors hold value for money and local channels. The three have completely different types of moat, so competition is not a price war but each defending its own price band. That suits Apple — it faces no rival in its own price band.

The real variable is foldables. Global foldable shipments will reach about 30 million units in 2026 (rough estimate), with Huawei and Samsung taking 70%, and Apple has no product yet. Foldables are not a new category but a form-factor upgrade of the high-end market — if they go mainstream, a slice of Apple's high-end share will be carved away. The market widely expects Apple to launch its first foldable in 2027, and that is a battle it must win.

Cycle position: end demand at the bottom, costs rising — the most uncomfortable combination

Look at the two cycles together. End-demand cycle: 2024–2025 was the trough (global shipments fell for two consecutive years), and 2026 warms on a replacement-driven rebound, with Apple the biggest beneficiary. Cost cycle: in early 2026 DRAM rose 60%–70% and NAND doubled, Q3 contract prices rose another 15%–20%, HBM is crowding out capacity, and institutions expect the shortage to persist into 2027.

Recovering demand plus rising costs is the worst combination for device makers: raising prices suppresses demand that has only just recovered, while holding prices lets gross margin get eaten. Apple's choice is "modest price increases plus absorbing the rest itself," and the cost is gross margin falling from 50.1% to 47%–48%.

Compare the upstream and downstream: memory makers (Samsung, SK hynix, Micron) are the biggest winners this round, with gross margin rising from 30% to above 50%; foundries (TSMC) hold 58% gross margin by raising prices on advanced nodes; assemblers (Hon Hai) sit at 6% gross margin, entirely passive. Apple sits in the middle, the only link able to pass costs on to consumers, but that pass-through has a ceiling.

Competition: Apple's real threat is not Samsung, but Huawei and the Chinese supply chain

Samsung leads global shipments, but it is losing high-end share, and its AI strategy (rolling Galaxy AI across the full lineup) has not created differentiation. Google's Pixel share is small; the threat is not hardware but "Gemini free for Android" — which turns AI into a public good for the Android camp and prevents Apple from charging a premium for AI.

Huawei is the real problem. In China's above-RMB 6,000 market, Huawei has taken a substantial share with foldables plus the HarmonyOS ecosystem, and its chips (Kirin) have returned to 7nm-class in-house supply.Apple's Greater China revenue this quarter was USD 18.816 billion, up 22.4% year on year, but below the consensus of 3.9% — that gap is what Huawei took.

The Munger-style question: what will this industry look like in ten years? Most likely "more concentrated, more expensive, slower." Phones will not disappear, but they will not grow again either; AI hardware (glasses, earbuds, pins) may create new categories, but Apple is not the leader in those directions.The most profitable company in a non-growing industry deserves 20x, not 34x.

One industry variable that is often underestimated: the geographic migration of capacity. To hedge tariffs, Apple is moving assembly to India and Vietnam, with India accounting for less than 20% of iPhone assembly in 2026 (rough estimate).The cost of migration is yield and supporting infrastructure — engineer density, tooling supply and power stability at Indian plants are all far below Zhengzhou, and this cost will keep shaving 0.5–1 point off gross margin over the next two to three years. No one in the industry can sidestep this; Apple is simply the one best able to pay for it.

Conclusion

Industry verdict: 6/10. Apple has no rival in its own price band, but industry volume is not growing, the cost cycle is turning up, and neither of the two new battlegrounds—foldables and AI hardware—is its home turf. This is an industry position of "can hold, cannot grow fast."
