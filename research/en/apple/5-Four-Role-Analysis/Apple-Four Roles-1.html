Business analysis: the essence of this business is "collecting rent on experience" — Apple

Core view

Verdict first: this is the best consumer-electronics business in the world, and there is no second. Its essence is not selling hardware but collecting "rent on experience"—users pay a premium for "not having to think, not having to learn, not having to worry," and hardware is merely the vehicle for charging that rent.

Revenue structure: three legs, two of them rental

Revenue this quarter was USD 109.417 billion, broken down as: iPhone USD 54.252 billion (49.6%), Services USD 30.739 billion (28.1%), Mac USD 10.352 billion (9.5%), Wearables and Home USD 7.883 billion (7.2%), iPad USD 6.191 billion (5.7%). Hardware is sold once, services are billed for life—that is what "rent" means.

The quality of the services line deserves separate scrutiny: 75.6% gross margin, USD 123 billion in annualized revenue, almost zero capital expenditure. It comprises four pieces: App Store commissions (15%–30%), subscriptions (iCloud, Music, TV+), default search placement (Google pays roughly USD 20 billion a year), and advertising (Search Ads and App Store ads). Two of the four have their legal foundations under challenge—that is the hidden risk in the business model.

The good news on hardware is that the mix is shifting upward. iPhone revenue rose 21.7%, but unit shipments grew only in the single digits; all the incremental revenue came from a higher share of Pro and Pro Max. Mac rose 28.7%, a direct result of the M5 refresh—the gross profit on one MacBook Pro equals that of three iPhone SEs. What Apple has done over the past five years is essentially "sell more expensive things to the same user."

Why users pay a premium: not features, but switching costs

Ask one question: where is the iPhone better than an Android flagship at the same price? On specs alone, the answer gets blurrier every year—screens, cameras, fast charging: Android is not worse, and in some areas ahead. Yet users still buy. The reason is not features, it is switching cost: photos sit in iCloud, chats in iMessage, the watch and earbuds are already paired, subscriptions live in the Apple ID, family members are in Family Sharing.

How high is that cost? Switching phones means logging back into twenty apps, migrating twenty thousand photos, re-pairing three devices, and teaching the family to use everything again. For most people, that hassle is worth USD 200—exactly the price gap between an iPhone and a comparable Android handset. This is the source of Apple's pricing power, and the fundamental reason it can still raise prices while the overall market shrinks.

But switching cost has a ceiling: it only works on people already inside the ecosystem. New users carry no sunk cost, which is why Apple's share in India, Africa and Southeast Asia has never climbed—users in those markets are buying their first phone and choose on value for money. This means Apple's growth must come from raising prices on existing users, not from new users, which also explains why its revenue growth ceiling sits at 8%–10%.

The two weak spots in this business

The first is the hardware cycle. iPhone accounts for nearly half of revenue, and the replacement cycle has stretched from 24 months in 2019 to around 34 months in 2026 (rough estimate, back-solved from shipments and installed base). The longer the cycle, the greater the single-year volume volatility — much of the +21.7% in 2026 is the release of pent-up demand from 2024–2025, not new demand.

The second is the legitimacy of the "rent." The App Store's 30% cut was forced open in the EU under the Digital Markets Act and in the US by courts requiring it to allow external payment links. Apple's response is "lower the rate + add services" — cutting the take rate from 30% to 27%, then making it back through advertising and subscriptions. This is a rent-collection model being repriced, not a stable annuity.

A Duan Yongping-style summary: a good business is not the same as a good stock. This is a business I would be willing to hold for ten years, but the entry price must make "me ten years from now" thank "me today." USD 303 is not that price.

One more valuation observation: the 34x the market gives Apple is really a weighted blend of the multiples on two separate businesses. Split by gross profit: products contribute RMB 31.55 billion (57.6%), services RMB 23.24 billion (42.4%); if products get 26x and services get 44x, the weighted multiple is 33.6x, exactly the current price (rough calculation: 0.576×26 + 0.424×44 = 33.6). The implication of this split is that multiple expansion can only come from the services side — every 5x cut to services knocks 1.1x off the overall valuation, equivalent to USD 10 on the share price.

Conclusion

Business conclusion: 9/10. The experience-rent model is irreplaceable, and switching costs constitute genuine pricing power; the two weak points (hardware cycle, legality of commissions) do not change the nature of the business, only its growth ceiling. The business is worth buying; wait for the price.
