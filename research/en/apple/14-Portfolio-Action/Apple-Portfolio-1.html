Core view

Current price USD 303.42; recommended portfolio weight 0%–2%. Not a bearish call — the risk/reward simply doesn't compensate: 23% upside against 29% downside, a neutral −4% skew and negative expected return.

Scale in at three levels: 2% below USD 280, 5% below USD 260 and 8% below USD 240. Trim at USD 373, the bull-case target. Stop-loss on logic, not price — a genuine break is gross margin below 46% for two consecutive quarters, or services growth below 10%.

On correlation and portfolio structure, Apple is a "consumer electronics + services" mix, with 0.8 correlation to the Nasdaq and −0.2 to memory stocks. If the portfolio already holds memory or semis, Apple is a natural cost-side hedge.

Why not buy now: the odds in three numbers

Weight the three scenarios at 1/3 each: bear case USD 215 (−29%), base case USD 290 (−4%), bull case USD 373 (+23%), giving an expected price of USD 292.7, 3.5% below the current price. The expected return is negative; this is not "a bit expensive," it is "inverted odds."

Why not wait for it to fall? Because waiting has a cost. If Apple really does drive EPS to USD 11.3 by 2027 through AI monetization, USD 373 is only the starting point, and the opportunity cost of waiting is 23%. So the answer is not "stay fully in cash and wait," it is "hold a 2% tracking position"—small enough not to affect the portfolio, but enough to make you track it seriously.

How does the math work on a 2% position? Worst case loses 2% × 29% = 0.6% of portfolio net value; best case gains 2% × 23% = 0.46%. The odds are still asymmetric, but the cost is small enough to bear, and it buys you the incentive to read the earnings report every quarter.

There is also a portfolio-level reason: Apple is negatively correlated with memory/semiconductors (about −0.2), because the cost side moves in the opposite direction. If the portfolio already holds Micron, Samsung and TSMC, adding a little Apple offsets half the risk of memory price increases—not to make money on Apple, but to reduce the volatility of the whole portfolio.

How are the three add-on price levels calculated?

Everything anchors to FY2027 EPS of USD 10.0: USD 280 = 28x, USD 260 = 26x, USD 240 = 24x. The three tiers map to three levels of conviction: 28x is "fair but not cheap," 26x is "margin of safety," 24x is "the market is pricing a pessimistic scenario."

Why not add another tier below? Because below USD 240 sits the 52-week low of 246.63, a level that usually signals systemic risk (a 20% market drawdown), not a company problem. In that situation the right move is to add by portfolio weight, not by single-stock judgment.

The trim line of USD 373 comes from the optimistic scenario: EPS of USD 11.3 × 33x. At that level, even the bull case is fully realized; holding on means betting on a fourth scenario—and that fourth scenario (full AI monetization + foldable success + services back to 15%) has less than a 15% probability, not worth reserving position for.

Stop-loss: use logic, not price

Apple is not a name suited to price-based stop-losses. In June 2026 the stock fell from 307 to 275 (−10.5%), then rallied back to 340 in July—a price stop would have had you selling the low and watching it climb back.

Use a logic-based stop instead, with three triggers: ① gross margin below 46% for two consecutive quarters—signalling that memory costs are eating not just the buffer but pricing power itself; ② services growth below 10%—signalling the "software company" story is over and the stock should carry a hardware multiple; ③ Greater China turning negative YoY—signalling that Huawei's pressure has shifted from share diversion to displacement.

If any two of the three occur simultaneously, cut to 0 regardless of price. That means the assumptions you bought on were wrong—not the market.

Portfolio level: a natural hedge for semiconductor holdings

Where the portfolio already holds memory or semiconductor names (Samsung, Micron, SK hynix, TSMC), Apple's correlation is negative (about −0.2). The reason is that the cost side runs in reverse: when memory prices rise, memory stocks benefit while Apple's gross margin comes under pressure. That hedge is particularly useful in 2026, with the memory super-cycle still running.

Mind the hedge ratio, however. Apple's β is only about 1.0 and its volatility is lower than that of pure semiconductor stocks, so it can hedge only part of the exposure. A rough allocation: for every 1 part of memory stock position, hold 1.5 parts of Apple, to offset half the portfolio impact of a cost shock (rough calculation, based on the difference in the two's sensitivity to memory prices).

Where the portfolio holds no semiconductors, Apple is a "quality + low volatility" core holding, suited to a 5%–8% long-term position and not to trading. The right way to use it is "buy on the way down, hold for three years", not "chase rallies and sell dips".

Conclusion

Conclusion: at the current price the position is 0%–2%; wait for three price levels. Build to 3% at USD 280, 5% at USD 260 and 8% at USD 240; halve the position above USD 373. Stop-loss should be thesis-driven (gross margin, services growth, Greater China), not price-driven.

Portfolio level: if you already hold memory/semiconductor names, Apple is a hedge; if not, it is a quality core holding.Either way, the current price is not a "must-buy" price.
