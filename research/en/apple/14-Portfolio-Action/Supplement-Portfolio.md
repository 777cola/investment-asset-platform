## Why not buy now: the odds in three numbers

Assign each of the three scenarios a 1/3 weight: bear case USD 215 (−29%), base case USD 290 (−4%), bull case USD 373 (+23%), for an expected price of USD 292.7, 3.5% below the current price. The expected return is negative; this is not "a bit expensive," it is "inverted odds."

Why not wait for it to fall? Because waiting has a cost. If Apple really does drive EPS to USD 11.3 by 2027 through AI monetization, USD 373 is only the starting point, and the opportunity cost of waiting is 23%. So the answer is not "stay fully in cash and wait," it is "hold a 2% tracking position"—small enough not to affect the portfolio, but enough to make you track it seriously.

How does the math work on a 2% position? Worst case loses 2% × 29% = 0.6% of portfolio net value; best case gains 2% × 23% = 0.46%. The odds are still asymmetric, but the cost is small enough to bear, and it buys you the incentive to read the earnings report every quarter.

There is also a portfolio-level reason: Apple is negatively correlated with memory/semiconductors (about −0.2), because the cost side moves in the opposite direction. If the portfolio already holds Micron, Samsung and TSMC, adding a little Apple offsets half the risk of memory price increases—not to make money on Apple, but to reduce the volatility of the whole portfolio.

## How are the three add-on price levels calculated?

Everything anchors to FY2027 EPS of USD 10.0: USD 280 = 28x, USD 260 = 26x, USD 240 = 24x. The three tiers map to three levels of conviction—28x is "fair but not cheap," 26x is "margin of safety," 24x is "the market is pricing a pessimistic scenario."

Why not add another tier below? Because below USD 240 sits the 52-week low of 246.63, a level that usually signals systemic risk (a 20% market drawdown), not a company problem. In that situation the right move is to add by portfolio weight, not by single-stock judgment.

The trim line of USD 373 comes from the optimistic scenario: EPS of USD 11.3 × 33x. At that level, even the bull case is fully realized; holding on means betting on a fourth scenario—and that fourth scenario (full AI monetization + foldable success + services back to 15%) has less than a 15% probability, not worth reserving position for.

## Stop-loss: use logic, not price

A company like Apple is not suited to price-based stop-losses. In June 2026 it fell from 307 to 275 (−10.5%), then rallied back to 340 in July—a price stop-loss would have you selling at the low and then watching it climb back.

What you should use is a logic-based stop-loss, with three triggers: ① gross margin below 46% for two consecutive quarters—signaling that memory costs are eating not just the buffer but pricing power itself; ② services growth below 10%—signaling the "software company" story is over and the valuation should be that of a hardware company; ③ Greater China turning negative year over year—signaling that Huawei's pressure has shifted from "share diversion" to "displacement."

If any two of the three occur simultaneously, cut to 0 regardless of price. Because that means the assumptions you bought on were wrong, not the market.

## Portfolio level: a natural hedge for semiconductor holdings

If the portfolio already holds memory or semiconductor names (Samsung, Micron, SK hynix, TSMC), Apple's correlation is negative (about −0.2). The reason is that the cost side runs in reverse: when memory prices rise, memory stocks benefit and Apple's gross margin comes under pressure. This hedge is particularly useful in 2026, because the memory super-cycle is still running.

But mind the hedge ratio. Apple's β is only about 1.0 and its volatility is lower than pure semiconductor stocks, so it can only hedge part of the exposure. A rough allocation is: for every 1 part of memory stock position, hold 1.5 parts of Apple, to offset half the portfolio impact of a cost shock (rough calculation, based on the difference in the two's sensitivity to memory prices).

If the portfolio holds no semiconductors, Apple is a "quality + low volatility" core holding, suited to a 5%–8% long-term position and not suited to trading. The right way to use it is "buy on the way down, hold for three years", not "chase rallies and sell dips".
