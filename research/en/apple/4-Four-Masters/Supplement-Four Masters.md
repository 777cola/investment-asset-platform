## Duan Yongping: This is the only tech stock he has held as a long-term heavy position, but he bought it at 13x

Duan Yongping has said many times what Apple's business model is: charge the highest premium for the best product; users stay for the experience, not the low price. The second quarter of 2026 put that claim to the test — the global handset market grew marginally, Apple's share rose to 20%, iPhone revenue reached USD 54.252 billion, up 21.7% year on year, and the average selling price was pushed higher again by Pro models. Raising prices and still growing volume in a shrinking industry — that is the business model speaking.

But his purchase of Apple has always had one non-negotiable precondition, and it was never a secret: price. When he first bought in size in 2011, Apple traded at about 13x earnings; when he added in 2016, just over 10x. His logic is "good company + cheap price = an investment you don't have to babysit" — miss either condition and you wait. At 34x TTM and 30x FY2027, this is the price of a "good company", not the price of a "good investment".

He would probably add something uncomfortable: I don't understand AI, but I understand people. People pay for "not having to think", and that is what Apple sells. So AI is not a necessity for Apple, it is a bonus — and a bonus is not worth 5 turns of P/E. His conclusion would be: I would happily own this company for ten years, but I cannot bring myself to buy at this price.

There is another layer he often stresses: buying a stock is buying a company, and price determines return. Run it in reverse: buy at 34x and demand an 8% annualized return, and net profit must compound at more than 9% a year for the next decade (rough math: 34x implies a 2.9% initial yield; add 9% growth = 11.9%, minus the drag from multiple compression). A 9% ten-year net profit CAGR, for a company whose revenue compounded at 4.2% over three years, is a pace that requires AI monetization to actually materialize. That is why he will not buy at 34x — not because he doubts Apple, but because he refuses to build returns on "it has to happen".

## Buffett: the moat is intact, but 30x is not a price he would pay

Buffett's framework for buying Apple was never technology; it was consumer staples. Three things he said repeatedly: user loyalty close to See's Candies, stable cash flow, large-scale buybacks by management. None of these three has changed today — buybacks continue each quarter, shares outstanding are down 1.9% year over year, and per-share value compounds automatically; services revenue runs at an annualized USD 123 billion at a 75.6% gross margin, steadier than any consumer staples company.

He would also spell out what he does not understand: I don't understand AI, but Apple doesn't need to win AI to make money. That sentence is the foundation of Apple's valuation — it does not bet on model capability, only on "people continuing to use phones." The flip side is equally clear: if AI makes the phone form factor disappear, the moat must be re-rated. He would put this under "things to watch within ten years," not "next year's issue."

On price he has a hard discipline: no matter how good the company, never pay for anything above 25x (Coca-Cola was 15x in 1988, Apple 10x in 2016). At 25x FY2027 EPS of USD 10.0, his buy price is USD 250, 18% below the current price. What he would do now: nothing, wait.

He would also note a detail: Apple now buys back USD 100 billion a year, equivalent to repurchasing USD 400 million of its own stock every day. Buying back at 34x versus at 10x in 2016 are two completely different kinds of capital allocation — the former shrinks the share count while destroying cash, the latter buys a dollar for fifty cents. Buffett praised Apple's buybacks as "very smart" in 2018–2020, when the P/E was 13–20x. The same sentence does not hold now.

## Munger: three things that could sink it 30%, none of which need the broader market's help

Munger's method is to list every "bad thing" in full, then see whether the price compensates. He lists three for Apple. First: memory price increases persist for two years, gross margin falls from 50.1% to 44%–45%, EPS cut 15%. This is not hypothetical—gross margin guidance for this quarter has already dropped to 47%–48%, DRAM contract prices rose 60%–70% in early 2026, NAND doubled, and HBM is still crowding out capacity.

Second: Greater China share keeps slipping. This quarter USD 18.816 billion, +22.4% year on year, looks decent, but that is below the consensus of 3.9%, and it was achieved under pressure from Huawei and Xiaomi. If this line falls from USD 18.8 billion back to USD 15 billion, global revenue growth immediately drops from double digits to single digits.

The third is the most lethal: the services business gets broken up by regulators. App Store commissions are being challenged in both the EU and the US—this hits the valuation directly, not the profit: of Apple's 34x, 10x comes from services. He said something in Poor Charlie's Almanack that fits perfectly here: if a business needs a legal loophole to sustain high gross margins, that loophole will eventually be fixed. If any one of the three plays out, a 30% share-price drop needs no help from the broader market. The odds are wrong—wait.

He would also run a reverse thought experiment: how does Apple become Nokia in 2013? The answer is not "products get worse" but "the ecosystem becomes a burden"—when the operating system dares not rewrite itself for compatibility with old devices, when the app store rejects new form factors to protect commissions, when management dares not cut prices to trade for volume for fear of losing gross margin. Nokia also held 35% share in 2010 and also had a first-class supply chain. The way a moat is breached is usually not that a rival climbs over it, but that the owner refuses to come down. Apple shows no such symptoms now, but the first decision Ternus must make after taking over is whether he dares rewrite the interaction foundation of iOS for AI.

## Li Lu: what the AI-era gateway is worth depends on how it charges

Li Lu would ask a longer question: if AI assistants become the main entry point for human-computer interaction, who controls that entry point? Apple has 2.3 billion active devices and 1.5 billion paid subscriptions — one of the most valuable distribution networks in the world. Siri AI launched at the June WWDC, public beta feedback is positive, and the direction is right — turning the entry point from "open an app" into "say one sentence."

But he would immediately add a distinction: entry-point value does not equal entry-point revenue. The Android camp is using free AI to grab users, with greater intensity and lower cost; if Apple insists that "AI is a reason to sell hardware" rather than "AI is a separately charged product," this moat can only defend the hardware premium — it cannot support valuation expansion. The company's own words on the earnings call were "paid value-added services have not yet formed revenue at scale" — that is the reading Li Lu cares about most.

He would also look at an angle others ignore: whether AI weakens the "need to upgrade." If model capabilities mainly run in the cloud and old devices can use them too, upgrade cycles lengthen, and Apple's hardware premium becomes one-off revenue. Conversely, if on-device models require new chips (A20 and later), AI becomes a new reason to upgrade. The difference between these two paths determines the 2028 iPhone average selling price. He gives a price score of 4 — slightly higher than the other three, because he looks at ten years, not twelve months.

There is one more point he would certainly raise: demographics. Apple's core users are in Europe, the US and Japan, three markets with aging populations where upgrade frequency declines with age; growth is in India, Southeast Asia and Africa, where Apple's share is below 5%. This means Apple's ten-year growth must come from "monetizing services" rather than "selling more phones." And this judgment collides precisely with this quarter's services slowdown — which is why Li Lu, despite taking a ten-year view, still gives only a price score of 4.

## All four share one premise: the phone form factor will still exist in ten years

Across the four analyses there is one unspoken shared assumption: a decade from now, people will still use the form factor of "one screen + one system + one app store." In 2015 this assumption needed almost no discussion; in 2026 it does.

If AI turns interaction into "voice + glasses + ambient sensing," Apple's moat degrades from "ecosystem lock-in" to "brand preference" — and the latter is worth only 15x P/E, not 34x. Vision Pro is the company's answer to this direction, but 2026 sales remain in the million-unit range at a starting price of USD 3,500, far from the mass market.

None of the four treats this as a base case, because the probability is low (20% within ten years). But its existence shows one thing: Apple's current valuation embeds an implicit assumption of "unchanged form factor," and the market never prices that assumption separately. This is exactly the problem margin of safety is meant to solve — not to predict whether the form factor changes, but to avoid buying when the assumption is most expensive.

## Odds assessment: 23% upside, 29% downside, −4% neutral

The expected value across the three scenarios works out negative: bear case USD 215 (−29%), base case USD 290 (−4%), bull case USD 373 (+23%). Weighting each scenario at 1/3 gives an expected price of USD 292.7, 3.5% below the current price. This is not "a bit expensive" — it is inverted risk-reward: you need to get two things right (services acceleration + AI monetization) to make 23%, and getting one wrong costs you 29%.

To be fair, the bull case is not fantasy: if Siri AI charges USD 5 per user per month and penetrates 20% of 1.5 billion subscribers, that is USD 18 billion of revenue a year, which at 70% gross margin adds about USD 0.85 to EPS. The problem is that this number most likely will not show a full year in the FY2027 statements.

So the position sizing advice from all four is consistent: a 0 to 2% watching position, no adds. The level genuinely worth acting on is below USD 260 (26x FY2027), or wait for the evidence of "AI revenue disclosed separately" — at that point, even at USD 320, the risk-reward is better than it is now.
