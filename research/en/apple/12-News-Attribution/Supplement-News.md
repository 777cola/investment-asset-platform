## Two real signals: both point to costs, not demand

The −6.12% move on June 25 was one of the largest single-day declines of the quarter. The cause was not trouble at Apple, but Samsung and SK hynix notifying a further 15%–20% rise in third-quarter DRAM contract prices. The market did the math at the time: memory accounts for about 12% of the iPhone BOM, and another 20% increase means gross margin drops another 0.8–1 point. The math was right, and the guidance a month later was 47%–48%.

The −7.35% on July 31 confirmed the same thing. The results themselves were not bad: revenue of RMB 109.42 billion, above the consensus of 0.5%, iPhone +21.7%. But gross margin guidance went straight from 50.1% to 47%–48%, effectively telling the market the cost shock will persist into the next quarter, while services growth of 12.1% and Greater China below expectations appeared at the same time. Three marginal changes together, and the valuation premium could not hold.

What these two have in common: they change cash flow assumptions, not sentiment. The test is simple — if a piece of news makes you rewrite next year's EPS, it is a signal; if it does not, it is noise.

A quick calculation of how the −7.35% came about. The pre-earnings price of USD 333.43 corresponded to FY2027 consensus EPS of USD 10.39 and 32.1x; after the earnings, cutting EPS to USD 10.00 and compressing the multiple to 30.9x gives USD 309 — EPS −3.8%, multiple −3.7%, and the two small adjustments multiply to −7.4%. This was not a sentiment collapse but a re-rating of the valuation model, not a step more.

## Three noise points: a lively rally, but nothing has changed

The +4.84% tariff rebate on July 2 is the most easily misread positive in this earnings report. The USD 1.6 billion one-off gain flowed into Q3 EPS (USD 0.11) and will not repeat. The market treated it as "bad news fully priced in" at the time, and only a month later realized it was one-off — which is why the stock fell so hard after earnings.

The +4.01% on July 15 came from TSMC's earnings call. TSMC raised full-year guidance, and Apple benefited as its largest customer via spillover. But TSMC's guidance raise came mainly from AI chips (NVIDIA, AMD), not Apple orders. This was pure sentiment transmission.

The July 24–28 rally (+3.53%, hitting a 52-week high of 340.08) came from supply-chain restocking data and foldable-phone rumors. This is a classic case of expectations running ahead: restocking data is a Q4 matter and foldables are a 2027 matter, neither of which affects current-period profit. In hindsight, this rally was exactly the pre-earnings peak — buy on expectations, sell on facts.

The three noise events share one structure: all of them made the stock price "reflect in advance" something that had not yet happened. The tax rebate was priced in early as a positive, TSMC's good news spilled over into good news for Apple, and restocking data was priced in early as Q4 results. The hallmark of such rallies is that when the event actually happens, the stock does not react — or even moves the other way.

## Why the WWDC item counts as signal, not noise

Apple fell 3.64% after WWDC on June 9, which many attributed to "the product falling short of expectations." My read differs: Siri AI's product capability is adequate (on-device model, cross-app actions, positive public beta feedback); what the market sold off was the "absence of a paid model."

This pinpoints Apple's core contradiction right now: AI is a cost, not revenue. R&D spend of USD 40 billion a year and larger memory requirements for on-device models are all pushing costs higher; on the revenue side, "paid value-added services have yet to generate revenue at scale" is the company's own wording. The market recognized this on WWDC day, a month and a half ahead of the earnings report.

So this counts as a signal, but it belongs under "valuation," not "results." What it changes is the multiple the market is willing to pay, not the company's profit. That is also why its decline (3.64%) was far smaller than the earnings-report event (7.35%).

One more thing: the −6.12% move on June 25 came just two weeks after WWDC. Together these two declines exceed 9%, as the market spent two weeks shaving a slice off both the "AI story" premium and the "gross margin" premium. From 307.34 on June 5 to 275.15 on June 25, a 10.5% drop in a month, with the earnings report not yet out—that is what "expectations falling first" looks like. The July rebound (275→340) essentially retraced that decline, only to be knocked back down by the earnings report.

This stretch of trading offers a practical observation: the only days in the year truly worth watching for Apple are four—its earnings dates in February, May, August and November. Moves the rest of the time are 80% sentiment and flows. Of the seven events in three months, only two changed the valuation model—already a high proportion.
