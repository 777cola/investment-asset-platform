## Initial screen: all four eliminations rest on hard reasons

Samsung: The biggest beneficiary of this memory cycle, but not a "consumer electronics brand" — handsets account for only 30% of operating profit, with the rest from memory and displays. Buying Samsung means buying memory prices, not brand premium. 7.5x P/E is cheap, but that is how cyclical stocks are valued. Reason for exclusion: wrong business type.

Xiaomi: Gross margin 19.8%; handset gross profit is directly crushed by memory price increases (memory accounts for a higher share of handset BOM), and the auto business is still burning cash. Its ecosystem story (human-car-home) is real, but an 8.5% net margin leaves no room to maneuver. Reason for exclusion: moat too shallow; cost shock hits the bottom line directly.

Sony: Global No. 1 in CMOS sensors; gaming and music businesses are stable, but growth is only 5%, and the sensor business is dragged by handset shipments. Reason for exclusion: good company, but no growth engine.

Hon Hai: The AI server transition has been very successful, but gross margin is 6.1% and net margin 2.4% — it earns processing fees. Reason for exclusion: it is a supplier to Apple, not a substitute for Apple.

One more note on why market cap is not used as a screen. Samsung's market cap is USD 1.1 trillion, Google's USD 3.4 trillion, Apple's USD 4.41 trillion — all in the same order of magnitude; but scale does not explain business quality — Hon Hai's market cap is one-tenth of Apple's and its margin is one-twentieth, and these two facts are really the same fact.

## Final pick: Apple vs Google, two different bets

Both remaining names have a case to buy, but what you are buying is entirely different. Google trades at 21x P/E, has the strongest AI models and an accelerating cloud business, but it is not a hardware company—buying it means buying advertising and compute, with little link to the "consumer electronics" theme.

Apple trades at 34x P/E, grows 10% and runs a 50.1% gross margin. It is the only asset that combines brand, system and services in one, and the only company that can raise prices in a non-growing industry. 1.5 billion paid subscriptions and 2.3 billion devices: the world's most expensive distribution network.

Why Apple over Google? Because in this theme I want "the right to collect rent on the terminal gateway," not "model capability." Models will commoditise; gateways will not—Apple's deepest moat and its most valuable asset in the AI era. Google's gateway is the browser and search, which is another war entirely.

Google cannot be ignored entirely, though: buying an asset with 13% growth, 58% gross margin and 30% ROE at 21x P/E offers better odds than Apple. My approach is to put them in two different themes—Google for "AI infrastructure," Apple for "consumer electronics terminals." Do not mix two different businesses within the same theme for comparison; that is the most common valuation error.

Put more plainly: if you could only buy one "consumer electronics" stock, the answer is unambiguous. But if the goal is "15% annualised over the next three years," Apple at its current price cannot deliver that—it would need EPS to rise from USD 10 to USD 13 while the multiple holds, and the probability of both happening at once is not high.

## Why it: three conditions that cannot be replicated

First, vertical integration of system and hardware. Apple is the only company globally doing chips, system and hardware at once, which is what makes the experience "effortless." Samsung has hardware but no system, Google has a system but no hardware, and Huawei has a system but is constrained by process node.

Second, services cash flow. USD 123 billion annualized at a 75.6% gross margin, with almost zero capex. That funds USD 100 billion of buybacks a year and, in turn, continued accretion of per-share value. No second hardware company has this structure.

Third, brand pricing power. In an industry where everyone is cutting prices, only Apple can raise prices and still grow volume. This quarter iPhone revenue rose 21.7% while unit shipments grew only in the single digits — direct evidence of pricing power.

Stack the three conditions and the answer is unique. But one point needs stating: "the best company in the industry" and "the company worth buying now" are two different conclusions. The former holds, the latter does not — paying 34x for 10% growth is not enough odds.

One counter-check: if Apple fell to USD 220 tomorrow (25x TTM), none of the three conditions would change, but twice as many people would buy. That shows what decides a purchase is never "whether the company is good" but "whether the price fits" — an industry funnel can screen out the target, but not the entry point.
