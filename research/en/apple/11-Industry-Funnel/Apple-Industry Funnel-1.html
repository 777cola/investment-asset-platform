Core thesis

Put six consumer-electronics leaders into one table: Apple, Samsung, Xiaomi, Sony, Google, Hon Hai. The first screen eliminates four — Samsung is a beta play on the memory cycle, Xiaomi's gross margin is too thin, Sony's growth has stalled, and Hon Hai is a contract manufacturer, not a brand.

That leaves Apple and Google. Google has stronger AI capabilities and a cheaper valuation (21x), but it is not a hardware company — buying it means buying advertising and cloud. Final pick: Apple. It is the only name that combines brand, system and services in one, and the only company in the hardware industry able to reach a 50% gross margin.

But a final pick does not mean buying now. Apple trades at 34x on 10% growth — not enough upside relative to downside; that is why this report's rating is "Watch."

Initial screen: all four eliminations rest on hard reasons

Samsung: The biggest beneficiary of this memory cycle, but it is not a "consumer electronics brand" — handsets account for only 30% of operating profit; the rest is memory and displays. Buying Samsung means buying memory prices, not brand premium. 7.5x PE is cheap, but that is how cyclical stocks are valued. Reason for exclusion: wrong business type.

Xiaomi: Gross margin 19.8%; handset gross profit is directly crushed by memory price increases (memory accounts for a higher share of handset BOM), and the auto business is still burning cash. Its ecosystem story (human-car-home) is real, but a 8.5% net margin leaves no room to maneuver. Reason for exclusion: moat too shallow; cost shock hits the bottom line directly.

Sony: Global No. 1 in CMOS sensors; gaming and music businesses are stable, but growth is only 5%, and the sensor business is dragged by handset shipments. Reason for exclusion: good company, but no growth engine.

Hon Hai: The AI server transition has been very successful, but gross margin is 6.1% and net margin 2.4% — it earns processing fees. Reason for exclusion: it is a supplier to Apple, not a substitute for Apple.

One more note on why market cap is not used as a screen. Samsung's market cap is USD 1.1 trillion, Google's USD 3.4 trillion, Apple's USD 4.41 trillion — all in the same order of magnitude; but scale does not explain business quality — Hon Hai's market cap is one-tenth of Apple's and its margin is one-twentieth, and these two facts are really the same fact.

Final pick: Apple vs Google, two different bets

The remaining two both have reasons to buy, but what you are buying is completely different. Google trades at 21x PE, has the strongest AI models, and its cloud business is accelerating, but it is not a hardware company—buying it means buying advertising and compute, with little connection to the "consumer electronics" theme.

Apple trades at 34x PE, grows 10%, and has a 50.1% gross margin. It is the only asset that combines brand, system, and services in one, and the only company that can raise prices in a non-growing industry. 1.5 billion paid subscriptions and 2.3 billion devices: this is the world's most expensive distribution network.

Why choose Apple over Google? Because in this theme, what I want is "the right to collect rent on the terminal gateway," not "model capability." Models will commoditize; gateways will not—this is Apple's deepest moat and its most valuable asset in the AI era. Google's gateway is the browser and search, which is another war entirely.

But Google cannot be entirely ignored either: buying an asset with 13% growth, 58% gross margin, and 30% ROE at 21x PE offers better odds than Apple. My approach is to put them in two different themes—Google for "AI infrastructure," Apple for "consumer electronics terminals." Do not mix two different businesses within the same theme for comparison; that is the most common valuation error.

To put it more plainly: if you could only buy one "consumer electronics" stock, the answer is unambiguous. But if the goal is "15% annualized over the next three years," Apple at its current price cannot deliver that—it would need EPS to rise from USD 10 to USD 13 while the multiple holds, and the probability of both happening at once is not high.

Why it: three conditions that cannot be replicated

First, vertical integration of system and hardware. Globally, only Apple does chips, system and hardware at once, which lets it make the experience "effortless." Samsung has hardware but no system, Google has a system but no hardware, and Huawei has a system but is constrained by process node.

Second, services cash flow. USD 123 billion annualized, 75.6% gross margin, almost zero capex. That money funds USD 100 billion of buybacks a year, and thus the continued accretion of per-share value. No second hardware company has this structure.

Third, brand pricing power. In an industry where everyone is cutting prices, only it can raise prices and still grow volume. This quarter iPhone revenue rose 21.7% while unit shipments grew only in the single digits — direct evidence of pricing power.

Stack the three conditions and the answer is unique. But one thing needs stating: "the best company in the industry" and "the company worth buying now" are two different conclusions. The former holds, the latter does not — paying 34x for 10% growth is not enough odds.

One counter-check: if Apple fell to USD 220 tomorrow (25x TTM), none of the three conditions would change, but twice as many people would buy. That shows what decides a purchase is never "whether the company is good" but "whether the price fits" — an industry funnel can screen out the target, but not the entry point.

Conclusion

Conclusion: Apple is the final pick, but only for the watchlist. Of the six, it is the least replaceable, with three conditions (vertical integration, services cash flow, brand pricing power) that cannot be replicated.

Entry point below USD 260 (26x FY2027 EPS), or wait for AI revenue to be broken out separately. The industry funnel answers "what to buy"; the margin of safety answers "when to buy" — the two must not be conflated.
