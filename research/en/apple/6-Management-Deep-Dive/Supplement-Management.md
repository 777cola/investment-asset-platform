## Cook: supply-chain master + buyback machine, right on both bets

When he took over in August 2011, Apple had annual revenue of USD 108 billion and a market cap of USD 350 billion. By FY2025, revenue reached USD 416.2 billion and the market cap at one point exceeded USD 4 trillion. Fourteen years: revenue nearly quadrupled, market cap up tenfold. That track record is a textbook case in any industry.

The first thing he got right was turning the supply chain into a weapon. Apple's inventory turnover days fell from 10 in 2011 to under 6 today, and he has managed 200 core global suppliers into a network that can be swapped or expanded at any time. On this quarter's earnings call, Cook said the company is "struggling with the supply chain, with only three core memory suppliers" — coming from a supply-chain master, that statement signals the problem is genuinely serious.

The second thing he got right was capital allocation. Dividends resumed in 2012, large-scale buybacks began in 2013, and cumulative returns over fourteen years exceed USD 800 billion. He turned Apple from a company that piled up cash without spending it into one that buys back USD 400 million of its own stock every day. The cost is net assets compressed to USD 57 billion and ROE distorted to 200% — but from a shareholder-return perspective, it was the right choice.

## Ternus: Product judgment is sound, capital allocation is a blank slate

John Ternus joined Apple in 2001, starting in product design, took over hardware engineering in 2013, and has run the Mac, iPad and AirPods lines. His strongest achievement is the Mac's in-house silicon transition—moving the entire product line from Intel to the M series within three years, with gains in both performance and battery life, an extreme demonstration of hardware engineering capability.

His weaknesses are equally clear. First, no capital allocation track record: whether to continue the USD 100 billion annual buyback, at what price to buy back, and whether to raise the dividend—he has never made these decisions. And that is exactly the judgment Apple needs most right now—continuing large buybacks at a 34x P/E is effectively converting shareholder money into more expensive stock.

Second, no cycle experience. Cook lived through three downturns—2008, 2019 and 2022—and knows when to pull back. Ternus has stayed on the product lines and has never made the call to "cut capacity, cut projects, cut headcount." If memory costs keep rising and demand weakens in 2027, his first stress test will be a hard one.

Third, the product roadmap depends on whether he dares to bet. The foldable (market expects 2027), AI glasses and on-device model chips all require investment two years ahead. The Cook-era style was "wait for others to validate, then do it best"; if Ternus continues this style, Apple will fall behind in the AI hardware battle.

## Three historical accounts: the governance record of the Cook era

The first is the Irish back-tax bill. In 2016 the EU ruled that Apple's tax arrangement with Ireland constituted illegal state aid and ordered it to pay EUR 13 billion. Apple appealed for eight years and lost its final appeal at the European Court of Justice in 2024; the money has been paid in full. The nature of this is not tax evasion but exploitation of the rules—yet once the rules were rewritten, Apple became one of the biggest payers. It also explains why Apple's effective tax rate now sits at 15%–16%, higher than a decade ago.

The second is App Store regulation. In 2024 the EU fined Apple EUR 1.84 billion over its anti-steering clauses, and the Digital Markets Act subsequently forced it to open up external payments; in the US, the final ruling in the Epic case required allowing external payment links. Together, these two cut Apple's services gross margin from 78% to 75.6%, and it will keep falling. This is not a governance scandal but a business model being repriced by regulators.

The third is the January 2019 revenue guidance cut—the first in a decade. The cause was weak iPhone sales in China, which Cook acknowledged in an open letter. The value of this lies in "he told the truth": he did not blame FX or macro but said directly that "our product pricing was too high and upgrade subsidies fell." That is rare at a USD 4 trillion company.

There is one more ledger item that never shows up in the financials: supply-chain labor. After the 2022 Zhengzhou factory labor incident, Apple wrote "supplier responsibility" into its procurement contracts as a hard metric, publishes an annual supplier responsibility report, and reached roughly 98% compliance coverage in 2026. This generates no revenue, but it determines whether Apple can hold its political ground—for a company that puts 80% of assembly in one country, labor and geopolitics are the same thing.

## Three things to watch after the succession

First, buyback discipline. Look at the FY2027Q1 cash flow statement: if buybacks stay above USD 25 billion per quarter and the share price holds above USD 300, the new CEO is carrying on the old policy rather than rethinking it. The right approach is to buy more when the price is lower, not to commit a fixed amount each quarter.

Second, how the AI money is spent. Apple will spend about USD 40 billion on R&D in 2026, but AI-related capex (in-house compute, data centers) has stayed restrained, relying mainly on partnerships with Google and OpenAI. If Ternus starts building his own large-model training clusters, that is a strategic shift; if he continues with partnerships plus on-device, that is continuity.

Third, team retention. Whether core executives (Federighi, Parekh, Sabih Khan) stay within 12 months of the CEO handover is the most direct gauge of whether the transition is smooth. Historically at Apple, executive departures have tended to precede product missteps by a year.
