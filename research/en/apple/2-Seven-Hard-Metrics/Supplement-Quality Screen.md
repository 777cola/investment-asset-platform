## Six clean passes, none reliant on accounting tricks

A 50.1% gross margin is the highest on record, with services gross margin of 75.6% lifting the overall figure; net margin is 27.2%, a level only Apple can achieve among hardware companies. Neither was built in a single year—over the past decade Apple has turned "high price + high services mix" into a structural advantage, so even if iPhone sales are weak in a given quarter, the services line keeps collecting money.

The debt point needs to be explained clearly: Apple carries about USD 98 billion in long-term debt against about USD 130 billion in cash and securities, for net cash of about USD 30 billion (rough calculation, based on the latest balance sheet). It is not "debt-free," it is "borrowing less than it saves"—the cost of issuing debt is lower than the tax on repatriating overseas cash; this is financial engineering, not operating risk.

The cash flow point is also clean. Apple's operating cash flow runs consistently slightly above net profit because depreciation and amortization exceed capital expenditure (about USD 12 billion a year in capex, an extremely asset-light structure for a company with USD 460 billion in revenue). This is the single item most wanted in "negative screening": making money does not require constantly pouring money in.

One more word on the quality of the services business. USD 30.739 billion in services revenue at a 75.6% gross margin means this segment alone contributes USD 93 billion in gross profit a year, more than the entire gross profit of Samsung Electronics. And its capital expenditure is almost zero—server and bandwidth costs are booked as expenses, not on the capex table. This is the most easily underestimated of the seven points: companies where high margin and asset-light hold true at the same time are very rare.

## Article 4 passes, though for historical rather than present-day reasons

FY2023 revenue was RMB 383.29 billion, FY2024 RMB 391.04 billion and FY2025 RMB 416.16 billion, a two-year CAGR of 4.2%. Apple delivered no volume growth over these three years: iPhone shipments flatlined between 220 and 230 million units, with all growth coming from ASP (Pro mix) and services. That 4.2% shows buying Apple as a growth stock is wrong.

FY2026 changes the slope. Q3 revenue +16.4%, iPhone +21.7%, Mac +28.7%; extrapolating the first three quarters gives FY2026 revenue of about USD 469 billion, +12.7% YoY. This acceleration comes from the replacement cycle (delayed release of the iPhone 17 cycle) and the Mac M5 refresh, not from new categories.

So I score this criterion a fail, but not a veto. The hard metrics screen for whether a company is first-rate, and Apple's answer is unaffected by this item; what this item does affect is the multiple valuation should command — for a company compounding at 4% over three years and 12% in the current year, the fair multiple is 25–30x, not 40x.

## Will the one that falls short prove fatal? No, but it determines the purchase price

Put the seven tests in one table and the conclusion is that Apple's problem was never "will the business deteriorate" but "does the price fit." Not one of the seven points to "the company is in trouble": gross margin sits at a historical high, net margin is 27%, cash flow covers profit, and shareholder returns have run for thirteen consecutive years.

Fatality must be assessed separately. If the fourth test stays below 5% for three years, Apple gets repriced from "growth stock" to "cash cow" and its valuation centre falls from 30x to 22x, implying a share price of USD 220 — that is valuation risk, not operating risk. And if the 2026 acceleration is merely a one-off release of replacement demand, it will fall back below 5% in 2027.

Trading implication: passing six of seven means it can enter the watchlist, not the "buy now" list. The seven tests screen out bad companies; selecting a good price relies on something else entirely — the margin of safety in the fifth gate, which this report develops in m01 and m04.

## Three of the seven rank first in their industries; two look overvalued

Against consumer-electronics peers, Apple's gross margin of 50.1% compares with 35% at Samsung, 19.8% at Xiaomi and 6.1% at Hon Hai; net margin of 27.2% versus 12% at Samsung, 8.5% at Xiaomi and 2.4% at Hon Hai; and operating cash flow/net profit of 1.0x, which among peers only Samsung comes close to. On all three, Apple ranks first, and first by a wide margin.

What is overstated is ROE and revenue growth. ROE above 200% reflects equity compressed by buybacks to USD 57 billion — not efficiency but a leverage-driven accounting effect: for the same net profit, the thinner the equity, the higher the ROE. Scoring Apple on ROE is like grading someone who mortgaged their own house as "excellent asset turnover."

Revenue growth needs a caveat on the base. The +12.7% in 2026 rests on a 4% base across 2024–2025 — a rebound after a trough, not an accelerating trend. The test is simple: whether FY2027 can hold above 8%; if it falls back to 5%, the fourth criterion still fails.
