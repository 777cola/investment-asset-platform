## Upstream: memory is the only real winner in this cycle

Tracing the chain from upstream to downstream, it becomes clear that 2026 profits are concentrating at the very top. DRAM contract prices rose 60%–70% early in the year, NAND doubled, and both rose another 15%–20% in the third quarter; DRAM gross margins at Samsung, SK hynix and Micron climbed from just over 30% to above 50% — a price cycle seen once a decade.

Why can they raise prices? Because AI has crowded out supply. HBM (high-bandwidth memory) consumes three times as much wafer per unit as ordinary DRAM; once a line switches to HBM, consumer DRAM supply shrinks. Institutions expect the shortage to persist into 2027, with HBM prices potentially rising another 80%–100% in 2027. This is not speculation; it is a physical constraint on capacity.

Foundry is milder. TSMC will hold gross margin at 58% in 2026 on the exclusivity of 2nm and 3nm, and is raising advanced-node prices 5%–10%. Apple is its largest customer, but Apple has no second option — a relationship of mutual dependence, not one-sided pressure.

Lining up gross margins across the three upstream layers makes it clear: memory above 50%, foundry 58%, equipment and materials around 50% — all above 50%. Downstream assembly is just 6%, while at the brand end Apple is at 50% and Xiaomi at 20%. This is no accident; it is the result of "whoever is scarce captures the profit" — upstream scarcity is capacity and technology, downstream scarcity is brand and channels, and the non-scarce assembly middle earns only processing fees.

## Mid- and downstream: assembly earns only processing fees, while brands capture 70% of profit

Assembly is the hardest layer. Hon Hai's gross margin is 6.12% and net margin 2.4%; on a USD 1,000 iPhone, the assembly step captures USD 8–12 in processing fees (rough calculation). It bears all the risk of capacity, labor and yield, and earns an "efficiency residual."

The brand end is another world. Apple takes roughly 70% of global smartphone industry profit (rough calculation: summing operating profit across companies), Samsung about 20%, and the remaining Chinese vendors share less than 10%. This distribution structure has not changed in twenty years, because the brand end holds "pricing power," not "capacity."

The channel and services layer is the most easily overlooked. App Store's 15%–30% cut, the default search slot at about USD 20 billion a year, iCloud subscriptions—these revenues carry a 75.6% gross margin and require almost no capital expenditure. It is the only link in the chain that "takes a cut without participating in manufacturing," and the link regulators most want to break up.

One more detail: panels are the most typical "heavy-asset, low-return" link in this chain. BOE and LG Display each spend over USD 4 billion on an 8.6-generation OLED line, yet gross margin is only 10%–20%. This explains why Apple does not make panels, memory or assembly—it only holds "the power to define" and "the power to collect rent," leaving heavy assets to others. This is why Apple has the highest return on capital, and why its capital expenditure is only 2.6% of revenue.

## Apple's position: the only company that can collect fees from both sides

Draw the eight links of the chain as one diagram, and Apple stands in three positions at once: upstream chip design (in-house A/M series), midstream systems and services (iOS, App Store), and downstream brand and channels (retail stores, carriers). No other company globally occupies all three at once — Samsung has manufacturing but no system, Google has a system but no hardware, Huawei has a system but is constrained by chip process nodes.

The benefit of this position is "profit capture": in the cost of an iPhone, chip design fees, system licensing fees and channel markups all stay inside Apple. The downside is "concentrated liability": memory price increases, assembly relocation and regulatory fines all land on it.

Now a set of specific division-of-labor data: in the bill of materials for an iPhone, chips and memory account for more than 40%, screens and lenses each account for just over 10%, and assembly less than 5%. Yet the gross profit Apple takes equals half the handset's selling price. That means everyone else on the chain splits the remaining half — the more suppliers there are, the thinner each segment.

So its gross margin is the "residual item" of the chain — upstream price increases, weak downstream demand, and what is left over goes to it. 50.1% was when the residual was largest; 47.5% is what it looks like after upstream takes part of the residual. Understand this and it is clear that gross margin volatility is not a question of operating capability but is determined by position in the chain.

This position has another side effect: it must bear the cost of "definition errors" itself. If a directional judgment on the iPhone 18 proves wrong (for example, AI features fail to move users, or the foldable is a year late), no one on the chain shares the burden — suppliers still collect processing fees, memory still rises in price. That is the other side of "the best position": the greatest returns, and the most concentrated risk.

## Cycle position: moderate demand recovery, rising costs

Two cycles overlap: end demand is recovering modestly (global handset shipments edge up in 2026, Apple holds 20% share, replacement demand returns), while costs are rising fast (memory up 60%–100%, advanced nodes up 5%–10%, yield loss from ramping Indian capacity).

This combination is the worst case for device makers: raising prices suppresses demand that has only just recovered, while holding prices eats into gross margin. Apple opts for "a modest price increase plus absorbing the rest," at a cost of 2.6 points of gross margin. If memory rises another round in 2027, that buffer is gone and it will have to raise prices in earnest—whether demand then falls is the biggest uncertainty of this cycle.

Relief most likely arrives in the second half of 2027: new lines from Samsung, SK hynix and Micron come on stream in 2027, and HBM capacity expansion will free up some supply of conventional DRAM. But even if prices fall back, they will not return to 2024 levels—the memory capacity upgrade driven by AI is a permanent step up in demand.

For investing, the implication of this cycle position is direct: gross margin over the next four quarters will only move down, not up. Anyone waiting for "gross margin recovery" as a catalyst must wait until the second half of 2027; before then, any rebound is a valuation rebound, not an earnings rebound.
