③ Moat anatomy: three layers, only one is solid — Apple

Core view

The word moat is overused. This piece does one thing only: break Apple's moat into three layers and test each layer to see whether it is real or just talk. The test is simple: ask, "If a competitor spent USD 100 billion, could it replicate this?"

Layer one: ecosystem switching costs (the real moat, 9/10)

Test: give rivals USD 100 billion — can they get 100 million iPhone users to switch? The answer is no. The problem is not money; it is "migration" — photos, chat histories, subscriptions, accessories, family sharing. You cannot pay users to move these.

Evidence of this moat is not in the financials but in behavior: in the first half of 2026 the global handset market grew slightly, and Apple's share rose to 20%; the average iPhone price is USD 1,050 and still climbing. A brand with "insufficient product strength" cannot raise prices and gain share at the same time.

Its durability is the highest because it does not depend on any single technology. Even if Apple trails by a year in AI, even if its foldable launches late, users still will not switch just because switching is "a hassle." This is Apple's only true moat.

It has boundaries: it works only on people already inside the ecosystem. New users have no sunk cost, so Apple's share in India, Africa and Southeast Asia has consistently stayed below 5%. This moat defends the installed base but cannot open up incremental markets.

Second layer: brand premium (half true, 9/10 but erodes)

Test: give a competitor USD 100 billion—can you build a "high-end brand"? Theoretically yes, practically very hard, but not impossible—Huawei did it in China, in five years.

The evidence of brand premium is gross margin: 50.1%, 15 points above Samsung and 30 points above Xiaomi. But a brand erodes in two situations: first, a shift in form factor (foldables, AI hardware); second, a price war. China is seeing the first—Huawei used foldables to redefine "high-end" from "system experience" to "form-factor innovation."

So this layer scores 9, but durability is marked "medium-high." It is still here, but of the three layers it will be the first to fall.

The watch items are specific: quarterly Greater China revenue and share of the above-RMB 6,000 segment. This quarter USD 18.816 billion, +22.4% year on year, but below the consensus of 3.9%—some erosion is already visible in that number.

Layer 3: In-house chips and supply chain (not a moat, but a cost advantage, 7/10)

Test: give a rival USD 100 billion—can they build a chip on par with the M6? Yes, but it takes five years, and even then it may not be cheaper. So this is an advantage, not a moat.

The real value of the A series and M series is "a one-generation lead in performance per watt," which lets Apple build thinner chassis and stronger on-device AI on the same battery. But it is a cost and experience advantage, not pricing power—users will not pay USD 200 more because a chip is 20% faster; they pay for "no lag, no heat, no battery drain," and the Android camp can deliver 80% of that experience.

The key evidence is this quarter: Cook said the company is "struggling with the supply chain—memory has only three core suppliers." A company with a moat should not lack a second supplier for a critical component. Supply chain is Apple's strength, but it has no bargaining power on memory—that is the difference between a "cost advantage" and a "moat."

Combined score 34/50. That score is the best in consumer electronics, but it is not "invincible"—it is "very hard to catch." The difference between the two is the difference between a 30x and a 40x fair multiple.

The depreciation of a moat: how much will remain in ten years

Moats are not permanent; they depreciate. Assign each of the three layers a "residual value in ten years": ecosystem switching costs (9 points) depreciate slowly, still 7 points after ten years, because the hassle of migrating does not disappear when technology changes; brand (9 points) depreciates quickly, possibly 6 points after ten years, because form-factor shifts redefine "premium"; chips (7 points) depreciate fastest, 5 points after ten years, because the process-node gap is converging.

Summing up: today's 34/50 leaves roughly 26/50 in ten years. This depreciation rate means the justified multiple for "long-term holding" is 20–25x, not the current 34x.

A caveat: depreciation is not decline. 26/50 is still a good score — most consumer-electronics companies are down to 10 points after ten years. Apple's problem is going "from excellent to good", not "from excellent to mediocre".

Conclusion

Conclusion of Part Three: only one of the three layers is a true moat. Ecosystem switching costs 9 points (real), brand 9 points (erodes), chips and supply chain 7 points (an advantage, not a barrier). Total 34/50.

This structure dictates that Apple "can hold onto users, but not onto its multiple" — the moat protects the company, not the valuation.
