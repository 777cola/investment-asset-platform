⑥ Management: end of an era, a bet on the handover — Apple

Core view

On September 1, 2026, Cook hands the CEO role to Ternus. This is Apple's most important management change in fourteen years, and the market has barely priced it. This piece explains why it deserves separate attention.

What Cook leaves behind

Three numbers: revenue grew from USD 108 billion to USD 416.2 billion (FY2025); services went from zero to an annualized USD 123 billion; cumulative shareholder returns exceeded USD 800 billion. And one invisible metric: inventory turnover compressed from 10 days to under 6.

Three things he got right: turning the supply chain into a weapon, building services into a second curve, and making capital allocation a discipline (no major M&A in fourteen years, with all cash returned to shareholders). The common thread: "don't do anything superfluous"—under his tenure, Apple never chased the metaverse, never built a car, never bought a content company.

What he got wrong or left undone: a slow start in AI (Siri five years behind), a passive China strategy (no product response when Huawei returned), and conservative pricing (foldables three years late). All three point to the same weakness: he is an operator, not a product definer.

That is not a slight. An operator's value lies in "taking 1 to 10"—Apple's revenue nearly quadrupled in his hands, driven precisely by the fine-grained execution of supply chain, channels and services. But the "0 to 1" phase is over, and the next phase demands product judgment.

What is Ternus taking over?

He inherits a company with excellent cash flow, slowing growth and an awkward position in AI. Of these three, the first two need nothing from him; the third demands a big decision.

A hardware engineer by training, he ran Mac and iPad and led the M-series transition—his strength is making complex things simple, which is precisely Apple's product philosophy. But the CEO job is not only product; it is also capital allocation, investor communication and political maneuvering. He has no track record in those three.

The first decision to watch: buybacks. If he keeps repurchasing USD 100 billion a year above USD 300, he is continuing the old policy; if he slows buybacks and puts the money into AI compute, he is reassessing. That decision will say more about what kind of CEO he is than any product launch.

The second decision: foldables. The market expects a 2027 launch, the only path to lift the average price from USD 1,050 to USD 1,800. If he commits in his first year, he is willing to bet on form factor; if he keeps delaying, Apple has chosen to defend its existing price band.

Governance: clean, but not without issues

Apple's governance structure is standard for a large US company: a majority-independent board, separate CEO and chairman roles, and executive pay tied to shareholder returns. Eighty percent of Cook's USD 74.6 million compensation is stock, tied to the share price.

The historical ledger has three entries: the EUR 13 billion Irish back-tax ruling finalized in 2024, the EUR 1.84 billion EU antitrust fine in 2024, and the first revenue guidance cut in a decade in 2019. None is a scandal, but all three point to one thing: the company's business model is being repeatedly stress-tested by regulators and the cycle.

There is also an item that never shows up in the financials: supply-chain labor. After the 2022 Zhengzhou factory incident, Apple wrote "supplier responsibility" into its procurement contracts, with compliance coverage of about 98% in 2026. This determines whether it can hold its ground politically—for a company that puts 80% of assembly in one country, labor and geopolitics are the same issue.

Management score: 7. Cook is a full mark; Ternus is an unknown. The unknown warrants a discount—which is why the score is 7, not 9.

Compensation structure and incentive misalignment

Cook's compensation is USD 74.6 million, 80% in stock; Ternus's SVP package is about USD 27 million, likewise mostly stock. The implication of this structure: management's interests are tied to the share price, not to "long-term return on capital."

Where is the difference? A share price that doubles in three years can come from buybacks plus multiple expansion, while long-term return on capital depends on "how much each dollar invested earns back." Management can do the former (step up buybacks); the latter is slow, invisible, and drags on near-term EPS.

That is why Apple has spent fourteen years doing only small acquisitions and returning USD 800 billion to shareholders—not for lack of opportunities, but because the incentive structure makes it prefer "buying its own stock." That choice was right at a 10x P/E; it is wrong at 34x. If Ternus changes the incentive metrics after taking over (adding ROIC or AI revenue share, for example), that would be the real signal.

Conclusion

Conclusion of Part Six: Cook is a perfect operator; Ternus is an unproven product person. The handover itself was smooth (announced in advance, unanimous board support, Cook staying on as advisor), but the new CEO has no track record on capital allocation or AI judgment.

There is only one thing to watch: the price discipline in his first buyback decision after taking office. That matters more than any product launch.
