② Business essence: how a piece of equipment becomes an annual fee — Apple

Core view

This piece answers one question: what exactly makes Apple money, and why can't others replicate it. The answer lies not in the product specs but in the chain that turns a device into an annual fee.

Step one: lock in users with hardware

The iPhone's average selling price is roughly USD 1,050 (back-of-envelope: USD 54.252 billion revenue ÷ about 52 million units), USD 200–300 above comparable Android flagships. Why will users pay it? Not because the specs are better, but because avoiding the hassle of switching is worth that price.

Where the hassle lies: photos sit in iCloud, chats in iMessage, the watch and earbuds are already paired, subscriptions are tied to an Apple ID, Family Sharing links five people, and every banking app is logged in. Switching once means re-logging into twenty apps, migrating twenty thousand photos, reconfiguring three devices and retraining family members on all of it.

How high is that cost? A rough estimate: a full migration takes 3–5 hours, which at an opportunity cost of USD 40 per hour comes to USD 120–200. Exactly the price gap between Apple and comparable Android devices. That is no coincidence but the boundary of pricing—Apple sets its premium at the number that is "migration cost", not a cent more.

It also explains why "price-cut promotions" do not work for Apple: a USD 100 price cut will not make Android users switch, because the money saved does not compensate for the hassle of migrating. Apple's pricing power is, in essence, "user laziness".

Step two: turn hardware users into subscribers

Once users are inside the ecosystem, Apple monetises a second layer: iCloud storage (USD 0.99–9.99 per month), Apple Music (10.99), TV+ (9.99), Arcade, AppleCare and App Store commissions (15%–30%). 1.5 billion paid subscriptions, each contributing roughly USD 80 per year on average (123 billion ÷ 1.5 billion, back-of-envelope).

The economics of this layer are excellent: marginal cost near zero, very high renewal rates (iCloud and AppleCare renew at above 80%) and prices that can be nudged up every year. A 75.6% gross margin means every USD 100 collected leaves USD 75.6 behind.

Why will users pay? Because "storage is full" is a necessity and "losing my photos" is a fear. Apple turned both into automatic charges — users don't need to decide, only to not cancel. This is the highest form of the subscription business: no interruption, just collection.

A caveat: this layer's ceiling is approaching. 1.5 billion subscriptions against 2.3 billion devices puts penetration at 65%; the remaining 800 million devices are either low-end models or in emerging markets, with weak willingness to pay. So services growth must shift from "headcount" to "ARPU," and the key to ARPU is charging for AI.

Step three: collect rent from third parties

The third layer is the most profitable and the most fragile: App Store commissions of 15%–30%, the Google search default slot at roughly USD 20 billion a year, and App Store ad slots. This revenue carries near-zero cost, with gross margins above 85%.

Its fragility stems from "legitimacy": the EU Digital Markets Act requires opening up external payments, US courts require allowing external links, and developer class actions keep coming. In 2024 the EU issued a EUR 1.84 billion fine, and services gross margin fell from 78% to 75.6% — that is the price.

The whole chain can be summed up in one sentence: charge once for hardware (USD 1,050), charge for a lifetime via subscriptions (USD 80/year), and collect rent from others via the platform (15%–30% commission). The stacking of these three layers is the source of the 50.1% gross margin. Others cannot replicate it, not because of technology, but because they lack an entry point of 2.3 billion devices.

The fragile points of this chain are also clear: the first layer depends on product strength, the second on penetration, the third on law. The third layer is the most likely to collapse — it does not require users to change behavior, only regulators to change one rule.

A counterexample: why the iPad never became an annual-fee business

Within the same ecosystem, iPad services revenue falls far short of iPhone's. The driver is usage frequency, not device count — an iPhone is opened a hundred times a day, an iPad three times a week, and users will not pay for cloud storage and subscriptions for "a slab for weekend binge-watching."

This counterexample shows that ecosystem lock-in is not about owning many devices; it is about where the data lives. The phone carries identity and communication; the tablet carries content consumption — the former must migrate, the latter can be abandoned. Apple's moat is built on the phone, not the iPad.

To read Apple's future, then, watch not how many devices it sells but which device holds the user's data. If AI glasses are merely a phone accessory, they cannot replicate the phone's annual fees; only if they become a new data entry point does Apple's second curve hold.

Conclusion

Part Two conclusion: Apple's business is a three-tier structure of "one-time charge + lifetime annual fee + rent collected from third parties." The first tier is saturated, the second is maturing, and the third is being repriced by regulation—the key to understanding every one of its financial figures.

Follow this chain and it becomes clear why "iPhone sells well" explains only half the profit; the other half sits in users' pockets, deducted annually.
