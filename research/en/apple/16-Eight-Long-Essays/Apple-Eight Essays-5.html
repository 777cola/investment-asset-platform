⑤ Industry and competition: who is raising prices in a no-growth industry — Apple

Core view

The easiest thing to get wrong about this industry is the claim that "the phone market is saturated, so Apple is finished." Saturation is real, but "saturated" and "unprofitable" are two different things. This piece looks at how profit gets allocated in an industry that isn't growing.

Industry structure: five players split the market, each holding one price band

Global smartphone shipments in 2026 will reach about 1.2 billion units, up slightly year on year. Share: Samsung about 19%, Apple 20%, Xiaomi about 14%, Transsion about 9%, OPPO/vivo combined about 16%. No one exceeds 20%, but no one is losing their footing either.

This structure is characterized by "tiered competition" rather than "homogeneous competition." Apple defends the above-USD 800 segment, Samsung defends all price points, Xiaomi defends USD 200–600, Transsion defends emerging markets. Each tier has its own cost structure and channels; crossing tiers to attack is not easy.

So the price war mainly occurs in the mid-tier (Xiaomi, OPPO, vivo, Honor), while the high end is instead a "form-factor war" — foldables, AI features, imaging. Apple's position at the high end is solid, but it is no longer "the sole innovator" — this is the biggest change of the past five years.

The upstream supply chain follows an entirely different logic: memory (Samsung, SK hynix, Micron) and foundry (TSMC) post gross margins of 50%–58%, built on technological monopoly; assembly (Hon Hai) posts a 6% gross margin, built on efficiency. Apple sits at the brand end and takes 70% of profits, at the cost of having no pricing power over its upstream suppliers.

Profit distribution: Apple takes 70% because it sits at the rent-collection layer

Sum the operating profit of the major vendors: Apple takes about 70%, Samsung about 20%, and the rest split less than 10% (rough calculation). Yet Apple's unit shipment share is only 20%. This mismatch is the steepest the "smile curve" has ever been.

The reason is not that Apple charges more; it is that it sits in two positions at once: "brand" and "toll collector." Samsung earns USD 30 per phone sold; Apple earns USD 250 per phone sold (rough calculation: net profit of RMB 133.3 billion ÷ 240 million units). An 8x gap, and the gap lies in services and commissions, not hardware.

Is this distribution structure stable? Stable, but with two cracks: first, regulators are compressing commissions (services gross margin fell from 78% to 75.6%); second, Chinese vendors are moving upmarket (Huawei foldables, Xiaomi's premium push). Neither will change the landscape overnight, but both will slowly turn "70%" into "60%."

Implication for investors: the industry's "best asset" is not in dispute; the dispute is over the "best price." A company earning 70% of the profit should not be worth only 20x, but it should not be worth 34x either. The number in between depends on whether services can hold up.

Cycle position: demand is restocking, costs are rising

2024–2025 was a trough for the handset industry (global shipments fell for two consecutive years), and 2026 recovers as replacement demand returns.Apple is the biggest beneficiary: iPhone +21.7%, Mac +28.7%.

But costs are rising at the same time: DRAM up 60%–70%, NAND doubled, advanced nodes priced 5%–10% higher.Recovering demand plus rising costs is the worst combination for device makers—raising prices squeezes demand, holding prices squeezes gross margin.

Apple's choice is "modest price increases plus absorbing the rest itself," at the cost of a 2.6-point drop in gross margin.That choice shows it values share above gross margin—a signal worth remembering: when a company with pricing power starts prioritizing share, it usually means it lacks confidence in its product.

Relief most likely comes in the second half of 2027 (new memory capacity comes online), but even if prices fall, they will not return to 2024 levels—the memory capacity upgrade driven by AI is a permanent step-up.So the midpoint of gross margin should be 48%, not 50%.

Why foldables are a watershed

Foldables are not a new category; they are a form-factor upgrade in the premium market. Global foldable shipments will reach about 30 million units in 2026, with Huawei and Samsung taking 70%, and Apple still has no product. That gap is not obvious financially (30 million units against a 1.2 billion unit market), but it is fatal when it comes to the power to define premium.

Why? Because premium users are not just buying features; they are buying the idea that what they use represents the best. When Huawei uses foldable screens to redefine "the best" as "the one that folds," Apple's Pro models shift from "most advanced" to "most mature." Mature is a compliment, but in consumer electronics it means "no longer cool."

So the market's expectation for Apple's 2027 foldable is essentially an expectation of redefinition. If it delivers, the average price can rise from 1,050 to 1,800; if it remains absent, premium share will be carved away piece by piece—a slow process, but irreversible.

India: the only growth market, and the hardest to crack

The only part of the global phone market still growing is India (about 160 million units shipped in 2026, +6% year on year). Apple holds less than 5% share in India, but its premium-tier share is rising, and 2026 India revenue hit a record high (rough estimate).

Where is the difficulty? Price. Mainstream models in India sell for USD 200–300, while the iPhone starts at USD 799. Apple's response is "older models + local assembly + instalment plans" — using the iPhone 15/16 to attack the mid-range, using Indian plants to avoid the 20% import tariff, and using local financial institutions for 24-month interest-free plans.

This path can work, but slowly. For India to become "the second China", GDP per capita must double again, and that is a ten-year matter. So within Apple's decade of growth, India is a slow variable, not a catalyst.

Conclusion

Conclusion of Part Five: in a no-growth industry, Apple captured 70% of profits through the "rent-collection layer." But its share advantage is being eroded by form-factor innovation (foldables), and rising costs are squeezing its buffer.

The industry assigns Apple a "can hold, can't grow fast" position — which determines a fair multiple of 25–30x, not 40x.
