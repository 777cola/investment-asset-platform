⑤ Industry and competition: who is raising prices in a no-growth industry — Apple

Core view

The easiest mistake to make about this industry is to conclude that "the phone market is saturated, so Apple is finished." Saturation is real, but "saturated" and "unprofitable" are two different things. This piece examines how profit is allocated in an industry that isn't growing.

Industry structure: five players divide the market, each occupying a single price band

Global smartphone shipments should reach about 1.2 billion units in 2026, up slightly YoY. Share: Samsung about 19%, Apple 20%, Xiaomi about 14%, Transsion about 9%, OPPO/vivo combined about 16%. No one exceeds 20%, but no one is losing their footing either.

The structure is one of "tiered competition" rather than "homogeneous competition." Apple defends the above-USD 800 segment, Samsung defends all price points, Xiaomi defends USD 200–600, Transsion defends emerging markets. Each tier has its own cost structure and channels; crossing tiers to attack is not easy.

Price wars therefore break out mainly in the mid-tier (Xiaomi, OPPO, vivo, Honor), while the high end is instead a "form-factor war" — foldables, AI features, imaging. Apple's position at the high end is solid, but it is no longer "the sole innovator" — the biggest change of the past five years.

The upstream supply chain follows an entirely different logic: memory (Samsung, SK hynix, Micron) and foundry (TSMC) post gross margins of 50%–58%, built on technological monopoly; assembly (Hon Hai) posts a 6% gross margin, built on efficiency. Apple sits at the brand end and takes 70% of profits, at the cost of having no pricing power over its upstream suppliers.

Profit distribution: Apple takes 70% because it sits at the rent-collection layer

Sum the operating profit of the major vendors and Apple takes roughly 70%, Samsung about 20%, with the rest splitting less than 10% (rough calculation). Yet Apple's unit shipment share is only 20%. That mismatch is the steepest the "smile curve" has ever been.

The reason is not that Apple charges more; it is that it occupies two positions at once: "brand" and "toll collector." Samsung earns USD 30 per phone sold; Apple earns USD 250 per phone sold (rough calculation: net profit of RMB 133.3 billion ÷ 240 million units). An 8x gap, and it sits in services and commissions, not hardware.

Is this distribution structure stable? Stable, but with two cracks: first, regulators are compressing commissions (services gross margin fell from 78% to 75.6%); second, Chinese vendors are moving upmarket (Huawei foldables, Xiaomi's premium push). Neither will change the landscape overnight, but both will slowly turn "70%" into "60%."

Implication for investors: the industry's "best asset" is not in dispute; the dispute is over the "best price." A company earning 70% of the profit should not be worth only 20x, but it should not be worth 34x either. The number in between depends on whether services can hold up.

Cycle position: demand is restocking, costs are rising

2024–2025 marked the trough for handsets, with global shipments falling two years running; 2026 recovers as replacement demand returns.Apple is the biggest beneficiary: iPhone +21.7%, Mac +28.7%.

Costs, however, are rising in tandem: DRAM up 60%–70%, NAND doubled, advanced nodes priced 5%–10% higher.Recovering demand plus rising costs is the worst combination for device makers—raising prices squeezes demand, holding prices squeezes gross margin.

Apple has opted for modest price increases while absorbing the rest, at the cost of a 2.6-point drop in gross margin.The choice shows it values share above gross margin—a signal worth remembering: when a company with pricing power starts prioritising share, it usually means it lacks confidence in its product.

Relief most likely arrives in the second half of 2027, when new memory capacity comes online, but even then prices will not return to 2024 levels—the AI-driven memory capacity upgrade is a permanent step-up.So the midpoint of gross margin should be 48%, not 50%.

Why foldables are a watershed

Foldables are not a new category so much as a form-factor upgrade within the premium market. Global foldable shipments will reach about 30 million units in 2026, with Huawei and Samsung taking 70%, while Apple still has no product. Financially the gap looks immaterial—30 million units against a 1.2 billion unit market—but it is fatal to the power to define premium.

Why? Because premium users are not just buying features; they are buying the idea that what they use represents the best. When Huawei uses foldable screens to redefine "the best" as "the one that folds," Apple's Pro models shift from "most advanced" to "most mature." Mature is a compliment, but in consumer electronics it means "no longer cool."

So the market's expectation for Apple's 2027 foldable is essentially an expectation of redefinition. If it delivers, the average price can rise from 1,050 to 1,800; if it remains absent, premium share will be carved away piece by piece—a slow process, but irreversible.

India: the only growth market, and the hardest to crack

India is the only part of the global handset market still growing (about 160 million units shipped in 2026, +6% YoY). Apple holds less than 5% share in India, but its premium-tier share is rising, and 2026 India revenue hit a record high (rough estimate).

The constraint is price. Mainstream models in India sell for USD 200–300, versus a USD 799 starting price for the iPhone. Apple's response — older models, local assembly and instalment plans — pitches the iPhone 15/16 at the mid-range, uses Indian plants to sidestep the 20% import tariff, and leans on local financial institutions for 24-month interest-free plans.

The approach can work, but slowly. For India to become "the second China", GDP per capita must double again — a ten-year proposition. Within Apple's decade of growth, India is therefore a slow variable, not a catalyst.

Conclusion

Part Five conclusion: in a no-growth industry, Apple captured 70% of profits through the "rent-collection layer." Form-factor innovation (foldables) is eroding that share advantage, however, and rising costs are squeezing its buffer.

The industry frames Apple as "can hold, can't grow fast" — which supports a fair multiple of 25–30x, not 40x.
