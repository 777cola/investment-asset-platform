① Cognitive reset: this is not a hardware company, it is a toll collector — Apple

Eight Long Reads · Part One
Research date 2026/08/03  ·  Price $303.42 (-1.78%)  ·  Market cap About USD 4.41 trillion  ·  PE(TTM，FY2026Q3) Approximately 34x
← Back to the hub

Core view

Most people still see Apple as it was in 2012: a one-product phone company whose run ends with Steve Jobs's legacy. By 2026 that view no longer holds at all.

Old view one: Apple is a hardware company

Hardware is a "make a margin on every unit sold" business: fixed gross margin, pronounced cycles, and a moat built on product strength. Apply that framework to Apple and you reach the conclusion that "once the iPhone peaks, it's over" — a call many investors made in 2016, right before missing a tenfold gain.

The real Apple is "hardware + annual fees." Services revenue is USD 30.739 billion a quarter, USD 123 billion annualized, 28.1% of revenue, at a 75.6% gross margin. That amounts to installing a toll booth on 2.3 billion devices: users buy the device, then pay again every year. Services' stability hedges out hardware's cyclicality.

The comparison makes it clear: at pure hardware companies (Hon Hai at 6.1% gross margin, Xiaomi at 19.8%), profit swings with the cycle; Apple's net income ran from USD 45.7 billion in 2016 to an estimated USD 133.3 billion in FY2026E, an almost straight line upward for ten years, with only a small pullback in 2019. That is not a hardware curve; it is a subscription curve.

Another angle: in 2016 Apple sold 215 million iPhones and earned USD 45.7 billion in net income; in 2026 it will sell about 240 million and is expected to earn USD 133.3 billion. Units are up only 12%, yet net income has nearly tripled. All the incremental growth comes from average selling price and services, not from selling more.

Old perception #2: Apple is a one-product company, dependent on the iPhone

iPhone still accounts for 49.6% of revenue, but it is no longer "a product" — it is "an entry point." Step through that entry point and there are seven toll roads: App Store commissions, iCloud subscriptions, Apple Music, TV+, payments, advertising and AppleCare.

Together those seven lines run at USD 123 billion annualized on almost no capex. Their cost is servers and bandwidth; marginal cost is close to zero. That is the difference between "collecting rent" and "selling goods": selling goods requires materials, production lines and inventory; collecting rent requires only that users stay.

So the call that "iPhone has peaked" is not wrong in itself, but it does not lead to "Apple has peaked." iPhone shipments peaked back in 2015 (230 million units), and over the following decade Apple's revenue still rose 80%. A peak in volume and a peak in profit are not the same peak.

The reverse also needs stating clearly: if services revenue ever starts to decline, that is the real peak. Because services are "cash flow from the installed base" — once they decline, it means users are not "slowing their upgrades" but "leaving the ecosystem." The two signals mean entirely different things.

New thesis: a company with three overlapping identities

The right way to frame this is a three-layer stack: layer one is a hardware company (iPhone, Mac, iPad, wearables; 72% of revenue at a 40.1% gross margin); layer two is a subscription business (Services; 28% of revenue at a 75.6% gross margin); layer three is a distribution platform (App Store commissions, default search placement, rent collected from developers and from Google).

Each layer commands a wholly different multiple: hardware at 20–26x, subscription at 40–45x, platform at around 30x (held down by regulation). Weighted by gross profit, that works out to exactly 33.6x — which is where the current price of USD 303 comes from. The market is not pricing randomly; it is pricing off this structure.

This is where the cognitive reset matters: treat Apple as a hardware company and 34x looks absurdly expensive; treat it as a subscription company and 34x is reasonable. Both views are internally coherent; the difference is whether you believe Services can keep growing 15%. FY2026Q3 answered that it cannot quite — growth fell to 12.1%.

So this piece is not out to vindicate Apple; it is out to take the valuation apart. Do that and you find that of the 34x, 26x is a fair multiple for the hardware side and 44x an optimistic one for the services side. The moment the services side admits it is wrong, the whole multiple comes down.

Why most people get this multiple wrong

Backing out the growth requirement from the P/E is the simplest move, and the one fewest people make. A 34x P/E means the market demands net profit CAGR above 9% over the next decade just to deliver an 8% annualized return (rough math: a 2.9% initial earnings yield at 34x, plus 9% growth, minus the drag from valuation mean reversion).

What was Apple's net profit CAGR over the past decade? From RMB 45.7 billion in 2016 to RMB 133.3 billion in FY2026E, an 11.3% ten-year CAGR. That looks sufficient. But break it down: half of that 11.3% came from buybacks (share count down about 25%) and gross margin expansion (46.5% to 50.1%), with only 4% coming from revenue.

That is the problem: the growth engines of the past decade (buybacks plus gross margin) have both topped out — net cash is down to RMB 30 billion, and gross margin has peaked and is rolling over. The next decade must regenerate 9% from revenue plus services, a far harder task than the last ten years.

Conclusion

Part One conclusion: Apple carries three overlapping identities, and its valuation can be derived on a gross-profit-weighted basis. But the current 34x rests on an assumption of 15% services growth — an assumption that broke last quarter.

After the cognitive reset, the question is no longer whether Apple is a good company but whether the services leg can still support 44x.
