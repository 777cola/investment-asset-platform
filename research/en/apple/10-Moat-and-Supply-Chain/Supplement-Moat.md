## Position in the value chain: design, systems and channels, with all three segments covered

Apple's position in the supply chain can be summed up in one sentence: it does not manufacture, but it decides what gets made, how it gets made, and who it gets sold to. Chips are fabricated by TSMC but designed by Apple, the operating system is in-house, channels are a mix of direct retail and carriers, and services are distributed through the App Store.

At the core of this position is "definition power." Apple sets the performance targets for its A-series chips and TSMC executes; Apple sets the iPhone BOM and suppliers quote; Apple sets the App Store commission rate and developers accept it. Definition power is far scarcer than production capacity—capacity can be built with money, but definition power depends on the ecosystem.

But definition power has boundaries. On memory, Apple can only "accept the price + lock in long-term agreements," not define it; on advanced process nodes, it can only "accept price hikes + queue," not substitute. In other words, Apple's strength is concentrated in what it designs itself, and its weakness is concentrated in what it does not produce itself. This boundary is the source of this round of gross margin decline.

The specific structure of the supply chain is worth listing out: chips are fabricated by TSMC (A20, M6), memory comes from Samsung, SK hynix, Micron and Kioxia, CMOS from Sony, lenses from Largan and Sunny Optical, displays from Samsung Display and LG Display, acoustics from Goertek, and assembly is split among Hon Hai, Luxshare and Pegatron, with India accounting for less than 20% of capacity (rough estimate). Twenty core suppliers, and apart from memory, all of them can be replaced—this is what "tiered bargaining power" actually looks like.

## Moat, item by item: the hardest to replicate is switching costs

Ecosystem switching costs score 9, and the driver is behavioural rather than technical: photos in iCloud, chats in iMessage, watch and earbuds paired, subscriptions tied to an Apple ID, Family Sharing linked together. Switching phones means re-logging into twenty apps, migrating twenty thousand photos and re-pairing three devices. That hassle is worth USD 200 to most people — precisely the price gap between an iPhone and a comparable Android handset.

Brand scores 9, with caveats. Among global premium phones Apple is the default option; a 20% share this quarter with average selling prices still rising shows the brand at work. The 1-point deduction reflects foldables siphoning off share in China — once "form factor" becomes the reason to buy, brand premium is diluted by functional differentiation.

Cost and supply chain score 7, network effects 6, licences 3. None of these three is a "moat"; they are "buffers": the supply chain can absorb a short-term shortage but not sustained price increases; network effects hold only within the Apple user base; and on licences Apple is a negative asset — it is regulated, not licence-dependent.

Why only 6 for network effects? Because this is a "local network." iMessage is free and works well between Apple users, but the moment the other party is on Android, that advantage breaks. A true network effect means "the more users, the more valuable to each user"; Apple's user growth does not increase value for existing users — what it adds are weak effects such as "second-hand accessories are easier to buy, repairs more convenient." That is the fundamental difference between it and WeChat or Meta.

The scores add up to 34/50 — "very hard but asymmetric." Asymmetric means 9 on defence, 5 on offence. It can hold on to the users of 2.3 billion devices, but cannot break into the low-price markets of India and Africa; it can absorb one shortage, but not three years of price increases. This asymmetry caps growth at 8%–10%, not 15%.

## The most dangerous competitor: storage first, Huawei second

Many would rank Huawei as the first threat; my ranking differs. The first threat is the memory trio: Samsung, SK hynix and Micron together hold over 90% of DRAM, leaving Apple no choice but to accept price increases. The gross margin guidance cut this quarter, from 50.1% to 47%–48%, is precisely that bill. This threat does not need to beat Apple; it only needs to keep raising prices.

The second threat is Huawei, but only in China's above-RMB 6,000 segment. Huawei's strengths are foldable form factor + HarmonyOS ecosystem + in-house chips; its weaknesses are capacity and overseas channels. The damage to Apple is "carving away a high-margin market," not "replacing Apple"—Greater China did USD 18.8 billion this quarter; if that falls to USD 15 billion, global growth drops 3 points.

The third threat is Google, in a different form. Pixel does not sell, but Gemini is free to all Android vendors, effectively turning AI into a public good. If AI is a public good, Apple cannot charge a premium for AI—the most fragile assumption in Apple's valuation, and the hardest to defend.

What the three share: none of them is taking Apple's users head-on. Memory is taking its gross margin, Huawei a slice of the China market, Google the premise of "can AI be monetized." This is not a question of "who replaces Apple" but of "can Apple still sustain 34x"—the moat protects the company, not the multiple.

If I had to rank the three threats by "when it happens," the answer is: memory is happening now (persisting over the next four quarters), Huawei will show results in the next two quarters (iPhone 18 first sales in China), Google will only become visible in the next two years (as the AI charging model settles). The three have different time scales, and different implications for positioning—near term, watch gross margin; long term, watch the entry point.
