# Apple（AAPL）— Duan Yongping Supplement

- Research date：2026/08/03
- Price：303.42 USD
- Market cap：About USD 4.41 trillion

## Notes

This Q&A simulates how Duan Yongping would apply his publicly stated methodology; it is not his own view. Historical purchase P/E ratios (about 13x in 2011, about 10x in 2016) are drawn from public interviews and Apple's historical share price. Financial data is from the FY2026Q3 report (2026/07/30). The USD 260 entry point is FY2027 EPS of USD 10.0 × 26x.

## Q1: Is this a good business?

Good. What makes it good? It does not sell hardware; it collects rent on the experience. Users pay USD 1,000 for a phone, then pay subscription fees every year, spend on the App Store, buy earphones and watches — acquire a customer once, charge them for years. Apple is the only company in the world to have pulled this off.

The numbers bear this out: gross margin 50.1%, services gross margin 75.6%, net margin 27.2%. A company that sells phones earns a higher net margin than most software companies — which shows it is not selling phones.

So I often say Apple's business model is "charge the highest premium with the best product." That sounds like a slogan, but it is verifiable: the overall market is not growing, yet its share still rises to 20%; others cut prices, yet it still raises them. That is the business model speaking, not luck.

One more point: a good business has another trait — "it does not need to keep pouring money in." Apple's annual capex is only 2.6% of revenue, and nearly all the money it earns is free cash flow. By contrast, TSMC must invest 30% of revenue in fabs and memory makers 40%, while Apple "earns its own gross margin on other people's capacity" — that is what I call a "good business."

## Question 2: Will it still be around in ten years?

Yes — but be clear what "yes" means: the company will be around; the phone form factor may not be. These are two separate questions.

The company survives because its cash flow is too thick: roughly USD 103 billion in free cash flow a year, USD 30 billion in net cash, and brand pricing power intact. A company like this does not die from a single technology shift; it has ten years to adjust.

The phone form factor, however, is at risk. If AI turns interaction into "voice + glasses," the iPhone goes from "entry point" to "accessory." Apple has Vision Pro, but a million units a year cannot support a second growth curve. My judgment: Apple will still be here in ten years, but the way it makes money may not be the way it does now. The impact on valuation is greater than the impact on profit.

## Question three: Do you understand it?

I understand it. That is what I like most: its revenue structure has not changed in twenty years—sell hardware, charge service fees, take a cut of app revenue. All three segments can be modelled separately, forecast and verified.

Contrast that with AI companies: no one can say clearly what their revenue will rest on three years from now, model capabilities turn over every six months, and customer budgets get cut at a moment's notice. I do not invest in that kind of thing—not because it is bad, but because I do not understand it. If I do not understand something, I will not dare add to my position even when it falls, and then holding it is meaningless.

I understand Apple, so I dare to buy when it falls—provided the price is right. Understanding it is a necessary condition, not a sufficient one.

## Question 4: At what price to buy

When I bought in 2011 the P/E was 13x; when I added in 2016 it was just over 10x. Back then no one was bidding, because everyone thought phones were about to saturate. Now it is 34x, and everyone thinks it still has AI. That is the difference: a good price always shows up when no one wants it.

By my math, FY2027 EPS of USD 10 at 26x — USD 260 — carries a margin of safety. Below USD 280 you can start looking, below USD 260 you can act, below USD 240 you can build a heavy position. At USD 303, I do not act.

Some will ask: what if it never falls? If it does not fall, I do not buy — that is not a loss, that is discipline. The market offers opportunities every year, and cash in hand does not bite. The real risk is not missing out; it is buying at the wrong price and then being unable to hold.

One more calculation: buy at 34x, and even if profit grows 10% a year, with the P/E back to 26x after five years, your annualized return is just over 4%. Buy at 26x, with the same growth, and it is 8% to 9%. Same company — the only difference is one number on the day you buy.

## Question 5: What scares you most?

The biggest fear is not Huawei, nor falling behind in AI. The biggest fear is the company giving away its gross margin itself.

Look at this quarter: gross margin 50.1%, next-quarter guidance 47%–48%. Memory price increases are real, but Apple has brand and pricing power, so in theory it could raise prices and pass them through. It didn't. It chose to absorb them. Why? Because it fears losing share.

When a company with pricing power starts fearing share loss, it signals that it no longer believes its product strength is sufficient. That is far more serious than a 2-point drop in gross margin. A fallen gross margin can recover; a changed mindset is hard to reverse. This is the only thing I worry about, and the reason I check gross margin guidance every quarter.

## Question 6: Why not buy a small position first?

Buy a little, within 2%. Not to make money, but to force yourself to look carefully. Research without a position is armchair theorising; only with a position will you read the financials every quarter, calculate gross margins and watch Greater China.

But only 2%. No more, because the odds are wrong—23% upside, 29% downside, and a 4% loss even in the neutral case. Going heavy at these odds is a bet on conviction, not on calculation.

I often say: the most important thing in investing is "what not to do." For Apple right now, the most important thing is to do nothing—finish the research, draw the price line, then wait. When that day comes, you'll find buying takes only a minute, because the answer was worked out long ago.
