Core view

Li Lu does not look at a single quarter. What he looks at is: where this earnings report places Apple within its "ten-year story". His answer: the tail end of the hardware story, the middle of the services story, the beginning of the AI story.

The end of the hardware story: volumes no longer growing, but room left on price

iPhone revenue rose 21.7%, but this is a replacement cycle, not new users. In a global market of 1.2 billion units, Apple's 20% share is already near its historical peak (17% in 2021). There is little room for share to rise further; the only remaining lever is average selling price.

Where is the ceiling on ASP? iPhone's average selling price has already climbed from USD 750 in 2019 to roughly USD 1,050 in 2026 (rough estimate). Pushing it higher means confronting the psychological threshold of "RMB 20,000 for a phone"—a threshold already reached in the China market.

The middle chapter of the services story: 12% growth is the new normal

Services came in at RMB 30.739 billion, +12.1%, the first stall for this line in more than three years. Li Lu's view: this is not an accident but a sign that this line is maturing. 1.5 billion paid subscriptions are already close to saturation (global smartphone users number about 4.5 billion, and Apple covers a third of them), so further growth must come from ARPU rather than user count.

What drives ARPU higher? Turning AI into a paid service. That is why he zeroed in on the line from the earnings call that "paid value-added services have yet to generate revenue at scale" — that statement shows the company itself has not figured out how to price AI, not merely that it has yet to start charging.

The AI story's opening: right position, slow pace

Apple is actually well positioned in AI: 2.3 billion devices, 1.5 billion subscriptions, the strongest on-device chips in the world. It does not need to win the model race; it only needs to make models run best on its devices. Siri AI, launching in June, points in exactly that direction.

But the pace is slow. Google, OpenAI and Meta are all using free AI to capture the entry point, while Apple is still waiting for "on-device models to mature." Li Lu's judgment: the winner on this path is not the one with the strongest model but the one with the broadest distribution — and Apple has the broadest distribution. That is why he is willing to give it a price score of 4 (rather than 2).

He also watches two "decade variables." First, India: the world's second-largest handset market, where Apple's share is under 5%, but its premium-tier share is rising, and 2026 India revenue is at a record high (rough estimate). Second, foldables: the market expects Apple to launch one in 2027, and if it does, that is the only path to pushing the average price from USD 1,050 toward USD 1,800. Both are "possible but not yet happened"; his approach is to assign them zero valuation and give them only two slots on the watchlist.

Conclusion

Li Lu-style conclusion: on a ten-year view, Apple's position is better than its valuation. Hardware is winding down, services are maturing, AI is just beginning; of the three chapters, the third holds the most value but the most uncertainty. One can build a position below USD 260 and hold for ten years.
