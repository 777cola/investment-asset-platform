Buffett reads financial reports: gross margin, cash flow, buyback efficiency — Apple

Core view

Every time Buffett reads an earnings report, he does three things: check the change in gross margin, check whether cash flow covers dividends and buybacks, then check what management did with the money. This report answers all three: two good, one bad.

Gross margin: 50.1% is a good number, but unsustainable

Gross margin of 50.1%, up 3.6 points YoY, on gross profit of RMB 54.77 billion — among the highest in Apple's history. But he would immediately ask: where did those 3.6 points come from?

Breaking it down, Services held at 75.6%, so the entire increase came from Products: product gross margin rose from roughly 34.5% to 40.1%, up 5.6 points in a year. Of that, about 2 points came from tariff refunds (USD 1.6 billion), about 2 points from the Pro mix and about 1.5 points from reduced promotions. Two of the three are unsustainable — that is the real meaning of next quarter's 47%–48% guidance.

His verdict: a company's best quarter is often the quarter least suited to valuing it. Extrapolating 50.1% gross margin into 2027 amounts to pricing a decade off the best single year.

One comparison: 50.1% is below Coca-Cola (about 60%) and Microsoft (about 69%), but neither needs to spend USD 40 billion a year on R&D to stay competitive. A hardware company reaching 50% does so by pushing cost pressure onto suppliers and premium pricing onto users — the ability to push on both ends is what a moat means.

Cash flow and buybacks: the machine is still running, but mind the cost

Free cash flow runs at about USD 103 billion a year, buybacks at USD 100 billion and dividends at USD 15.6 billion. He knows this combination well — it is what he calls "what a good company should do": return money to shareholders instead of making reckless acquisitions. Apple has made no major acquisition in fourteen years, and he admires that.

But he would also note that net cash has fallen from over USD 80 billion three years ago to about USD 30 billion. That is not danger; it is the inevitable result of spending on buybacks faster than it earns. If net cash turns negative in 2028, Apple will have to choose between continuing buybacks and preserving financial flexibility — and at that point the pace of buybacks must slow.

His conclusion matches ours: this is a company you can hold for twenty years, but USD 303 is not a buy price. His buy line has always been below 25x, which corresponds to USD 250.

Finally, the ratio he cares about most: shareholder earnings (net income + depreciation − maintenance capex) divided by market cap. On FY2026E net income of USD 133.3 billion and maintenance capex of about USD 4 billion, shareholder earnings are about USD 129 billion, or 2.9% of the USD 4.41 trillion market cap. That yield sits below the ten-year Treasury (about 4.1%) — the plainest yardstick Buffett uses to judge whether something is expensive, and the reason he is not buying a single share.

Conclusion

Buffett-style verdict: business A, capital allocation A, price C. Gross margin has peaked and cash flow is healthy, with generous buybacks, but the valuation is not cheap. Wait for a price below USD 250, or for buyback discipline to improve.
