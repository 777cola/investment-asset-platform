Duan Yongping on reading financial reports: focus only on whether users are still willing to pay more — Apple

Core view

Duan Yongping reads financial reports differently from most. He starts not with profit but with a single question: are users still willing to pay more for this thing. This quarter, the answer is yes.

The evidence he seeks: flat volume, rising price

iPhone revenue was USD 54.252 billion, up 21.7% YoY, while unit shipments grew only in the single digits. That gap is evidence of rising average selling prices — in an industry with no volume growth, the only company that can raise prices is the one with pricing power.

He also tracks a second number: global share rising to 20%. Share and average selling price rising together shows Apple is not "cutting prices to buy volume" but "raising prices and still gaining volume." Both happening at once has occurred only twice in consumer electronics history: once with the large-screen iPhone in 2014, and once now.

There is counter-evidence too: iPad revenue fell 5.9%. He does not see this as a problem — the iPad's problem is that "it works so well that there is no need to upgrade," a side effect of product strength, not a decline in competitiveness.

He also looks at the services line: USD 30.739 billion, 28% of revenue, 1.5 billion paid subscriptions. This amounts to adding an "annual fee" layer on top of the hardware business — users buy the device, then pay every year. This structure is unique in consumer electronics, and it is the fundamental reason Apple's gross margin reaches 50%.

The item he was least satisfied with: the buyback price

Duan Yongping is clear on buybacks: a buyback only creates value when the price is below intrinsic value. Apple repurchases USD 100 billion a year; at USD 230 that is smart, above USD 300 it is handing money to sellers.

He runs the math: buying back at 34x earnings means paying USD 1 for USD 0.029 of current earnings; at 13x (the 2013 level), USD 0.077. Same money, 2.6x difference in efficiency. So he gives no valuation credit simply because "the company is buying back"; he asks instead: why not wait until it is cheap?

His summary runs roughly like this: I understand this business and would happily hold it for ten years; but today's price reflects "what others are willing to pay for it," not "what it is worth." Making money in stocks comes from buying cheap, not from buying right.

One more question he would press: in ten years, will users still pay USD 1,000 for "a device"? His answer leans optimistic — people pay for convenience, and that demand will not disappear. But if AI turns the "device" into a "subscription," Apple's charging model is forced to change, from a one-time USD 1,000 to USD 30 a month. That is good for cash flow (more stable) and bad for current revenue (slower recognition). This is the one thing he finds "impossible to see through."

Conclusion

Duan Yongping-style verdict: business 9 out of 10, price 3 out of 10. Flat volumes with rising prices confirm pricing power intact; a 20% share confirms competitiveness intact; buybacks at 34x, however, count against capital allocation. Wait on price; make no move.
