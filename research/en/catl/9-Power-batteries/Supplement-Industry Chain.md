## Upstream, it buys more lithium than anyone in the world, yet owns not a single large mine of its own

CATL is the world's largest battery maker and the world's largest buyer of lithium, nickel and graphite. Procurement scale brings discounts—one source of its cost advantage over second-tier players. But "largest buyer" does not equal "pricing power": when lithium prices rose from RMB 50,000/tonne to RMB 600,000/tonne in 2022, it still had no choice but to accept them.

So it reached upstream: taking stakes in lithium mines, in-house cathode supply, and battery recycling. Recycling is the smartest piece—battery recycling is effectively an "urban mine": the feedstock is batteries others have used up, and its cost position actually becomes more competitive as lithium prices rise. In the first half of 2026, battery materials and recycling revenue was RMB 31.531 billion with a gross margin of 27.04%, 6.4 points higher than power batteries—evidence for this logic.

But the scale is still small: RMB 31.5 billion is only 11.4% of total revenue and cannot change the basic pattern of "costs fluctuating with lithium prices." In the upstream link, CATL is strong but not the dominant player.

## Midstream: it defines this segment and sets its ceiling

Cell manufacturing is the most technology- and capital-intensive link in the chain. CATL's position comes down to three numbers: 40.2% global share, 498GWh of output in the first half, and 94.86% capacity utilisation. In manufacturing, a 40% share usually confers pricing power; in batteries, it confers only cost leadership.

Why? Because downstream customers (automakers) deliberately cultivate second suppliers. Cells are a standardised product (provided they meet automotive-grade requirements), and the price of a standardised product is set by marginal capacity, not by the leader. CATL's 20.63% gross margin is what it earns above marginal capacity, not a brand premium.

A further midstream shift: integration is moving into the hands of automakers. Automakers are building their own PACK, developing their own BMS and even their own cell plants (BYD, GAC, Geely), and CATL's length along the chain is being cut short from both ends.

## Downstream: automakers are squeezing prices, leaving energy storage as the only outlet still commanding a premium

There are two downstream buyers: automakers and energy-storage customers. Automakers work to "cut costs 5%–10% a year"—standard practice in the vehicle industry—and that pressure is passed all the way down to the cell. Energy-storage customers, by contrast, buy on "cost per kWh," a more direct price comparison—so although the 23.96% energy-storage gross margin is higher than for power batteries, it is also falling (-1.56pct).

The only place still carrying a premium is overseas. Overseas revenue was RMB 87.13 billion at a 29.97% gross margin, 8.8 points above domestic. That premium comes not from technology but from "supply security + carbon-footprint compliance + localised delivery"—European automakers are willing to pay for certainty.This is the only corner of the entire chain not yet contaminated by the price war, and it is the anchor for CATL's profit over the next three years.

## Cycle position: demand remains strong, capacity heading for oversupply

The 2026 picture is clear. Demand is still growing above 50% (CATL revenue +54.8%, volume +60%), yet capacity under construction on the supply side is enormous (CATL 764GWh, with second-tier players expanding in parallel). This is a classic capacity-expansion wave ahead of the cycle peak.

The solar parallel: 12–18 months after the expansion wave, the price war intensifies, gross margins bottom, and second- and third-tier players are cleared out. Batteries differ in their safety barriers and automotive-grade certification, so the shakeout will be slower and gentler, but the direction is the same.

Investment implication: 2027–2028 is the pressure period for gross margins, and only after 2029 is a post-shakeout margin recovery likely. So the time horizon for this investment is at least three years, not three months.

## Quantifying bargaining power: whom can it actually squeeze?

Turning bargaining power into numbers: days payable outstanding vs days sales outstanding. CATL has strong leverage over upstream suppliers (long payable days) and collects quickly from downstream customers (short receivable days, because automakers must pay deposits to lock in capacity). The gap between the two is its "free working capital."

That gap should still be widening in the first half of 2026 — revenue +54.8%, procurement scale expanding in tandem, absolute float increasing. But watch the direction: once share slips, suppliers will demand shorter payment terms, the gap will narrow fast, and cash flow will deteriorate before profit does. This is the dividend its position in the chain confers, and also its vulnerability.

Compare upstream: lithium salt plants have short payable days (mostly cash on delivery), separator makers next, cathode makers longest. CATL's bargaining power is strongest over cathode makers and weakest over separator makers (Semcorp holding 30%–40% gross margin is the evidence). Who it can squeeze and who it cannot comes down to whether the counterparty has exclusivity.
