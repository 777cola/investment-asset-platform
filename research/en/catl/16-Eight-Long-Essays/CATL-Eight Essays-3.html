Core view

Split the moat into five parts: scale, cost, the customer validation cycle, capital threshold and customer relationships. The first four still hold; the fifth is thinning. Decomposing it this way beats a vague claim that "there is a moat", because each part decays at a different rate.

Scale and cost are two sides of the same coin

The value of scale lies not in being "big" but in "dilution." First-half output reached 498GWh at 94.86% capacity utilisation, versus 60%–80% for second-tier players.On the same production line, 94% versus 70% utilisation implies a unit fixed-cost gap of over 25%. That is why CATL can cut prices and still profit, while second-tier players lose money when they do.

Scale also brings procurement discounts: as the world's largest buyer, CATL has the strongest bargaining power over cathodes, anodes and electrolyte, and the weakest over separators—Semcorp's ability to hold a 30%–40% gross margin is proof.Whom it can squeeze depends on whether the other side has exclusivity.

Both advantages are widening: once the 764GWh under construction comes online, the scale advantage will step up again.But the premise is that the capacity can be sold—when the industry runs into oversupply in 2027–2028, scale will turn from an advantage into a burden.

The catch-up speed of second-tier players deserves separate mention: CALB, EVE and Gotion will successively complete automotive-grade production line validation in 2024–2026. Although their capacity utilisation in 2026 will be only 60%–80%, "being able to deliver" is already established.The test of a moat is not "whether rivals can do it" but "how much advantage you have left when they can"—what remains now is a 10%–15% cost gap.

Third and fourth: validation cycles and capital thresholds are being bypassed via "equity"

Validation used to be a hard barrier: switching suppliers takes an automaker two years, requires re-certification and carries recall risk.In 2026, however, automakers found a way around it — not by switching suppliers, but by taking a stake in them.Li Auto injected RMB 2.65 billion into Sunwoda Power for an 11.17% stake, bundling validation cost and equity alignment into a single solution.

Capital was circumvented the same way: building 100GWh costs RMB 30–40 billion over five years, which second-tier players cannot fund alone, but automakers are willing to put up the money and local governments to match it.The barrier remains, but it is no longer "second-tier players can't get in" — it is "second-tier players can get in and still won't make money."

The shift matters:the moat has gone from "blocking entry" to "allowing entry but limiting returns."The former protects margins; the latter protects only share. The market has grasped this, which is why it cut the valuation from 30x to 14x.

Article 5: Customer relationships are shifting from "sole supplier" to "sole supplier + second supplier"

This is the fastest-decaying of the five. Li Auto's new L8 moves entirely to Sunwoda, Xiaomi's Longjia battery is dual-sourced, and AITO has brought in CALB and Gotion—all three in the second half of 2026, sooner than expected.

The magnitude is no cause for panic: Li Auto and Xiaomi together account for 13.6% of domestic installations. They can compress gross margin, but they cannot crush share. Once the paradigm takes hold, however, it will spread to 25%–30% of domestic installations in 2027–2028.

The moat's boundary is clear: it keeps out new entrants (five years, RMB 50 billion), but cannot stop an existing customer from adding a second supplier (only two years, a few billion RMB). That is what "deep but not wide" means.

The flip side of a moat: what is most likely to breach it

This is not a price war—CATL can withstand a price war (lowest cost). Nor is it new entrants—building a plant takes RMB 50 billion and five years. The most easily breached flank is the "customer structure": if two of the top five customers build their own batteries or bind deeply with second-tier suppliers, CATL's order pool shrinks in one stroke.

Signals have already appeared in 2026: Li Auto holds 11.17% of Sunwoda Power, Xiaomi dual-sources, and AITO adds a second supplier. Taken individually, all three are "one more supplier"; taken together, they mean "the customer structure is being reorganised." A moat is strong against external intrusion but weak against internal reorganisation.

Conclusion

Moat verdict: four of the five are intact; the fifth is thinning fast. Scale and cost are CATL's real moat — enough to keep it earning above peers for another decade. The validation cycle, capital threshold and customer relationships are being bypassed by a new paradigm: automakers taking stakes in second-tier suppliers. Investment implication: the moat is enough to thrive, not enough to guarantee margins hold — so buy, but buy at a manufacturing valuation.
