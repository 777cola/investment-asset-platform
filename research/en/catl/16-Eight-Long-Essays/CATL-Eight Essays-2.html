Part Two · The Nature of the Business: It Sells "the Most Expensive Component of the Vehicle" — CATL

Why customers pay, how much they pay, and for how long

Core view

Batteries account for 30%–40% of vehicle cost, making them the single most expensive component. That dictates three things: customers must choose suppliers carefully, must push hard on price, and must seriously prepare second sources. CATL's entire business logic rests on the tension among these three.

Why customers pay: not because it is cheap, but because "nothing goes wrong"

For automakers, the first criterion in choosing a battery is not price but safety and consistency. If a single cell fails, recall costs can reach several billion yuan, and brand damage is even greater. So they are willing to pay a premium for "stable yield and on-time delivery" — this is the fundamental reason CATL can earn a 29.97% gross margin in Europe.

The second criterion is co-development capability. CATL's engineers are stationed at automakers to work on envelope design, thermal management and BMS integration. Once this work becomes embedded in a vehicle platform, switching suppliers means redoing the entire vehicle validation. This is the stickiness of a "semi-custom component," stronger than any contract.

Price is only the third criterion. This also explains why second-tier players can win orders in 2026: not because they are cheap, but because they are "good enough." Once second-tier yield reaches the passing line of "no incidents," price becomes the deciding factor.

Working backward from the customer's books is more intuitive: for a RMB 200,000 EV, battery cost is about RMB 60,000–80,000 (6.0–8.0 million), or 30%–40%. If battery prices fall 10%, vehicle cost drops 2–3 points — in an era when vehicle gross margins are only 15%, those 2–3 points are the difference between life and death. This is the fundamental drive for automakers to push prices down and seek second suppliers, regardless of whether they like CATL.

Why gross margin has a ceiling: the fate of standardized products

Once a battery meets automotive-grade requirements, it is a standardized product. The price of a standardized product is set by marginal capacity—as long as second-tier capacity with 60% utilization remains in the industry, price will stay under pressure. CATL's 20.63% power battery gross margin is the premium earned over marginal capacity, not brand pricing power.

This ceiling is roughly 25%. It has already reached 23.93% (blended), and further gains must come from mix improvement (overseas share, energy storage share, in-house materials), not price increases. The margin assumption should therefore be "gradual decline + structural offset," not "mean reversion."

Compare with genuinely high-margin components: separators at 30%–40% (equipment and yield barriers), automotive electronics at 35%–45% (certification barriers + software). Batteries sit in the middle: higher than structural parts, lower than separators. That is their true position in the value chain.

How long this business can keep earning: three variables determine the endgame

The first variable is industry growth. Global electrification is still climbing and energy storage has only just begun, leaving at least five to eight years of volume growth. The second variable is share. With 40.2% global share and 46.7% domestic share, decline is the trend; the pace of decline is unknown. The third variable is technology route. Solid-state batteries may not reach scale until 2028; until then, liquid lithium battery capacity is a valid asset.

Taken together, the "shelf life" of this business is roughly five to eight years—long enough to be worth investing in, not long enough to ignore technology replacement. That is why it deserves only 15x, not 30x.

Who actually makes money in this chain

Line up the gross margins of each link in the chain: separator 30%–40%, battery cell 20%–24%, cathode 10%–15%, vehicle 15%–20%, resources 30%–50% (cyclical). CATL sits in the middle, higher than cathode and vehicle, lower than separator and resources.

What this position means: it cannot earn the money of "scarcity" (separator, resources), nor can it escape the pressure of "price comparison" (vehicle customers). What it earns is the money of "scale + yield," the most stable kind of money, and also the thinnest. Understand this, and you will not expect it to return to a 30x valuation.

Conclusion

Business essence: selling the most expensive component of a vehicle, earning a premium through "no failures" and "co-development," achieving cost advantage through scale, with the ceiling set by the price of standardized products.This is a good business, but not one you can coast on. Buying it means accepting two things: gross margin drifting slowly downward, and the possibility that technological generational change rewrites the story in five years.
