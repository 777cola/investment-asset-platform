Who switches technology, where and when — and when capacity becomes oversupplied

Core view

Reading the industry requires three maps: a geography map (who holds capacity, and where), a timeline (when the technology roadmap shifts) and a capacity map (when oversupply arrives). Only by overlaying all three does it become clear that CATL's position is "the best position", not "a safe position".

Geography: capacity follows customers, and customers follow policy

Three factors effectively set the geographic distribution of battery capacity in 2026: China's cost, Europe's carbon rules and the US subsidy threshold. CATL holds the largest capacity in China, has the most complete footprint in Europe (Thuringia, Germany already in production; Debrecen, Hungary starting production in 2026; a joint venture with Stellantis in Spain), and is largely absent from the US.

The implication: Europe is its profit zone (overseas gross margin 29.97%), the US its blank zone. The US market is split among LG, Panasonic, SK On and BYD (via Mexico/Thailand). The "40.2% globally" figure therefore excludes the US — both a gap and a safe buffer (insulated from direct US policy shocks).

How long can the European premium last? It depends on the pace of building local European capacity. CATL building its own plants in Europe is "locking in the premium through localization" and also "single-handedly ending the premium" — but it would rather be the terminator itself than let LG be.

Timeline: three milestones in the technology transition

Node one, 2026: semi-solid-state batteries enter small-scale vehicle installation, concentrated in high-end models. Costs are high and volumes small, so the impact on CATL is negligible. Node two, 2027: small-batch trial production of all-solid-state; a second-tier player announcing vehicle installation would trigger a narrative shock that "CATL's technology lags." Node three, 2028–2030: all-solid-state reaches scale, at which point depreciation and retrofitting of liquid-electrolyte capacity become a real financial problem.

That CATL does not lead this round is public fact. But its advantage is engineering capability — once the route is settled, it drives costs down faster than anyone. "Not leading" therefore does not equal "will lose"; it only means "no longer the default first."

Investment implication: technology nodes are the ceiling on valuation, not a current-period variable in profit. Before 2027, profit is set by share and price; after 2027, valuation is set by technology route.

Capacity chart: 2027–2028 are surplus years

CATL has 764GWh under construction and second-tier players are expanding in parallel, so global capacity growth is clearly outpacing demand growth. The direct consequence of oversupply is that price wars become the norm, and the power battery gross margin is highly likely to fall another 2–4 points from 20.63%.

The shape of the oversupply, however, differs from solar. Solar module capacity is homogeneous and the cheapest producer wins; battery capacity is heterogeneous (automotive-grade certification, yield, customer lock-in), so the shakeout is slower and gentler, manifesting as "second-tier players cannot make money but do not exit" rather than "second-tier players go bankrupt."

CATL's gross margin should therefore not collapse but grind along the 18%–21% range for a long time. Investment implication: do not expect a re-rating on gross margin recovery; expect only profit growth and an improving overseas mix.

Endgame projection: a three-way split, not a single winner

Stack the geography, technology and capacity maps together and the endgame is roughly "two Chinese players plus two overseas players": CATL (global + Europe), BYD (vehicle integration + exports), LG/Panasonic (North America), plus one local European plant (policy support).

Within this landscape CATL's share erodes slowly from 40.2% to 33%–36% and gross margin slips from 20.63% to 18%–20%, yet revenue still grows on rising industry volume. That is a "profit grows, valuation does not" combination — the typical endgame for a manufacturing leader.

If this scenario holds, the right way to own it is to hold for three years and earn the profit growth (15%–25% a year), not count on a re-rating. It also explains why our target price is only RMB 378 (+29%), not a double.

Why "de-CATL-ification" happened in 2026

Three forces have converged. First, second-tier capacity and yields are now in place—these players previously lost orders on inadequate delivery, a problem largely resolved in 2026. Second, automakers' margin pressure has reached a tipping point—two years of vehicle price wars have left batteries, at 30%–40% of cost, as the only major item still to compress. Third, battery technology has plateaued—with no generational gap, second-tier products are "good enough."

Automakers act only when all three conditions hold at once. It also means "de-CATLization" will not stop just because CATL cuts prices—what they want is not cheaper batteries, but more choice.

Conclusion

Sector view: best-in-class positioning, but not without risk. Geographically, Europe is the profit pool and the US a blank space; technologically, 2027 marks the inflection point; on capacity, 2027–2028 brings oversupply. The endgame is a three-way split, with CATL's share falling from 40% to 33%–36%. Investment implication: earn from profit growth, not multiple expansion.
