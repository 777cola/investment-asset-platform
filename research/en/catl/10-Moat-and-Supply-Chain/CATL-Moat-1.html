Core thesis

Moat scorecard, item by item: cost 9/10, scale and manufacturing 8/10, switching costs 7/10, brand 6/10, network effects 5/10. This is a "cost-driven + engineering-driven" moat, not a "brand-driven + network-driven" one.

Moats of this kind share one trait: they keep new entrants out — a plant costs RMB 50 billion and takes five years — but they cannot stop a customer from adding another supplier. That is exactly what happened in 2026, when Li Auto, Xiaomi and AITO all added second suppliers. The most dangerous rival is not CALB poaching orders, but BYD, which makes the battery part of the vehicle itself.

Position in the value chain: a midstream "final assembly plant" bridging upstream and downstream, but not the chain leader

CATL sits in the "materials into cells" segment of the chain: upstream are lithium, nickel, cathodes, anodes, separators, electrolytes, structural parts and equipment; downstream are modules/PACKs, vehicles and energy-storage systems. Its procurement scale is the world's largest, which earns it supplier discounts, and its product is a core component for automakers, which gives it bargaining power over customers. Together, these two ends make it the most profitable midstream company in the chain.

But it is not the chain master. The chain master is the one that defines standards — Tesla defined 4680, BYD defined the blade cell, and CATL defined the "large cell + high energy density" engineering route. Leadership in an engineering route is sustainable, but not irreplaceable. That is why its gross margin is only just above 20%, while TSMC reaches 58% in foundry.

Supply chain: its ability to stretch supplier payables is its most underappreciated advantage

A 60%–65% debt-to-asset ratio with interest-bearing debt below 10% — the flip side of that structure is that it owes its suppliers a lot of money. A huge accounts-payable balance and long payment terms mean it finances its own working capital with suppliers' money. It can do so because it is the world's largest buyer — suppliers dare not press for payment.

Downstream, it holds contract liabilities (automakers must pay deposits to lock in capacity). Capturing float at both ends is the cash-form expression of its scale advantage, and the reason it can hold out in a price war. Second-tier manufacturers cannot: they get no discounts from upstream and no prepayments from downstream, so any price cut hits cash flow first.

The risk: if share slips, its ability to hold float declines in tandem. This is a mechanism where positive feedback can also run in reverse — a 5-point share loss would lead suppliers to shorten payment terms, and cash flow would deteriorate before the income statement does.

Competitive comparison: five rivals, five playbooks

BYD is vertically integrated, uses its batteries in-house at the lowest cost and competes head-on with CATL's customers in the RMB 100,000–200,000 vehicle segment. CALB wins first-supplier status on mid-range models on price and responsiveness, while Sunwoda ties automakers in as shareholders through "joint ventures + equity" (Li Auto holds 11.17%). Gotion enters the Harmony Intelligent Mobility Alliance via Huawei's "Jupiter" battery supply chain, and LG Energy Solution takes share of European and US procurement on the back of North American local capacity.Of the five playbooks, only BYD's is structural; the rest are price-based.

The most dangerous rival: BYD, not CALB

CALB is fighting for orders; BYD is fighting over the premise of whether batteries should be bought externally at all. If every automaker builds its own batteries like BYD, CATL's customer pool shrinks permanently—this is not a share problem, it is a market-definition problem.

BYD's blade battery plus vehicle integration pushes battery cost to the industry's lowest, and it amortizes R&D without selling externally. For every car it sells in the RMB 10–20 million price band, it takes one battery's worth of demand away from CATL.In the first half of 2026 BYD held about 16% global share, the only player with a chance of catching CATL by carrying batteries through whole vehicles.

CATL's response is to move upmarket and overseas: premium models (Qilin, Shenxing) and overseas customers (BMW, Mercedes-Benz, Volkswagen) are places BYD cannot enter near term.The path is right, but it narrows CATL's market from all EVs to mid-to-high-end plus overseas.

If only one moat remains, which one is it?

Suppose the technology route shifts in five years and the brand never gets built—what can CATL still live on? There is only one answer: cost. Not the cost of being "5% cheaper than others," but the cost of "lowest unit investment at the same yield"—which comes from the engineering capability of the production line, the in-house share of equipment, and the self-supply share of materials.

This moat is invisible: it is not in patents, not in the brand, but in investment per GWh and manufacturing cost per kWh. For a rival to replicate it, it is not a matter of buying one piece of equipment but of rebuilding an entire engineering system—five years and RMB 50 billion.

But a cost moat has an inherent flaw: it only guarantees "earning more than rivals," not "earning more than last year." When an industry price war runs its course, even the lowest-cost player must cut prices. That is why CATL's gross margin still fell 1.07 points in 2026—it beat its rivals but lost to the industry.

Conclusion

Moat verdict: a cost-based moat, deep but not wide. Cost scores 9 and scale 8, and these two alone ensure it out-earns peers. Switching cost scores 7, brand 6 and network 5; none of the three stops customers from adding a second supplier. The most dangerous rival is BYD (redefining the market); the most immediate damage comes from CALB and Sunwoda (taking orders). Investment implication: the moat is enough to thrive on, not enough to guarantee margins hold.
