## First-round screen: gross margin below 15% is an automatic out

Batteries are a heavy-asset manufacturing business. A gross margin below 15% means a net margin of less than 5% after expenses, and a single round of price war can push the business into losses. On that line, LG Energy Solution (10%–15%), Gotion (14%–16%) and CALB (around 15%) all sit at the edge or below it. **They are not bad companies; they are companies without a safety buffer.**

CATL at 23.93% and EVE at 15%–18% clear the line. BYD does not disclose gross margin for its battery segment separately, but because the output is used in-house and carries no selling expenses, its actual cost is the lowest — **it is not that its gross margin is high, but that it does not need a gross margin.**

## Second-round screen: is there an overseas premium?

This is the most critical screen for 2026. The domestic price war has squeezed power-battery gross margins to around 20%, and the only remaining premium is overseas (CATL at 29.97%). Whoever has a high overseas revenue share and can deliver locally will own the next three years of profit.

CATL's overseas revenue is RMB 87.13 billion, 31.5% of the total, with three bases under construction in Hungary, Spain and Indonesia; LG Energy Solution has North American capacity but gross margin of only 10%–15%, showing its "localization" has not earned a premium; CALB, Gotion and EVE have essentially no overseas capacity.**After this round of screening, only CATL is left.**

## Final pick: Why CATL, not BYD

If the question is simply "who is the best battery company," BYD and CATL can be placed side by side—BYD's Blade Battery costs less, and its vertical integration is more efficient. But investing is not about picking the best company; it is about picking the best security.

More than 90% of BYD's batteries are used in-house, so buying its stock means buying the whole vehicle business (and also bearing the vehicle price war, channel inventory, and export tariffs). **CATL is the only pure battery play, and it holds the global number-one share.** That is the reason for the final choice.

EVE Energy deserves separate attention: it is a pure energy-storage play, with a higher energy-storage revenue share and faster growth, but its scale is only one-tenth of CATL's, and its ability to withstand a price war is weak. **If you want energy-storage elasticity, it is the alternative; if you want certainty of share, only CATL fits.**

## Why buy now: the funnel screens for the target, the price determines the timing

The funnel answers "who to buy," not "when to buy." CATL now trades at 14x 2026 expected earnings and 11.6x 2027, at the low end of its five-year valuation range; the stock has also hit a 52-week low, down 36.3% from its peak. **The right target plus a low price is the buy point.**

Conversely, if CATL traded at 30x, the funnel's conclusion would become "good company, wait for a pullback." **The funnel picks the beauty; valuation picks the timing.**

## The three eliminated players each had their own opportunities

Being filtered out of the funnel does not mean a company is poor. LG Energy Solution has local capacity in North America; if the US market shuts out Chinese batteries entirely, it is the sole beneficiary. CALB, relying on low prices plus rapid response, has already established a firm position in RMB 100,000–150,000 models. Gotion is tied to Huawei's "Jujing battery" and holds stable orders within the Harmony Intelligent Mobility system. **Their problem is not a lack of opportunity, but that the opportunity is not large enough to support a "first-tier company" valuation.**

The practical implication for investors: if you only want to capture the money from "battery industry growth," buying CATL is the most efficient route (largest share, lowest cost, strongest overseas presence); if you want to capture the money from "share shift," CALB and Sunwoda offer greater elasticity but carry a notch more risk. **The two logics cannot be mixed.**

## Beyond the funnel: why not wait for a "better price"

The funnel only selects "who to buy"; price determines "whether it is worth buying now." Some will say they will wait for it to fall to RMB 200 before buying—that is the price under the pessimistic scenario, corresponding to "share falling to 33% + full burden of the consumption tax." **If it really falls to RMB 200, the fundamentals are most likely already broken, rather than the price simply being cheaper.**

This is the relationship between the funnel and timing: **use the funnel to rule out "should not buy," use valuation to decide "how much to buy," and do not use price forecasts to decide "whether to buy."** Waiting for a lower price often brings worse financial statements.
