Core view

We run the six major global power-battery players through a screen: CATL, BYD, LG Energy Solution, CALB, EVE Energy and Gotion High-tech. The three coarse-screen criteria are: can it make money on its own (gross margin), does it command an overseas premium, and is it big enough. Only CATL and BYD pass at least two of the three.

CATL wins the final cut over BYD for one reason: BYD does not sell its batteries to third parties, so buying BYD means buying the whole vehicle business; CATL is the only pure battery play. Its overseas gross margin of 29.97% is also the only premium left in the field.

First-round screen: gross margin below 15% is an automatic out

Batteries are a heavy-asset manufacturing business. A gross margin below 15% leaves a net margin of less than 5% after expenses, and a single round of price war can tip the business into losses. LG Energy Solution (10%–15%), Gotion (14%–16%) and CALB (around 15%) all sit at that line or below it. These are not bad companies; they are companies without a safety buffer.

CATL at 23.93% and EVE at 15%–18% clear it. BYD does not disclose gross margin for its battery segment separately, but because output is used in-house and carries no selling expenses, its actual cost is the lowest — not because its gross margin is high, but because it does not need one.

Second-round screen: is there an overseas premium?

This is the most critical screen for 2026. The domestic price war has squeezed power-battery gross margins to around 20%, leaving overseas as the only remaining premium (CATL at 29.97%). Whoever combines a high overseas revenue share with local delivery capability will own the next three years of profit.

CATL's overseas revenue is RMB 87.13 billion, 31.5% of the total, with three bases under construction in Hungary, Spain and Indonesia. LG Energy Solution has North American capacity but gross margin of only 10%–15%, showing its "localization" has not earned a premium; CALB, Gotion and EVE have essentially no overseas capacity.After this round of screening, only CATL is left.

Final pick: Why CATL, not BYD

If the question is simply "who makes the best battery," BYD and CATL sit side by side—BYD's Blade Battery costs less and its vertical integration is more efficient. But investing is not about picking the best company; it is about picking the best security.

More than 90% of BYD's batteries go in-house, so buying its stock means buying the entire vehicle business—and with it the vehicle price war, channel inventory and export tariffs. CATL is the only pure battery play, and it holds the global number-one share. That is what drives the final choice.

EVE Energy warrants separate attention: a pure energy-storage play with a higher energy-storage revenue share and faster growth, but at one-tenth of CATL's scale its ability to withstand a price war is weak. For energy-storage elasticity, it is the alternative; for certainty of share, only CATL fits.

Why buy now: the funnel screens for the target; the price determines the timing

The funnel answers "who to buy," not "when to buy." CATL trades at 14x 2026E earnings and 11.6x 2027E, the low end of its five-year valuation range, and the stock has hit a 52-week low, down 36.3% from its peak. The right target plus a low price is the buy point.

Conversely, at 30x the funnel's conclusion would become "good company, wait for a pullback." The funnel picks the beauty; valuation picks the timing.

The three eliminated players each had opportunities of their own

Falling out of the funnel does not make a company poor. LG Energy Solution has local capacity in North America and would be the sole beneficiary if the US market shut out Chinese batteries entirely. CALB has already established a firm position in RMB 100,000–150,000 models on low prices plus rapid response. Gotion is tied to Huawei's "Jujing battery" and holds stable orders within the Harmony Intelligent Mobility system. Their problem is not a lack of opportunity, but that the opportunity is not large enough to support a "first-tier company" valuation.

The practical implication for investors: to capture the money from "battery industry growth," buying CATL is the most efficient route (largest share, lowest cost, strongest overseas presence); to capture the money from "share shift," CALB and Sunwoda offer greater elasticity but carry a notch more risk. The two logics cannot be mixed.

Beyond the funnel: why not wait for a "better price"

The funnel only decides who to buy; price decides whether it is worth buying now. Some investors say they will wait for a pullback to RMB 200—the pessimistic-scenario price, implying share falling to 33% plus the full consumption tax burden. If it really falls to RMB 200, the fundamentals are most likely already broken, rather than the stock simply being cheaper.

This is how the funnel and timing fit together: use the funnel to rule out "should not buy," use valuation to decide "how much to buy," and do not use price forecasts to decide "whether to buy." Waiting for a lower price often brings worse financial statements.

Conclusion

Funnel conclusion: six in, one out — CATL is the only name that clears a gross margin above 15%, carries an overseas premium, ranks first by scale and can be bought as a pure play. BYD drops out not on weakness but because owning it does not give pure battery exposure; LG and the second-tier players drop out for lack of a margin of safety. After the final cut only one question remains — is 14x low enough. The answer sits in Valuation and the Six Gates.
