## Position in the value chain: confined to the "design and packaging" layer

Zhongji Innolight's position in the chain, in one sentence: it buys chips, designs, controls packaging and sells modules. It makes neither optical chips (EML, CW lasers) nor electrical chips (DSP), which together account for more than 50% of material cost. What it does is optical path design, optical coupling, packaging processes and delivery management—precisely the two metrics customers care about most: yield and lead time.

The advantage of this position is exceptional capital efficiency: with no investment in wafer fabs, total assets of RMB 68.94 billion support half-year revenue of RMB 41.78 billion and a half-year weighted ROE of 37.62%. The downside is bargaining power squeezed at both ends: the largest upstream supplier accounts for 38.3% of procurement, while the top five downstream customers account for 81.9% of revenue—concentration at both ends exceeds its own, and the only buffer in between is a 46% gross margin.

## The truth about bargaining power: profiting from the "irreplaceable three months"

Module makers have no long-term pricing power over customers, only "window pricing power." When 1.6T first ramped, only two or three players worldwide could deliver reliably, and customers accepted an average price of RMB 2,178; once Eoptolink's 1.6T ramp completes, the price will be pulled back to around RMB 1,900. That is why the +36.6% ASP gain is both meaningful and time-limited.

The same holds upstream: RMB 950 million of prepayments and RMB 5.06 billion of long-term payables to lock in more than a year of materials confirm that optical chips and electrical chips remain a seller's market. That leaves the company two levers: lock in volume early and raise the silicon photonics mix (reducing reliance on EML). It is pulling both, but neither changes its "middleman" nature.

## Moat score: two hard, one medium, three soft

Two moats are hard: technology lead and scale. Technology lead is worth 2–3 quarters, or roughly 6–8 percentage points of gross margin—of the 46.59% in 2026H1, half is estimated to come from this time gap. Scale buys procurement bargaining power and a yield learning curve; shipments of 18.99 million units make chip procurement cheaper than for second-tier players and speed up yield ramp on new products.

Switching cost is a moderate moat. A cloud provider changing suppliers needs 12–18 months of re-certification, and that time cost is real; but cloud providers are institutionally required to dual-source, so any single vendor's share has a ceiling. The three soft moats—network effects, brand and licences—should not be counted on in a B2B hardware industry in the first place.

A combined 6.5/10 means: this moat is enough to capture the 1.6T cycle's gains, not enough to guarantee it is still the one at 3.2T. It must be re-dug every year, rather than dug deeper.

## The most dangerous rival: Eoptolink, then CPO

Eoptolink is the only rival going head-to-head for the same customers on the same product generation. 2026H1 revenue was RMB 20.91 billion (+100.3%), net profit RMB 7.53 billion (+90.98%), gross margin 48.44% and net cash ratio 21.5% — gross margin 2 points above Innolight, with better cash flow. Its playbook is cost control plus fast follow; once 1.6T reaches volume, its first move will be on price.

The second threat comes from upstream players Coherent and Lumentum: they sell both chips and modules, and the stronger the industry cycle, the more profit they capture. Every module Innolight sells requires chips bought from them, a structure that cannot be changed.

The third threat is CPO. NVIDIA's Spectrum-X CPO switches were announced for full mass production in March 2026, entering production ramp in August, with second-generation 3.2T optical engines and 16 200G channels per engine. Near term it replaces pluggable modules on switch faceplates; longer term it could erode a slice of the short-reach market. This is not tomorrow's threat, but it determines the P/E multiple the stock gets after 2028.
