## Value distribution: where in the curve the money is made

A 1.6T optical module breaks down into four cost blocks: optoelectronic chips above 50%, packaging and testing around 20%, PCB and structural parts 10%–15%, and labour and manufacturing overhead 10%. Gross margin distribution runs exactly the other way: upstream chips 40%–60%, midstream modules 25%–48%, and downstream cloud providers amortise through scale.

Four 2026H1 figures make this structure plain: Tianfu Communication (upstream passive components) at 60.87% gross margin, Zhongji Innolight at 46.4%, Eoptolink at 48.44%, and Accelink at 25.53%. The closer to the chip, the higher the margin; the closer to system assembly, the thinner the margin. Zhongji Innolight already sits at the top of the module makers, but it remains in the "module maker" box.

Downstream, Nvidia and four cloud providers spent a combined USD 164.9 billion in capex in 2026Q2, up 86% YoY. They pay, so they set the rules—dual sourcing, 3%–5% annual price cuts, volume locked by project. Module makers earn the rent from "no one can replace this generation of product yet."

## Zhongji Innolight: a technology-intensive assembly operation

It does three things: optical path design (silicon photonics solutions, optical coupling), packaging (where yield is profit) and delivery management (18.99 million units shipped in the half-year, capacity across five sites). It does not do two things: it makes neither optical chips nor electrical chips. That division of labour underpins a high return on capital — no wafer fab investment, ROE 37.62% — but caps its bargaining power, since it can only partially pass through upstream price increases.

Its relative advantage is system integration: combining third-party chips, its own silicon photonics solutions and its own packaging into a deliverable module, and earning a 46% gross margin — no small feat in an industry where Eoptolink trails by just 2 points. But note one number: in 2026Q1, purchases from its largest supplier accounted for 38.3% of total procurement. Supplier concentration is close to customer concentration, with both ends in the 40%–80% range, leaving a thin buffer in between.

So the positioning must be precise: it is the most technology-intensive assembly link in the chain, not the technology owner. In-house silicon photonics is a real asset, but the barrier to silicon photonics is falling; the 1.6T first-mover advantage is real, but first-mover advantage is worth only 2–3 quarters.

## Downstream customer concentration: top five customers account for 94.8%, overseas

This is the most easily overlooked structural risk in the industry. Zhongji Innolight's top five customers account for 81.9%, with the largest customer at 25.1% (2026Q1 basis), up from 75.98% in 2025 and still rising; overseas revenue is RMB 39.615 billion, or 94.8% of the total, with the US market accounting for more than 60%.

Why is concentration rising? Because demand is concentrated in the largest AI clusters: whoever buys more GPUs buys more optical modules. Rising industry concentration is determined by the demand structure, not by the company's sales strategy. The market widely believes the largest customer is Nvidia and the second largest is Google—if that judgment holds, Zhongji's orders are essentially "a function of Nvidia's shipments."

The two sides of concentration must be viewed together. The good side: large customers place large orders, qualification cycles are long (12–18 months), and switching costs are high, so once you are in, the relationship is relatively stable. The bad side: pricing power rests entirely with the counterparty, payment terms are set by them (accounts receivable RMB 15.001 billion, equivalent to 65 days of revenue), and inventory risk is borne by the company itself (inventory RMB 19.826 billion). This is the origin of "funding operations with working capital."

## The cyclicality of optical modules: three downturns, one pattern

Optical modules are not a steady-state industry: three cycles overlap—cloud capex (3–4 years), technology generations (roughly one every 2 years) and inventory (2–4 quarters). When they align, earnings surge; when they diverge, de-rating and earnings misses arrive together.

History offers two precedents. In 2019, in the gap between 100G and 400G, the company's net profit fell about 18% YoY—not because demand vanished, but because the prior generation was cut in price while the next had yet to ramp. From 2H22 through 2023, North American cloud providers cut orders and destocked, the whole sector de-rated, and Zhongji's gross margin fell to 32.99% in 2023.

Gross margin best illustrates the cyclicality: 32.99% in 2023 → 46.4% in 2026H1, 13pp over three years. Net margin moved from 20.59% to 32.68% over the same period, a 12pp swing—at the cycle trough, the same revenue generates only 60% of the profit.

Where we are now: pricing gains persist (ASP +36.6%), capacity expansion has begun (construction in progress +160.7%, with the whole industry expanding in sync), and upstream is tightest (long lead times, materials locked up). All three are standard features of the mid-to-late cycle—not the top, but not far from it; not a buying point, but a watching point.
