Core view

Four moats, each of differing durability. Technology lead: worth 2–3 quarters, about 6–8 percentage points of gross margin; scale: worth 3–5 points of cost advantage; switching costs: worth a 12–18 month qualification period; in-house silicon photonics: worth one cost-reduction pathway.Together they score 6.5/10 — enough to win this generation, not enough to guarantee the next. By comparison, TSMC's moat "deepens the more it is dug," while Zhongji's "must be re-dug every year."

Rule one: technology lead worth 2–3 quarters

1.6T silicon photonic modules ramped to volume 2–3 quarters ahead of competitors. That time gap shows up in the numbers: in 2026H1 the average selling price was RMB 2,178 at a 46.59% gross margin, versus RMB 1,595 and 39.96% in 2025H1. More than half of the 6.63pp of margin expansion came from first-mover advantage.

Why does being first convert into pricing power? Because customers' next-generation switches have fixed launch dates — whoever delivers first wins the launch order, and launch orders command the best pricing.But that advantage has a clear expiry: Eoptolink's 1.6T is already in volume ramp and Coherent is pushing hard too. The window runs to roughly the first half of 2027.

Second point: scale, worth 3–5 points of cost

Shipments of 18.99 million units in the half-year and 21.09 million units in 2025 deliver two things: procurement bargaining power (chipmakers will offer their largest customer better pricing and priority capacity) and a yield learning curve (on the same process, making 10 million units versus one million units leaves a gap of several points in yield).

Scale advantage is more reliably defended than technological first-mover status because it does not vanish overnight, but it has a ceiling: once a competitor's shipments reach 60%–70% of yours, the procurement bargaining gap is largely erased. Eoptolink's 2026H1 revenue was RMB 20.91 billion, already half of Innolight's, and the scale gap is narrowing.

Third, switching costs, worth 12–18 months; fourth, silicon photonics, which offers a cost-reduction path

Switching optical module suppliers forces a cloud provider to re-run compatibility, reliability and mass-production consistency validation—a 12–18 month cycle. That is real stickiness, but cloud providers are institutionally required to dual-source (sometimes triple-source), which caps it: you can win at most 50%–70% share, never 100%.

In-house silicon photonics is the most "technical" of the four. It reduces reliance on EML optical chips (easing upstream bottlenecks), raises integration (cutting packaging cost) and enables higher-speed modules (3.2T NPO and 6.4T NPO already developed). But the silicon photonics barrier is falling—TSMC's silicon photonics foundry capacity is opening up, and rivals can use it too.

The conclusion: this is not a moat you keep by standing still; it is a moat you keep only by running fast. Its value equals "ran fast over the past three years" multiplied by the probability of "can still run fast over the next three years"—I put that probability at 60%.

Conclusion

Conclusion: moat 6.5/10 — a "speed" moat, not a "structural" one. The moat rests on a 2–3 quarter technology lead, 3–5 points of scale, 12–18 months of switching costs and silicon photonics as one route to lower cost. All four depreciate with each iteration, so the valuation cannot command a "perpetual moat" multiple (above 30x) — only a "current-period rent" multiple (20–25x).
