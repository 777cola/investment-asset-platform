Core view

This is a "founder-controlled + professional-manager-operated" company, with a cleaner governance structure than most A-share manufacturing firms. Wang Weixiu is the actual controller; in August 2023 he handed the chairmanship to Liu Sheng, founder of Suzhou InnoLight, whose 2025 compensation was RMB 4.1834 million and who holds 3.652 million shares. The main thread of capital allocation is "financing and capacity expansion first": a private placement in 2021, HKD 53.4 billion raised via H shares in July 2026, and share capital up 47% over five years; dividends and buybacks are catching up (RMB 12 per 10 shares for the 2026 interim, and about RMB 5 billion of buybacks in August–September), but their pace consistently ranks behind expansion.

Key people and division of roles: who runs the business, who controls the company

The company's governance structure deserves a separate mention: control sits with the Wang Weixiu family of Longkou, Shandong; operations are run by the returnee technical team at Suzhou InnoLight. The 2017 "snake swallows elephant" deal worth RMB 2.8 billion stitched together a motor winding equipment company and a Silicon Valley-backed optical module company, and the market was not optimistic at the time. Nine years on, the acquired side's man is chairman and president, while the acquirer's people have retreated to the controlling shareholder position—a succession that is unusual among A-share manufacturers.

Liu Sheng's path is clear: Lucent R&D engineer → head of Pine Photonics' China R&D center → senior manager at Opnext → founded Suzhou InnoLight in 2008 → appointed president of the listed company in 2017 → took over as chairman in August 2023.A technical background, front-line R&D experience and more than two decades in optical communications—this is the organizational reason the company got both the 800G and 1.6T windows right.

The pay structure also tells the story: Liu Sheng received RMB 4.1834 million in 2025, the only person in the table above RMB 4 million, up 14.71% year on year; Executive Vice President Wang Xiaodong received RMB 1.0070 million.The top executive's pay is four times the second-in-command's—enough to bind him, but against a market cap of RMB 1.09 trillion, the incentive intensity is hardly aggressive.

Capital allocation: raise funds to expand capacity first, then discuss returns

Line up nine years of moves and the logic is remarkably consistent: every industry upcycle, raise capital first, then expand capacity, with dividends last. In November 2021, a private placement of 87.08 million shares funded capacity for 800G; in July 2026, an H-share raise of HKD 53.4 billion funded capacity and materials for 1.6T and 3.2T. Both came before demand took off, and both enlarged the share count.

The cost is visible: total shares rose from 800 million in 2021 to 1.178 billion in September 2026, up 47% in five years. Adjust for that 47% dilution and EPS growth over the past five years runs a third below net profit growth. Net profit was RMB 10.797 billion in 2025, up 108.78% year on year, but per-share growth must be discounted — one technical reason this is "a good company, not necessarily a good stock."

Returns are improving, but only modestly. The 2026 interim dividend of RMB 12 per 10 shares, about RMB 1.4 billion, is triple the RMB 4 per 10 shares paid a year earlier; buybacks in August–September totaled about RMB 5 billion. Together that is roughly RMB 6.4 billion, about 47% of half-year net profit of RMB 13.651 billion — better than before, but far from companies that return all free cash flow to shareholders, because the money is needed for capacity expansion.

Integrity and governance: no major flaws, but two things to keep in mind

Before the report date, no hard flaws such as CSRC investigations, public exchange censure, or financial restatements surfaced. Audit opinions, goodwill (RMB 1.939 billion, 2.8% of total assets), and related-party transactions all showed no problems. For a company whose revenue grew 4x in five years, this counts as clean.

Two things to note. First, controlling shareholder ownership is declining. Zhongji Holdings and its concert parties (Wang Weixiu, Wang Xiaodong) announced on July 30, 2026 that, due to the H-share issuance, equity incentive exercises and secondary-market sales, their combined stake changed by more than 1%. Passive dilution is understandable; active selling near a HKD 1 trillion market cap is not a good signal.

Second, Wang Weixiu has pledged 1.35 million shares. That is only 1.9% of his 69.73 million shares, so the risk is negligible, but it indicates funding arrangements at the family level. Overall judgment: governance scores 7, with deductions not for integrity but for a "financing-first" capital allocation preference.

Succession: one handover done, no sign of the next

The company has already completed one critical succession: after the 2017 acquisition, the actual controller handed operating control to the acquired company's founder, and the handover held—over nine years Liu Sheng rose from president to chairman, and the 1.6T bet was made by his team. That is healthier than the "founder retires but never really leaves" pattern common in many family businesses.

The next question: Liu Sheng was born in 1971 and turns 55 in 2026; another decade at the helm is not a problem. But who comes after him? The company has disclosed no succession plan, and no obvious "number-two technical figure" has been pushed to the fore within management. For a company that survives on a technology bet every 18 months, this is a long-term concern, not a current risk.

Conclusion

Conclusion: management scores 7 — trustworthy, but do not expect it to save money for shareholders. Liu Sheng's team has proven its technical judgement through two windows, 800G and 1.6T; the governance structure is clean, and dividends and buybacks are improving. The deduction is for capital-allocation preference — share count up 47% over five years and a further 5.6% dilution from the H-share offering; expansion always takes priority over returns. That means buying it requires accepting that "part of profit growth is eaten by share dilution", and only on that premise does the 53x PE look less absurd.
