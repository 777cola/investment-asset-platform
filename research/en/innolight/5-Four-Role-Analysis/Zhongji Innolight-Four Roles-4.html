Core view

Seven verifiable risks suffice; thirty are unnecessary. Customer concentration at 81.9%, CPO already in mass production, price war, a single upstream supplier accounting for 38.3% of purchases, 94.8% of revenue from overseas, RMB 19.8 billion of inventory, share count up 47% over five years. Li Lu's framework asks first whether the company survives the worst case, then what it is worth if it does. This company survived both the 2019 and 2022 downturns and gained share, so survivability is not the issue — valuation is.

The first thing to guard against is not competition, but customers

Five customers account for 81.9% of revenue, the largest at 25.1% — widely assumed to be Nvidia. Overseas revenue was RMB 39.615 billion, or 94.8%, with the US accounting for over 60%. That leaves no room to "diversify risk": a single export control, customer production schedule adjustment or tariff list can rewrite the financials outright.

Li Lu would ask the harsher question: why do these customers need you? Because they need a second supplier, they need capacity, and they need someone to carry inventory risk on their behalf. All three hold — but none amounts to "we cannot do without you." That is a structural weakness, not a management failing, and it cannot be fixed.

CPO: not a 2026 story, but the valuation ceiling

At GTC in March 2026, Nvidia announced full mass production of its Spectrum-X CPO switches (SN6800/SN6810), with volume ramp in August, second-generation 3.2T optical engines and 16 x 200G lanes per engine. This is real progress, not a PowerPoint.

The impact path, however, needs to be clear: CPO replaces "pluggable modules on the switch faceplate," not all optical modules. Long-distance interconnect between racks and between clusters still uses pluggables. The near-term (2026–2027) impact is limited, because CPO yields, serviceability and ecosystem are not yet mature; longer term (after 2028) it will erode a slice of share, especially the short-reach market on the switch side.

Implication for valuation: The market's 53x P/E (TTM) implicitly assumes "pluggable modules can keep monetizing each speed upgrade." If CPO takes 20% of new interconnect ports in 2028, that implicit assumption gets a 30% haircut. This is not a reason to sell today; it is a reason not to give it 60x P/E.

Worst case: it survives, but is not worth the price

Li Lu's habit is to work out liquidation value first and profitability second. Total assets of RMB 68.94 billion, net assets attributable to shareholders of RMB 39.86 billion, goodwill of RMB 1.94 billion, and roughly RMB 55 billion of cash on the books after H-share financing (rough estimate). Even if demand reverses in 2027, the company will not become insolvent—it carries net cash and no significant interest-bearing debt.

But "surviving" and "being worth RMB 1.09 trillion" are two different things. The bear case (2027 net profit of RMB 24 billion, 20x) implies RMB 407 per share and a market cap of RMB 480 billion, 56% below the current level. That scenario does not require the company to make a mistake; it only requires cloud capex growth to fall from 86% back to 20%.

History offers two precedents: during the 100G-to-400G transition gap in 2019, net profit fell about 18% YoY; in the second half of 2022, cloud providers cut orders and the sector de-rated as a whole. It survived both times, and its share rose both times. The right stance on this company is therefore "buy at the cycle bottom, watch at the cycle top," not "never touch it."

Conclusion

Risk conclusion: low survival risk, high valuation risk. Of the seven triggers, two matter most: whether customer concentration rises above 85%, and whether the 1.6T per-unit average price begins to decline QoQ. The first determines revenue stability; the second caps gross margin. If either deteriorates, the current 53x P/E will not hold.
