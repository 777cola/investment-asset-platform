Core view

Risks need not run to thirty items; seven verifiable ones suffice. Customer concentration at 81.9%, CPO already in mass production, price war, a single upstream supplier accounting for 38.3% of purchases, 94.8% of revenue from overseas, RMB 19.8 billion of inventory, share count up 47% over five years. Li Lu's framework asks first whether the company survives the worst case, then what it is worth if it does. This company survived both the 2019 and 2022 downturns and gained share; survivability is not the issue — valuation is.

The first thing to guard against is not competition, but customers

Five customers account for 81.9% of revenue, the largest at 25.1% — the market widely assumes that is Nvidia. Overseas revenue was RMB 39.615 billion, or 94.8%, with the US market at over 60%. That leaves the company no room to "diversify risk": one export control, one customer production schedule adjustment, one tariff list can directly rewrite the financials.

Li Lu would ask a harsher question: why do these customers need you? The answer is they need a second supplier, they need capacity, they need someone to bear the inventory risk on their behalf. All three reasons hold, but none of them is "we cannot do without you." This is a structural weakness, not a management problem, and it cannot be fixed.

CPO: not a question of tomorrow, but of the valuation ceiling

In March 2026 at GTC, Nvidia announced full mass production of its Spectrum-X CPO switches (SN6800/SN6810), entering volume ramp in August, with second-generation 3.2T optical engines and 16 x 200G lanes per engine. This is real progress, not a PowerPoint.

But the impact path needs to be clear: CPO replaces "pluggable modules on the switch faceplate," not all optical modules. Long-distance interconnect between racks and between clusters still uses pluggables. The near-term (2026–2027) impact is limited, because CPO yields, serviceability and ecosystem are not yet mature; longer term (after 2028) it will erode a slice of share, especially the short-reach market on the switch side.

Implication for valuation: The market's 53x TTM PE implicitly assumes "pluggable modules can keep monetizing each speed upgrade." If CPO takes 20% of new interconnect ports in 2028, that implicit assumption gets a 30% haircut. This is not a reason to sell today; it is a reason not to give it 60x PE.

Worst case: it survives, but is not worth the price

Li Lu's habit is to calculate liquidation value first, then profitability. Total assets of RMB 68.94 billion, net assets attributable to parent of RMB 39.86 billion, goodwill of RMB 1.94 billion, and roughly RMB 55 billion in cash on the books after H-share financing (rough estimate). Even if demand reverses in 2027, it will not become insolvent—this is a company with net cash and no significant interest-bearing debt.

But "surviving" and "being worth RMB 1.09 trillion" are two different things. The bear case (2027 net profit of RMB 24 billion, 20x) implies RMB 407 per share and a market cap of RMB 480 billion, 56% below the current level. This scenario does not require the company to make a mistake; it only requires cloud capex growth to fall from 86% back to 20%.

History offers two precedents: during the 100G-to-400G transition gap in 2019, net profit fell about 18% year on year; in the second half of 2022, cloud providers cut orders and the sector de-rated as a whole. It survived both times, and its share rose both times. The right stance on this company is therefore "buy at the cycle bottom, watch at the cycle top," not "never touch it."

Conclusion

Risk conclusion: low survival risk, high valuation risk. Of the seven triggers, two matter most — whether customer concentration rises above 85%, and whether the 1.6T per-unit average price starts to decline quarter on quarter. The first determines revenue stability, the second caps gross margin. If either deteriorates, the current 53x PE will not hold.
