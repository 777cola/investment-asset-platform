Optical modules in 2026: demand is certain, the competitive landscape is deteriorating

Core view

The industry has never looked better; the competitive structure never worse. We size the global 800G+1.6T optical module market at roughly USD 14.6 billion in 2026, 64% of the total, with 800G shipments of 33.5 million units and 1.6T demand of 8.6–20 million units. Yet four Chinese vendors are adding capacity and customers simultaneously — the classic "certain demand, uncontrolled supply" combination. As Munger would put it, industry growth does not equal shareholder returns, especially when everyone can see the growth.

Demand: firm, and accelerating

Demand in 2026 is not in dispute. The four major North American cloud providers spent a combined USD 164.9 billion on capex in 2026Q2, up 86% YoY; 800G became the main shipment volume and 1.6T began ramping at scale. LightCounting estimates 800G and 1.6T combined will total about USD 14.6 billion in 2026, 64% of the overall optical module market, with 800G shipments of 33.5 million units and 1.6T demand of 8.6 million to 20 million units.

The scale of these numbers needs to be read correctly: the 1.6T range runs from 8.6 million to 20 million units, a 2.3x gap between the low and high ends. The industry research firms themselves cannot pin it down, which shows this market still runs on orders, not forecasts. For investors, that means any precise forecast built on 1.6T shipment volumes deserves a discount.

All four are expanding capacity simultaneously, and none will yield

Munger's inversion applies neatly here: if this were such a good business, why are entrants all expanding capacity rather than shrinking? Because nothing stops capacity expansion—capital can be raised (Zhongji has just raised HKD 53.4 billion), equipment can be bought, and customers want a second supplier.

The 2026H1 comparison is subtle: Eoptolink's 48.44% gross margin beats Zhongji Innolight's 46.4%, and its 36.0% net margin is higher too. Zhongji's premium therefore rests not on proprietary technology but on scale and first-mover advantage—for the same product generation, rivals' pricing power is far from weak. Upstream component maker TFC Optical posts a 60.87% gross margin, a notch above module makers—the value gap of chain position.

By share, Zhongji remains No. 1 (about 23% in 2025, with shipments of 21.09 million units) and holds about 50% of 800G silicon photonics. But "No. 1" in this industry is not a talisman—it is a target. In 2025 Eoptolink already overtook Coherent to become global No. 2, closing the gap far faster than in the previous cycle.

Cycle position: mid-to-late stage, not the starting point

Three signals together tell us where we are in the cycle.① Prices are still rising (blended ASP RMB 2,178, +36.6%)—a hallmark of a peak.② The whole industry is expanding capacity (Zhongji's construction in progress RMB 3.707 billion, +160.7%; Eoptolink and TFC were expanding over the same period)—a signal that supply is catching up.③ Upstream lead times are the tightest (optical and electrical chips account for over 50% of material cost, and electrical chips above 200G rely mainly on overseas suppliers)—a hallmark of the bottleneck phase.

The industry's two prior downturns followed a clear pattern: during the 2019 gap between 100G and 400G, the company's net profit fell about 18% YoY; in the second half of 2022, cloud providers cut orders and the entire sector de-rated. The common thread—the downturn came not from demand disappearing, but from the prior generation cutting prices while the next generation had yet to ramp.

Where we stand now: 1.6T is ramping, 3.2T has yet to be sampled (company commentary in April: "capacity being prepared, not yet at the sampling stage"), and CPO switches were announced in March 2026 as fully in mass production.In other words, this generation's payoff is still here, the next generation's bet is still unresolved, and substitute technology is already at the door.That is the standard setup for the mid-to-late stage of a cycle.

Conclusion

Sector view: real demand, poor structure, mid-to-late stage. Industry growth can deliver revenue but not valuation—everyone knows where the growth is, and capacity will flood there. The Munger-style judgment: in an industry with no barriers to entry, where customers actively cultivate a second supplier, the leader's excess profit is rent, not a moat.
