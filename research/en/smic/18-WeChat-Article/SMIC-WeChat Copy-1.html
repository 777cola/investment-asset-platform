Consider a set of incongruous numbers first.

In the first quarter of 2026, SMIC delivered a "strange" set of financial results.

Revenue was USD 2.506 billion, up 11.5% year on year and a record high; capacity utilization was 93.1%, 9.5 points higher than a year earlier and close to full capacity; guidance for the second quarter is even stronger — revenue up another 14% to 16% quarter on quarter.

But net profit was only USD 197 million, up 5% year on year. On an A-share basis, net profit was RMB 1.361 billion, up 0.36% year on year — essentially zero growth.

Revenue up 11.5%, profit up 5%. Where did the 6.5-point gap go?

The answer is one word: depreciation.

1. For every RMB 100 collected, RMB 43 goes back to equipment first

First-quarter depreciation and amortization was USD 1.088 billion, up 25.7% year on year and equal to 43.4% of revenue. In other words, for every 100 the company collects, 43 goes straight back to the machines.

The full year is harsher. On its February earnings call, the company itself said 2026 depreciation would rise about 30% year on year to roughly USD 5 billion. And what was full-year 2025 net profit? USD 685 million. Depreciation is seven times net profit.

This is not an operating problem; it is the structure of the business: a 12-inch line costs over USD 10 billion, depreciates over five years, and takes three years to ramp. Once equipment enters the fab, depreciation starts ticking monthly regardless of whether orders exist.

SMIC's income statement is therefore essentially a function of depreciation. One hundred million more in depreciation cuts profit by 30%; one hundred million less raises it by 30%. How much it earns depends first on how accounting allocates the cost, and only second on how many wafers it sells.

II. Who is making money: customers, equipment makers, local governments

The money a fab earns must be split four ways: customers want low prices, equipment makers want high prices, local governments want output value, and shareholders want returns.

Among these four parties at SMIC, shareholders rank last.

Three facts: First, no dividend for 2025, on the grounds that "2026 will still require large-scale capital expenditure"; second, no buybacks ever — however far the share price falls, there is no company-level buying; third, more share issuance — in July this year it issued RMB 40.6 billion in shares to acquire 49% of SMIC North, expanding share count from 7.96 billion shares to 8.561 billion shares, diluting existing shareholders by 7%.

No dividend, no buyback, and more issuance. Put these three together and translated into shareholder language it is one sentence: your role at this company is to put money in, not to take money out.

This is not management greed; it is determined by the ownership structure. State-owned shareholders including the Big Fund, Datang and Xinxin hold more than 20% combined, and the company's first goal is "to build up mainland China capacity"; shareholder returns are only the second goal.

III. A 205x P/E — where does the premium come from?

At the August 10 close, SMIC's A-shares stood at RMB 126.25, a market cap of RMB 1.08 trillion, corresponding to 205x TTM P/E and 6.3x P/B.

On the same day, its H-shares closed at HKD 66.20, equivalent to RMB 57.2. Same company, same financials, same voting rights per share — the A-share trades 121% above the H-share.

More interesting is the foreign stance. On August 9, UBS upgraded the H-share from Neutral to Buy and raised its target price from HKD 76 to HKD 96.2 — about RMB 83. The foreign target price for the H-share sits 34% below the current A-share price.

How to make sense of 205x? Three methods all fail to reconcile: at 4x P/B, the stock is worth RMB 81; at the 2026 consensus net profit of RMB 6.255 billion and 40x P/E, market cap is RMB 250 billion and the share price less than a quarter of today's; at the H-share price, RMB 57.

Three methods, three answers: 81, 29, 57. The current price is 126.25. The excess buys the "domestic substitution" story, not this year's earnings.

IV. So is it a bad company?

No. It is the only foundry in mainland China capable of delivering at scale, with a global share of 5% and ranked third, overtaking UMC and GlobalFoundries for the first time in the first quarter of this year.

Its customers have no second option: Huawei's Ascend and Kirin, Cambricon's AI chips, Will Semiconductor's image sensors and GigaDevice's memory all have to tape out on its production lines. No one can replace this "must-pass" position within five years.

Nor has it spent recklessly: debt-to-assets ratio of 33%, net debt of USD 6.6 billion and cash on hand of USD 72.8 billion mean it will not die from being unable to repay debt during an industry downturn.

The problem is not the company, but the price. A good company is not the same as a good stock; between them lies "how much you paid".

V. At what price can you buy

Three scenarios: bear case (price hikes peak in 2027, depreciation keeps climbing) at RMB 88; base case (full utilization persists, ASP rises 5% a year) at RMB 120; bull case (domestic substitution accelerates, price hikes beat expectations) at RMB 155.

Weighting them at 30%, 50% and 20%, expected return is -6.9%. In other words, buying at the current price carries a reasonable expectation of a 7% loss, with another 30% of downside below that.

So my approach is: only talk below RMB 100, only cheap below RMB 80. Or wait for two signals — capex falling below USD 5 billion, or the company starting to pay dividends. That would mark a shift from "building capacity" to "returning capital to shareholders," and the valuation framework would be re-rated.

As for whether it rises now, I don't know and I don't guess. On July 9 it rose 13.74% in a single day to RMB 173, then a month later fell back to RMB 126. With a stock like this, even calling the direction right doesn't guarantee a profit, and calling it wrong guarantees a loss.

Conclusion

One last point that may not be welcome: domestic substitution is real, the AI spillover is real, and SMIC's position is real. None of these three things is in doubt.

But investing is not voting for "the company with the most promise"; it is calculating "how much I get back in a few years for the money I put in." A company with ROE of 2.7%, negative free cash flow and no dividend, at 6.3 times book value — I cannot make that math work.

If I cannot make the math work, I do not do it. We can talk again when it falls to a level where the math works for me.
