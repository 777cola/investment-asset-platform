## Q: Is this a good business?

Answer: No. Foundry is the hardest kind of manufacturing business: the customer places an order, you spend on equipment first, and depreciation starts the moment the tools enter the fab, amortised over five years before profit slowly emerges. SMIC's 2026 depreciation is USD 5 billion against 2025 net profit of USD 685 million — depreciation is seven times net profit. This is not a question of "how much it earns" but of "whether what it earns is enough to pay for the equipment."

The standard for a good business is that it earns money without further capital outlay — Moutai and Apple, for example. SMIC is the reverse: no investment means falling behind, investment means no profit. It is more like an airline: full seats when demand is good, but the money earned goes to buying aircraft.

It does have one advantage — indispensability. For mainland Chinese chip design companies pursuing advanced processes, it is the only outlet. Indispensable does not mean profitable; indispensable only means "it will not disappear." The two must be viewed separately.

## Q: Will it still be around in ten years?

A: It will still be here, and in all likelihood larger than it is now. That judgment requires no understanding of semiconductors, only one fact: mainland China imports USD 300 billion of chips a year, and that gap cannot be closed by buying—only by building at home. SMIC is the only company in that "build at home" effort with scale capacity.

In ten years it may have double the capacity and triple the revenue, with gross margin perhaps reaching 25%, or perhaps still 20%. But the "still here" part is barely in doubt—it has state backing, the Big Fund, and the assets of four new fabs.

The problem is that "still here" and "how much is it worth" are two different things. PetroChina was still here ten years ago, and its share price has yet to break even. A company's existence does not mean its shareholders make money.

## Q: Can you understand it?

Answer: I understand the business, not the technology. The business is buying equipment, filling capacity and selling wafers; revenue splits across five application categories, two sizes and three regions, so you can see at a glance who is driving growth. That part I understand.

The technology I do not. Yields on N+2 and N+3, cost per wafer, how many exposures DUV multi-patterning requires, how many generations behind TSMC it sits — the company does not disclose these, and I cannot verify them. So I do not know whether its advanced process is making money or losing money.

What I do not understand does not stop me judging "is this a good business", but it does stop me judging "how much is it worth". I do not value the parts I cannot understand. That is also why I use P/B only, not P/E — at least P/B can be reconciled.

## Q: At what price would you buy?

Answer: I would only engage below RMB 100, and only below RMB 80 would it be genuinely cheap. The stock is now RMB 126.25, 6.3x PB — too expensive.

Do the math: long-term ROE is roughly 3%, and maybe 8% in its best years. For a company with ROE of 3%–8%, fair PB is 1.5–2.5x. Paying 6.3x amounts to assuming ROE doubles to 20% within three years — that is TSMC's level, not SMIC's.

Some will argue that domestic substitution is a ten-year trend and you cannot value it on current ROE. I agree on the trend, but not on the price. However long the trend, you still have to count cash flow. At 205x P/E it would take two hundred years to earn back the market cap — that is not investing, it is a game of pass-the-parcel.

So my approach is simple: don't do what you don't understand, don't buy what is expensive. Wait until it falls to a level I can actually calculate; if it never does, I stay out — no loss either way.

## Q: What do you fear most?

Two things. First, depreciation keeps rising while price increases stop. Gross margin then falls below 17%, profit turns negative YoY, and the stock and earnings sell off together. Depreciation of USD 5 billion in 2026, up 30% YoY, is already happening, just partly offset by price increases.

Second, equipment supply cuts. In April 2026 the US plans to add DUV to the ban. If it takes effect, expansion halts while depreciation continues — the ugliest combination. The company cannot control this, and neither can I predict it.

As for short-term stock moves, I am not afraid. What I fear is "something incomprehensible happening" — that is the real loss.

## What single question would you put to management?

A: I asked, "When will capex fall below USD 5 billion?"

That matters far more than "when will the advanced process node break through." Capex below USD 5 billion would signal the capacity expansion cycle is over and depreciation has peaked; free cash flow could then turn positive, and only on that basis can dividends be considered. On that day the company shifts from a "national project" to "a company that can be valued."

If management says "five more years," I will come back and look at it in five years; if the answer is "next year," I need to redo the valuation now. That answer decides everything; the rest is detail.
