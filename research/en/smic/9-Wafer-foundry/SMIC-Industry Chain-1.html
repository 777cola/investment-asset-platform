Core call: earnings stem from scarcity rent, not technology rent

In the foundry chain, the three most profitable segments are EDA, advanced process and advanced packaging — SMIC holds none of them. It sits in "mature process plus limited advanced process", where gross margin is 20.1%, 47 points below TSMC and 12 points below UMC. Its value lies not in earning much, but in being unavoidable for everyone. Upstream equipment and materials are controlled by others, and downstream customers have no second choice — the most uncomfortable yet most indispensable position in this chain.

Upstream: access to leading-edge equipment remains capped; materials prices are rising

Equipment is the one link in this chain that can choke SMIC. ASML is the only company globally capable of making EUV lithography machines, and SMIC cannot obtain them, so N+2 and N+3 can rely only on DUV multi-patterning—more exposures per wafer, lower yield, higher cost. In April 2026, the US plans to extend its China ban to DUV; if implemented, even mature-node capacity expansion would be affected.

Materials are in relatively better shape, but the trend is also reversing. In 2026, 12-inch silicon wafers rose 15%–25% cumulatively, while 8-inch wafers rose 8%–12% and were raised a further 5%–10% in the second quarter. Silicon wafers account for about 10%–15% of foundry costs, and this round of price increases directly costs 1–2 points of gross margin. Domestic substitution (Shanghai Silicon, Hangzhou Lion, Anji) is advancing, but high-end silicon wafers and photoresist still rely on imports.

Equipment and materials share one trait: SMIC has no bargaining power over them, only the right to choose the timing of purchases. This is one of the structural reasons its gross margin is only 20% versus TSMC's 67.7%—TSMC buys equipment at "major customer prices," while SMIC buys at "license + premium."

Midstream: mature nodes are "grunt work," but not in 2026

Mature nodes (28nm and above) have long been the main battleground of price wars, with gross margins stuck at 15%–25% year-round. 2026 is the exception: AI servers are absorbing advanced-node and memory capacity, pushing orders for power management, interface, driver and power discrete devices back to mature nodes, while 8-inch capacity is barely expanding globally, so prices have risen.

The data: SMIC's 8-inch BCD prices rose about 10%, with some orders up 20%; TSMC was reported in July to be raising mature-node quotes from early 2027, its first increase in more than three years; UMC's Q2 gross margin was 32.5%, up 3.3 points QoQ; GlobalFoundries' was 28.3%, up 4.1 points YoY. Four mature-node foundries improving at once shows this is industry-wide, not specific to SMIC.

But SMIC's improvement is the smallest: 20.1%, still down 2.4 points YoY. There is only one reason — its depreciation is growing fastest (+25.7%), while UMC and GlobalFoundries stopped expanding capacity long ago and their depreciation is flat. This is the difference between the "capacity expansion phase" and the "harvest phase".

Downstream: customers have no alternative, but nor do they have much money

Downstream sit the design companies. At the advanced-node end, Huawei takes about 43% of SMIC's N+2 capacity and Cambricon about 11%, leaving a dozen-plus AI chip companies to fight over the remainder. Institutions estimate SMIC's 7nm-class annual capacity equates to roughly 2.6 million AI chips, against domestic demand of more than 4 million. The shortage is real, but the customers are all still loss-making or barely profitable.

At the mature-node end, customers include Will Semiconductor, GigaDevice, SG Micro, Silan and BYD Semiconductor, making power, analog, MCU, CIS and power devices. These customers are extremely price-sensitive: after Chinese New Year 2026, domestic foundries were at one point preparing to cut prices 15%–20% to win orders, precisely because these customers could not absorb price increases.

This creates SMIC's pricing dilemma: advanced-node customers have demand but no money; mature-node customers have money but will walk. Price increases can only be selective—hence "structural price increases."

Regional and policy: a supply chain by design

This chain was planned into existence in mainland China: Huawei HiSilicon, Cambricon and Will Semiconductor in design; SMIC, Hua Hong and Nexchip in manufacturing; Naura, AMEC and ACM Research in equipment; National Silicon Industry Group, Anji and Jacques in materials. Every link carries state funding, and every link trails the international leaders by one to two generations.

This "full chain, nowhere strong" structure defines SMIC's role: it is the only link with scale capacity, so it must absorb the design companies' orders; yet its equipment and materials depend on overseas suppliers, leaving the pace of capacity expansion hostage to policy.It is both the engine of this chain and its most fragile link.

Policy cuts both ways for SMIC. Big Fund injections, capacity quotas and tax breaks give it resources no one else has; but pricing is capped by downstream customers' ability to pay, expansion proceeds on plan, and profit must be reinvested — all of which depress shareholder returns. Reading this chain means looking not only at "rising localisation rates" but at "who bears the cost of that rise".

Cycle position: third year of the upcycle, with two peak characteristics emerging

Three top signals: capex at a record high (USD 8.1 billion, 87% of revenue — already present); gross margin at a record high (20.1%, far below the 38% of 2022 — not present); customers starting to cut orders (not present). So the position is "late upcycle," not the top.

But the supply-side clock is already ticking: mainland China's mature-node capacity is forecast to reach 33% of global share in 2027, with four new 12-inch fabs ramping simultaneously. Supply typically lags demand by 18–24 months, meaning the 2026 shortage turns into a glut in the second half of 2027.

Implication for the stock: fundamentals have another two to three good quarters, while valuation already prices in 2029. The two are not contradictory — precisely the reason to "watch."

Conclusion

Conclusion: chain position B+, profit allocation C. SMIC sits in a "necessary but thin-margin" segment: no bargaining power upstream, and bargaining power downstream but its customers are poor too. The 2026 shortage gives it a one-off chance to collect rent; capacity released in 2027 will take that rent back. Worth tracking, not worth paying 6x P/B for.
