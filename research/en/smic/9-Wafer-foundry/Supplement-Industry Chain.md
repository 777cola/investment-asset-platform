## Upstream: equipment access capped below the leading edge, materials prices rising

Equipment is the only link in this chain that can choke SMIC. ASML is the only company globally that can make EUV lithography machines, and SMIC cannot obtain them, so N+2 and N+3 can only rely on DUV multi-patterning—the same wafer must be exposed more times, with lower yield and higher cost. In April 2026, the US plans to include DUV in its ban on China; if implemented, even capacity expansion in mature nodes will be affected.

The materials side is relatively better, but it is also moving in reverse. In 2026, 12-inch silicon wafers rose 15%–25% cumulatively, while 8-inch wafers rose 8%–12% and were raised another 5%–10% in the second quarter. Silicon wafers account for about 10%–15% of foundry costs, and this round of price increases directly eats 1–2 points of gross margin. Domestic substitution (Shanghai Silicon, Hangzhou Lion, Anji) is advancing, but high-end silicon wafers and photoresist still rely on imports.

What equipment and materials have in common is this: **SMIC has no bargaining power over them, only the right to choose the timing of purchases.** This is one of the structural reasons its gross margin is only 20% while TSMC's is 67.7%—TSMC buys equipment at "major customer prices," while SMIC buys equipment at "license + premium."

## Midstream: mature nodes are "grunt work," but not in 2026

Mature nodes (28nm and above) have long been the main battleground of price wars, with gross margins stuck at 15%–25% year-round. 2026 is the exception: AI servers are absorbing advanced-node and memory capacity, pushing orders for power management, interface, driver and power discrete devices back to mature nodes, while 8-inch capacity is barely expanding globally, so prices have risen.

The data: SMIC's 8-inch BCD prices rose about 10%, with some orders up 20%; TSMC was reported in July to be raising mature-node quotes from early 2027, its first increase in more than three years; UMC's Q2 gross margin was 32.5%, up 3.3 points QoQ; GlobalFoundries' was 28.3%, up 4.1 points YoY. Four mature-node foundries improving at once shows this is industry-wide, not specific to SMIC.

But SMIC's improvement is the smallest: 20.1%, still down 2.4 points YoY. There is only one reason — **its depreciation is growing fastest** (+25.7%), while UMC and GlobalFoundries stopped expanding capacity long ago and their depreciation is flat. This is the difference between the "capacity expansion phase" and the "harvest phase".

## Downstream: customers have no second choice, but they don't have much money either

Downstream sits the design companies. At the advanced-node end, Huawei takes about 43% of SMIC's N+2 capacity and Cambricon about 11%, leaving a dozen-plus AI chip companies fighting over the remainder—institutions estimate SMIC's 7nm-class annual capacity corresponds to roughly 2.6 million AI chips, while domestic demand exceeds 4 million.** The shortage is real, but the customers are all still loss-making or barely profitable.**

At the mature-node end, the customers are Will Semiconductor, GigaDevice, SG Micro, Silan, and BYD Semiconductor, making power, analog, MCU, CIS and power devices. These customers are extremely price-sensitive; after Chinese New Year 2026, domestic foundries were at one point preparing to cut prices 15%–20% to win orders, precisely because these customers could not absorb price increases.

This creates SMIC's pricing dilemma:** advanced-node customers have demand but no money; mature-node customers have money but will walk.** Price increases can only be selective—hence "structural price increases."

## Regional and policy: a supply chain by design

This chain was planned into existence in mainland China: Huawei HiSilicon, Cambricon and Will Semiconductor on the design side; SMIC, Hua Hong and Nexchip on the manufacturing side; Naura, AMEC and ACM Research on the equipment side; National Silicon Industry Group, Anji and Jacques on the materials side. Every link has state funding, and every link trails the international leaders by one to two generations.

This "full chain but nowhere strong" structure defines SMIC's role: it is the only link with scale capacity, so it must absorb the design companies' orders; but its equipment and materials depend on overseas suppliers, so the pace of its capacity expansion is constrained by policy.**It is both the engine of this chain and its most fragile link.**

Policy cuts both ways for SMIC: on one hand, Big Fund injections, capacity quotas and tax breaks give it resources no one else has; on the other, pricing is capped by downstream customers' ability to pay, expansion proceeds on plan, and profit must be reinvested — all of which depress shareholder returns. Reading this chain means looking not only at "rising localization rates" but at "who bears the cost of that rise".

## Cycle position: third year of the upcycle, two peak characteristics emerging

Three top signals: capex hitting a record high (USD 8.1 billion, 87% of revenue — already present), gross margin hitting a record high (20.1%, far below the 38% of 2022 — not present), customers beginning to cut orders (not present). **So the position is "late upcycle," not the top.**

But the supply-side clock is already ticking: mainland China's mature-node capacity is forecast to reach 33% of global share in 2027, with four new 12-inch fabs ramping simultaneously. Supply release typically lags demand by 18–24 months, meaning the 2026 shortage turns into a glut in the second half of 2027.

Implication for the stock price: fundamentals have another two to three good quarters, while valuation already prices in 2029. The two are not contradictory — precisely the reason to "watch."
