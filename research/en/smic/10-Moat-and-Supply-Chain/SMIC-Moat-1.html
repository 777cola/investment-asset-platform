Core call: wide moat, shallow profit pool

SMIC scores 6.6 on our weighted five-factor moat framework: licences and capacity quotas 9, customer switching costs 8, scale 8, cost 5, brand 3. The moat is wide enough that no second domestic fab able to take advanced-node orders will emerge within five years, but the pool it encloses is shallow — gross margin of 20.1%, and a moat keeps out competitors, not depreciation.

Position in the value chain: stuck in the middle, squeezed at both ends yet indispensable at both ends

Upstream sit equipment and materials, where SMIC has no pricing power. EUV is unobtainable and DUV was added to the proposed ban list in April 2026; 12-inch wafers are up 15%–25%, and SMIC can only absorb the increase. Costs at this end are rigid and do not bend to the company's operating efforts.

Downstream sit the design companies, where SMIC has pricing power—but only when capacity is tight. In February–March 2026 the company negotiated price increases customer by customer, covering only categories in short supply; mobile phones and panel drivers have "seen no price increase to date" because those customers are struggling too. Pricing power is therefore "conditional," and the condition is shortage.

The middle segment's value lies in irreplaceability: for domestic design companies pursuing advanced process nodes, SMIC is the only outlet; in automotive-grade BCD, SMIC has the largest capacity and the most complete certifications. Irreplaceability means "they must place orders with you," not "they must accept your price." The gap between those two is the gap between a 20% and a 67.7% gross margin.

Why moats cannot stop depreciation

A moat means "competitors cannot get in, customers cannot get out." SMIC satisfies both tests: a new fab takes three years, USD 10 billion and equipment licences, and no second domestic player will emerge within five years; customer qualification takes 18–24 months, and switching fabs means redoing the entire validation chain.

But a moat protects revenue, not profit. Depreciation does not care whether you have a moat—once equipment enters the fab it is amortised over five years, USD 5 billion a year, not a cent less. In 2026 depreciation grows 30% YoY against revenue growth of 11.5%; that gap between the two slopes is why gross margin falls.

A counter-check: if the moat protected profit, then with 2026 capacity utilisation at 93.1% and 8-inch prices up 10%, gross margin should improve sharply; in reality it rises only 0.9 points QoQ. The "scarcity premium" is being eaten by depreciation—the moat's returns all flow to equipment vendors.

The scoring weights confirm this. Licences and switching costs together carry half the weight, and both protect revenue; cost and technology together carry 30%, and only these determine profit—and they happen to be SMIC's two weakest items. A high moat score and a low profit score are not contradictory; they describe two sides of the same company.

Supply chain: three bottlenecks, none bypassable

The first bottleneck is lithography. With no EUV, SMIC's N+2 and N+3 nodes rely entirely on DUV multi-patterning, exposing the same layer three or four times over, which multiplies mask costs, tool occupancy time and yield loss. That is why its advanced-node capacity has never scaled, and why it has shifted focus to mature nodes and specialty processes.

The second bottleneck is spare parts and maintenance. Buying the equipment does not mean it can run indefinitely: original manufacturers' spare parts, software upgrades and on-site engineers all require licences. Since the Entity List, this line has been sustained by inventory and third-party channels, with no public data on supply stability, but it is a hidden risk widely acknowledged in the industry.

The third bottleneck is materials. Silicon wafers, photoresists, electronic specialty gases and CMP polishing pads remain dominated by Japan and the US in high-end categories. In the 2026 wafer price increase, SMIC has no choice but to accept; if controls extend to materials, utilization will feel the impact immediately. Of the three bottlenecks, lithography sets the ceiling, spare parts set stability, and materials set cost.

The real threat: Hua Hong and Nexchip, not TSMC

TSMC is chasing high-margin advanced-node orders that SMIC could never win anyway; the two are not on the same battlefield. The real threat to SMIC is domestic peers: Hua Hong posted revenue of USD 661 million in Q1 2026, up 22.2% YoY, with capacity utilisation above 100% for an extended period, while Nexchip and SiEn are expanding aggressively in CIS, power and MCU.

These players follow a simple playbook: win commodity categories on price. After Chinese New Year in 2026, several Chinese foundries plan to cut PMIC and DDI prices by 15%–20%, striking directly at the mature-node base that underpins SMIC. SMIC's choice is not to follow — protect gross margin and cede share. The near-term numbers look good; the long-term share gets eroded.

The second opponent is equipment controls. This is not competition but a supply cutoff: if DUV is banned, expansion halts while depreciation keeps running — the ugliest combination. This risk does more damage than any competitor, but it is unpredictable and cannot be hedged.

Conclusion

Conclusion: moat 6.6/10 — wide enough, not deep enough. It keeps competitors out, but not depreciation, and not a cut-off of equipment supply. A moat of this kind justifies a "cyclical premium", not a "monopolist premium" — 3–4x PB in an upcycle, 1.5–2x PB on the downside. At 6.3x PB, the market is treating a "wide river" as a "deep pool".
