③ Moat anatomy — SMIC

The river is wide, but the water is shallow.
Research date 2026/08/10  ·  Price ¥126.25 (-1.75%)  ·  Market cap About RMB 1.08 trillion  ·  P/E (TTM) Approximately 205x
← Back to the hub

Core view

A moat has to answer two questions: can competitors get in, and can customers get out? SMIC's answer is "they can't get in, they can't get out" — both solid. But a third question is often overlooked: can the moat protect profit? SMIC's answer is "no". This is a wide moat with shallow water.

First moat: licenses and production capacity quotas (9/10)

Building a 12-inch line takes three things: USD 10 billion, three years and equipment licenses. The third is the hardest. Since its 2020 addition to the Entity List, SMIC has needed licenses to buy equipment; EUV has never been obtainable, and by April 2026 even DUV had been placed on the proposed ban list. This barrier was erected by administrative action, not market competition, which makes it harder to cross than any technical barrier.

Add capacity quotas—local governments approving land, power and environmental permits—and Big Fund financing, and no new entrant has appeared in the domestic market over the past five years, nor will one in the next five. This is the hardest river to cross.

But there is a side effect: the administrative barrier also constrains SMIC's own expansion. Others cannot get in, and SMIC cannot get out—adding capacity requires permits first, and buying equipment depends on policy. The two-sided nature of the moat is clearest here.

Second moat: customer switching costs (8/10)

Automotive-grade chips take 18–24 months to qualify, requiring AEC-Q100 certification, volume-production validation and long-term supply agreements. Industrial and automotive revenue accounts for 14.0%, up 62.6% YoY — the stickiest part of the business, since switching fabs means redoing two years of certification.

Leading-edge processes are more extreme still: for a domestic design house to build 7nm-class chips, SMIC is the only option — switching fabs means switching countries. Huawei takes about 43% of N+2 capacity and Cambricon about 11%; this allocation is not market bidding but capacity quotas.

But switching cost has a precondition: switching cost only exists when the customer has alternatives; without alternatives, it is not choice but no choice. Many SMIC customers fall into the latter category. Revenue from no choice is stable, but pricing power is not there — which is why SMIC can only raise prices selectively.

Third moat: scale and cost (8/10, 5/10)

Scale: 1.078 million wafers/month (8-inch equivalent), third globally and first in mainland China. Scale delivers two benefits: procurement bargaining power (still weak versus equipment vendors) and capacity allocation, with orders shiftable across four fabs. Hua Hong sits at 508,000 wafers and Nexchip is smaller still; neither can close the gap near term.

Cost: 5 points. Depreciation is 43.4% of revenue, twice TSMC's; no bargaining power in wafer procurement; DUV multi-patterning lifts unit costs at advanced nodes. Cost is SMIC's weakest link and it is structural—not something management can fix.

Technology: 5 points. N+2 in mass production and N+3 advancing, but two generations behind, no EUV and yields undisclosed. Technology can hold orders, not gross margin.

The time dimension of moats: how many will survive five years?

Moats are not static. Look at the five moats five years out: the licence moat is the most stable, since regulation will not loosen; the switching-cost moat weakens as domestic EDA and IP mature and the cost of changing foundries falls; the scale moat comes under pressure, with both Hua Hong and Nexchip expanding capacity; the cost moat may improve as depreciation rolls off and domestic equipment lowers costs; and the technology moat hinges on whether an alternative path without EUV exists.

In other words, the aggregate moat score falls over five years, and the fastest-declining components are scale and switching costs — precisely the two that support the valuation today.

This is also why it cannot be valued on a perpetual basis. For a company whose moat is shrinking, fair value should decline over time; today's 6.3x PB implies a moat that is permanent and widening.

Conclusion

Moat anatomy conclusion: wide river (licence 9, switching 8, scale 8), shallow water (cost 5, technology 5), weighted score 6.6. The moat guarantees revenue, not rich profits. Paying 6.3x PB for a moat like this mistakes "won't disappear" for "highly profitable". One line to remember: a moat keeps rivals out, not depreciation, so it protects revenue, not profit. See this layer clearly and you won't mistake the moat for a valuation case.
