④ Financial reality — SMIC

Three lies hidden in three statements

Core view

Financial statements do not lie, but they mislead. Three items in SMIC's statements are easy to misread: "net profit +5%" on the income statement, "operating cash flow +RMB 5.1 billion" on the cash flow statement, and "debt ratio 33%" on the balance sheet. Each number looks fine on its own; only read together do they reveal the truth — it is making money, but not enough to pay for equipment.

Income statement: where the 5% net profit growth comes from

2026Q1 revenue RMB 2.506 billion (+11.5%), gross profit RMB 504 million (-0.5%) and net profit RMB 197 million (+5%). Revenue up, gross profit down, net profit up: three divergent directions, implying net profit growth did not come from the core business.

Breaking it down, gross profit fell USD 2.3 million YoY, while other income (government subsidies) came to about RMB 350 million and financial expenses were hit by RMB appreciation and lower interest income. Together, the two more than offset the gross profit shortfall. In other words, the +5% net profit growth is attributable to subsidies and FX, not operations.

The A-share basis is more telling: net profit RMB 1.361 billion, +0.36% YoY, essentially zero growth. This basis excludes part of the FX impact and is closer to true operating performance.

Cash flow statement: the other side of RMB 5.1bn operating cash inflow

Operating cash flow was RMB 5.132 billion (prior-year period: -RMB 1.172 billion), up RMB 6.3 billion YoY — an apparent massive improvement. Capex over the same period, however, was RMB 10.871 billion (USD 1.563 billion), with net financing inflows of RMB 16.044 billion.

Put the three numbers together: operations earned RMB 5.1 billion, investing spent RMB 10.9 billion, financing plugged RMB 16 billion. This is not "making money" — it is "financing plant construction." The operating cash flow improvement comes mainly from depreciation add-backs (USD 1.088 billion) and working capital changes, not from better earnings quality.

The full-year picture is clearer: EBITDA of RMB 5.256 billion, capex of RMB 8.1 billion, free cash flow net outflow of roughly RMB 2.8–4.0 billion (depending on working capital). How was this gap filled over the past five years? The 2020 STAR Market IPO of RMB 53.2 billion, the 2026 share issuance of RMB 40.6 billion, government subsidies, bank credit lines.

Balance sheet: the other side of a 33% debt ratio

Total assets USD 52.271 billion, total liabilities USD 17.250 billion, debt ratio 33%; cash USD 7.279 billion, net debt USD 660 million, net debt-to-equity 1.9%. A very healthy balance sheet, and the reason it weathered the 2023 trough.

But the health comes from equity financing, not profitability. Of USD 52.271 billion in total assets, 2025 net profit was USD 685 million, a return on total assets of 1.3%; equity attributable to shareholders is about USD 25 billion, for an ROE of 2.7%. What this balance sheet says: shareholders have put in a lot of money, the company uses it steadily, but earns very little.

One more thing to watch: the RMB 40.6 billion acquisition of 49% of SMIC North was paid in shares, raising total share capital from about 7.96 billion shares to 8.561 billion shares. The deal will lift net profit attributable to shareholders (reducing minority interests), but will not change return on total assets. When you see "net profit surges", distinguish operating improvement from the accounting effect of an equity acquisition.

An accounting detail: the useful lives used for depreciation

Semiconductor equipment is typically depreciated over five years, though some manufacturers use seven. Each additional year cuts annual depreciation by a meaningful amount and adds a corresponding amount to net profit. SMIC mainly uses five years—a relatively conservative choice for the industry.

How big is the impact? 2026 depreciation is about USD 5 billion. At seven years, annual depreciation would fall to about USD 3.6 billion and net profit would rise by USD 1.4 billion—twice the 2025 net profit. In other words, its income statement is far more sensitive to depreciation life than to revenue.

This also serves as a reminder: before reading its YoY net profit, first confirm whether the depreciation policy has changed. A policy change can double profit while cash flow does not move at all.

Conclusion

Financial reality: conclusion The company is profitable at the EBITDA line (RMB 5.256 billion) but that does not cover equipment (capex RMB 8.1 billion). It is growing (revenue +11.5%), yet growth does not translate into profit (net profit +5%). Its balance sheet is solid (debt ratio 33%), but what is solid is solvency, not return capacity. All three point to the same conclusion: these are not financials that can be read on P/E. The one line to remember: when reading these financials, start with cash flow and depreciation and leave net profit for last; reverse the order and you reach the opposite conclusion. Net profit is the result; depreciation and cash flow are the cause.
