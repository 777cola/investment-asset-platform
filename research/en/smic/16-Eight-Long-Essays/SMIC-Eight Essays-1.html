① Cognitive reset — SMIC

You think you're buying "China's TSMC" — you're not
Research date 2026/08/10  ·  Price ¥126.25 (-1.75%)  ·  Market cap About RMB 1.08 trillion  ·  PE(TTM) Approximately 205x
← Back to the hub

Core view

The market assigns SMIC a 205x P/E and 6.3x P/B, essentially pricing it as "China's TSMC." But put the two companies' financials side by side and the analogy collapses: TSMC runs a 67.7% gross margin, 33% ROE, positive free cash flow and pays dividends every year; SMIC runs a 20.1% gross margin, 2.7% ROE, negative free cash flow and pays no dividend. Same industry, two entirely different businesses. Until this step of the thesis is reset, every valuation that follows will be wrong.

Misconception 1: It is a "technology leader"

TSMC's premium comes from technological leadership: 2nm mass production, unmatched yields, and customers willing to pay a premium for first access. SMIC is not. Its N+2 is 7nm-class and N+3 is 5nm-class, achieved with DUV multi-patterning and no EUV—exposing the same layer three or four times, at higher cost and lower yield. The industry consensus is that N+3's actual performance is roughly equivalent to TSMC's 6nm-class.

Two generations behind on technology means it cannot capture "technology rent." What it captures is "shortage rent": domestic customers have no second option and must accept price increases when capacity is tight. The two types of rent are fundamentally different—technology rent can persist for a decade, while shortage rent tracks the capacity cycle and will disappear once capacity is released in 2027.

So the first assumption needs revising: it is not a technology leader but a holder of scarce capacity. The former merits a high valuation; the latter only a cyclical one.

Misconception 2: It is a "growth stock"

Revenue grew at a five-year CAGR of 19%, up 11.5% year on year in 2026Q1, with Q2 guidance of +14%–16% quarter on quarter — it looks like a growth stock. But a growth stock is defined as one whose profit grows in step with revenue and does not require proportional capital investment, and SMIC satisfies neither: 2026Q1 revenue rose 11.5% while net profit rose 5%, and capex was 87% of revenue.

A more accurate definition is "capital-intensive cyclical growth stock": revenue grows with capacity expansion, profit is suppressed by depreciation, and valuation swings with the cycle. The right valuation tools for such companies are PB and capacity replacement cost, not PE and PEG.

Run the PEG and the absurdity is obvious: 205x PE ÷ 11.5% growth = 17.8, extremely overvalued under any growth-stock framework. Run the PB: 6.3x against ROE of 2.7% implies ROE must rise above 15%. Both methods point to the same conclusion, just via different paths.

Misconception 3: Domestic substitution equals profit

Import substitution is real: China revenue accounts for 88.9%, consumer electronics up 26.9% YoY, industrial and automotive up 62.6%, global share rising to 5%, overtaking UMC and GlobalFoundries. These numbers show that "substitution" is happening.

But substitution does not equal profit. Substitution brings orders; orders must be filled by capacity; capacity must be built with capex; capex turns into depreciation; depreciation eats profit.Along this chain, every link adds revenue, and only the last link subtracts profit.

Evidence: 2025 revenue USD 9.327 billion, up 16.2% YoY; net profit USD 685 million, up 39% — seemingly solid, but net profit includes government subsidies and FX gains; 2026Q1 revenue up 11.5%, net profit up 5%, A-share net profit up 0.36%, essentially zero growth. The better the substitution, the heavier the depreciation, the thinner the profit.

Misconception 4: It won't fail, so it's safe

Many people buy SMIC because "the state won't let it fail." That statement is true, but it answers "will it go bankrupt," not "will I lose money."

PetroChina didn't fail either, yet its share price still hasn't returned to its IPO price since listing in 2007; BOE didn't fail either, raising over RMB 100 billion in capital over two decades while shareholder returns have been close to zero. What these companies share is this: they won't die, but they don't make much money for shareholders either. Their value lies in the industry, not the share price.

"Won't fail" offers the safety of a creditor, not the return of an equity holder. Buyers must be clear about whether they are creditors or shareholders—if a shareholder, the question is "can future cash flows cover the price I paid," not "will this company disappear."

Conclusion

Conclusion of the cognitive reset: SMIC is "the holder of mainland China's only scaled foundry capacity," a capital-intensive cyclical growth stock that earns money from shortage rents rather than technology rents. Seen through that identity, a 205x PE is not "a bit expensive" — it is "the wrong ruler." One line to remember: assume it is TSMC and you will measure it with TSMC's ruler, and it does not deserve that ruler. Get the identity wrong and every subsequent valuation step goes wrong with it, and wrong in a highly consistent way. This matters more than any valuation model.
