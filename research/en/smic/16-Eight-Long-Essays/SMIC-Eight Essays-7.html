⑦ Risk list — SMIC

Six risks, ranked by probability × impact

Core view

The value of a risk list lies not in how many items it lists, but in how accurately it ranks them. SMIC's six risks, ranked by "probability × impact", are: depreciation (high probability, medium impact), cutthroat competition (medium-high probability, high impact), export controls (medium probability, extremely high impact), A/H premium convergence (high probability, medium impact), governance and dividends (certain, low impact), and technology roadmap (low probability, high impact).

Ranked first: depreciation — certain and recurring every year

2025 depreciation was USD 3.81 billion, or 40.9% of revenue; 2026 is about USD 5 billion, up 30% year on year, or 43%–44% of revenue; for 2027 the company explicitly said it "will keep growing." That means the numerator of gross margin (price) has to race against the denominator (depreciation), and depreciation is locked in ahead of time.

Quantified: the extra USD 1.2 billion of depreciation in 2026 equals 12.9% of 2025 revenue. To offset it through pricing, ASP would have to rise 13%; in Q1 it rose only 2.5%. That 10-point gap is the source of the 2.4-point year-on-year decline in gross margin.

The defining feature of this risk is that it is "certain": it needs no bad news, it happens every year. So it is the first constraint on valuation — as long as depreciation keeps rising, the PE will not look good.

Number two: involution — Chinese capacity cannibalizing itself

Mainland China's mature-node capacity is expected to account for 33% of global capacity in 2027. Hua Hong, Nexchip and SiEn are all expanding, and their playbook is the same: win general-purpose categories on low price. A signal already appeared after Chinese New Year in 2026: several Chinese foundries were preparing to cut prices on PMIC and DDI by 15%–20%.

SMIC's response is not to match prices, protecting gross margin and ceding share. Financially correct in the short term, but share will be eroded over the long term. The bigger problem is that this race to the bottom is not a market outcome but the result of local governments wanting output value and jobs—it will not stop because the industry is losing money.

The trigger point for this risk is most likely in the second half of 2027: the four major new fabs reach full production, domestic capacity is released in a concentrated wave, and demand growth slows—all three conditions met at once.

Ranked third to sixth: regulation, premium, governance, technology

Export controls: In April 2026 the US plans to add DUV to its China ban, covering five companies including SMIC. Probability is low, but if enacted, expansion stalls while depreciation continues — the ugliest combination. In February 2026 Applied Materials was fined USD 252 million for supplying SMIC in violation of rules, showing enforcement is real.

A/H premium: The 121% premium can compress without any change in fundamentals. From 9 July to 10 August both listings fell 26%–27% in tandem and the premium did not narrow; but once capital flows reverse direction, the premium will compress passively.

Governance: No dividends, no buybacks, state-led — falling share prices meet no company-level buying. This is the risk of "no downside protection".

Technology: What comes after N+3? Without EUV, the next step down (3nm equivalent) is orders of magnitude harder. That is a post-2029 problem, but no one can answer it now.

Worst-case scenario: the 2027 triple hit

Stack three risks together: depreciation keeps rising in 2027 (capex does not come down), mature-node prices get pushed back by domestic peers (overcapacity), and the AI capex wave recedes (demand declines). The result: revenue growth drops to single digits, gross margin falls below 15%, and net profit returns to 2023 levels (less than RMB 1 billion for the full year).

How would the stock price move then? If PB falls from 6.3x back to 2x, that implies RMB 40 — 68% below the current price. This is not a forecast; it is the floor of a stress test.

How likely is it? The probability of all three happening at once is not high, but the probability of two happening at once is not low (depreciation + cutthroat competition). That is why we assign a 30% weight to the bear case, and why RMB 126 is not a buy point.

Conclusion

Conclusion of the risk checklist: The first two risks (depreciation, cutthroat competition) are high-probability and already unfolding; the latter four are low-frequency or slow-moving variables. Taken together, the most reasonable response is not to liquidate, but to "not chase highs and wait for the price to offer a buffer." A 205x PE price assumes none of the six risks occurs. One line to remember: the one that tops the risk checklist is always the one that happens every year yet no one wants to calculate. Because once it is calculated clearly, it becomes very hard to convince yourself to buy at a high level.
