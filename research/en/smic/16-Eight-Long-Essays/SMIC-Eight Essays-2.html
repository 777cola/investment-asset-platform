② Nature of the business — SMIC

A money printer, and also a money shredder

Core view

The foundry business reduces to a formula: profit = capacity × utilization × ASP − depreciation − materials − expenses. Of the four variables, capacity and depreciation are decided first and take effect later, whereas utilization and ASP are decided in the current period. That time lag is the source of every story in the foundry industry.

Revenue side: three variables, all equally essential

Capacity: 1.078 million 8-inch equivalent wafers per month, versus 1.059 million at end-2025, a net increase of 19,500 wafers in the quarter. Capacity cannot be added at will: a 12-inch line takes three years, USD 10 billion and equipment licences.

Utilisation: 93.1%, up 9.5 pts YoY and down 2.6 pts QoQ. Above 90% is a seller's market; below 85% is a buyer's market. This line determines whether the company can pick its customers.

ASP: up 2.5% QoQ in the first quarter. Price increases stem from shortage: 8-inch BCD up about 10%, some orders 20%; smartphone and panel driver ICs not up a cent. The structural dispersion in ASP matters more than the average.

Revenue is the product of these three variables, and Q1 2026 revenue of USD 2.506 billion is what that multiplication yields. To forecast the next quarter you need only forecast these three numbers — and guidance already gives two of them (capacity, utilisation), leaving only ASP.

Cost side: depreciation is rigid, materials are flexible

Depreciation: USD 3.81 billion in 2025 and about USD 5 billion in 2026, equal to 43%–44% of revenue. This reflects USD 8.1 billion a year of capex over the past three years, already locked in and amortised monthly over the next five years; it will not move with demand.

Materials: silicon wafers, photoresist and specialty gases, about 20% of cost. In 2026, 12-inch wafers rise 15%–25% and 8-inch wafers 8%–12%. This portion is variable, but the direction is up and the company has no bargaining power.

Labour and energy are small shares; R&D spend is USD 774 million (2025, 8.3% of revenue).

The cost structure dictates gross-margin sensitivity: for every 10% drop in revenue, the depreciation share rises 4.3 points and gross margin falls 4.3 points. Gross margin is only 20.1%; a 4-point drop puts it near the threshold. This is the cash-furnace side — when capacity sits idle, depreciation still accrues and profit vanishes instantly.

Cash flow: where did the money go?

EBITDA was USD 5.256 billion in 2025, a 57.3% margin — the "money printer" side of the story: operations throw off over USD 5 billion of cash a year. That cash does not stay in the company, however. Capex was USD 8.1 billion, a USD 2.8 billion shortfall, and another USD 8.1 billion goes in during 2026.

The cash-flow cycle is therefore: earn money → buy equipment → depreciate → earn money again → buy equipment again. Each turn brings larger capacity, larger revenue and larger depreciation, while cash to shareholders stays at zero. No dividend for fiscal 2025 is the direct result of this cycle.

When does it end? One of two conditions must be met: the capacity target is reached (no further expansion needed), or demand collapses (expansion loses its purpose). The former is good, the latter a disaster, but both turn free cash flow positive — the difference is that valuation re-rates upward in one case and de-rates in the other.

Who is paying for this formula

A fab's profit splits four ways: customers want low prices, equipment vendors want high prices, local governments want output value, and shareholders want returns. At SMIC the split came out as follows — customers got capacity (88.9% of revenue from China), equipment vendors got USD 8.1 billion in orders, local governments got investment and jobs from four new fabs, and shareholders got USD 685 million in net profit and 7% dilution.

Of the four, shareholders rank last. That is not management bias but a function of the ownership structure: the Big Fund, Datang and Xinxin represent industrial objectives, not financial returns.

Recognise this and the valuation method changes. SMIC cannot be valued on discounted shareholder cash flow, but on capacity replacement cost plus a cycle premium. The former yields an extremely low number; the latter explains the current price — but it explains why some people buy, not why you should.

Conclusion

Conclusion on the nature of the business: This is "a manufacturing business that can only sustain growth by continuously reinvesting capital." It is strong on both cash generation and cash consumption, and the net effect is that shareholders receive no cash. The appropriate valuation for a business of this kind is "replacement cost + cyclical premium," not "discounted future cash flow + growth premium." In one sentence: its cash-generating power accrues to equipment suppliers and customers; shareholders get their share only once expansion stops. This is not a moral issue but a structural feature of the business.
