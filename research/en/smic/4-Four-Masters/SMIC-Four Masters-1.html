Four Masters — SMIC

Core call: all four masters converge on "wait"

Duan Yongping asks who ultimately captures the economics, and the answer is "capacity"; Buffett asks how many times book scarcity deserves, and the answer is "a 2.7% ROE does not justify 6.3x"; Munger asks what could halve the stock, and the answer is "depreciation keeps rising, price increases stall, the A/H premium converges" — none of the three requires fundamentals to deteriorate; Li Lu gives it the highest strategic endorsement, but what he wants is cash flow ten years out, not 205x P/E. All four reach the same conclusion: they believe in the business, not the price.

Duan Yongping: who ultimately captures the economics of this business?

Duan Yongping asks only two questions of any company: what makes this business money, and who ultimately gets the money it makes. He does not like SMIC's answer to the first — it makes money by filling capacity, and every dollar it earns must immediately turn into equipment. 2026 capex is USD 8.1 billion and depreciation about USD 5 billion, against 2025 net profit of only USD 685 million. He often says "profit can be adjusted, cash flow does not lie," and SMIC's free cash flow is negative year after year: the next stop for its cash flow is the fab, not shareholders' pockets.

He would concede this is a respectable company: the only wafer foundry in mainland China that can deliver at scale, with Huawei's Ascend and Kirin chips both riding on it, leaving customers no alternative. But "respectable" and "worth buying" are two different things, and he keeps them clearly apart.

What he would really press on is the picture ten years out: will this company still be here? Most likely yes — the state will not let it fail. But "still here" does not equal "worth 6x PB." NetEase, Apple and Moutai, all of which he has owned, share one trait: the business generates money on its own. SMIC must keep investing to hold its position — two entirely different kinds of business.

So his conclusion is roughly this: a good asset, a hard business, waiting for a bad price.

Buffett: How many times book value can scarcity command?

Buffett would concede this is the kind of asset he likes: capacity that cannot be replicated, long customer qualification cycles, government backing. His Q3 2022 purchase of TSMC ADRs rested on the same logic — roughly 13–15x P/E and 3x P/B at the time, with TSMC's ROE at 30%.

Apply the same yardstick to SMIC: ROE 2.7%, P/B 6.3x. Run it through his framework — a company with long-term ROE of 3% warrants a fair P/B of roughly 1.5x; to justify 6.3x, you must assume ROE triples to 20% within three years. That assumption may prove right, but it is not a margin of safety — it is optimism.

He would also fixate on depreciation. Depreciation is real cash outlay brought forward, not an accounting game — 2026 depreciation of USD 5 billion is 6x 2025 net profit. When depreciation dominates a company's income statement, the P/E ratio loses its meaning, and he never pays for "profits that haven't happened yet."

He held TSMC for just two quarters before selling, and the reason was not valuation but geopolitical risk. SMIC's geopolitical risk exceeds TSMC's, and its valuation is twice as expensive — put the two together, and Buffett would not hesitate.

Munger: invert the question — what would make it halve?

Munger's habit was to list the ways to die first. SMIC has three. First, depreciation keeps growing at 30% while price increases stall. Depreciation is USD 5 billion in 2026 and will rise further in 2027; as long as ASP does not rise, gross margin will fall from 20.1% back below 17% and net profit will turn negative YoY — this requires no deterioration in demand, only prices standing still.

Second, equipment controls tighten further. In April 2026 the US plans to add DUV to the China ban, covering five companies including SMIC. If expansion is cut off, capacity ramp-up at the four new fabs stalls, depreciation continues to be booked and revenue does not grow — the ugliest combination.

Third, A-share sentiment recedes. This one requires no deterioration in fundamentals, only money changing direction: on the same day, H shares at HKD 66.20 convert to RMB 57, versus RMB 126.25 for A shares, a premium of 121%. When A shares hit RMB 173 on 9 July, that premium was above 160%; a month later it has fallen back to 121%, with A shares down 27% and H shares down 26% — the synchronized decline is coincidence, but convergence will happen sooner or later.

Munger would say: of these three, the first two require waiting, and the third is already happening. So do not ask "is the company good", ask "how much optimism is still in the price".

Li Lu: Who holds pricing power in a civilisation-scale shift

Li Lu would assign it the highest strategic rating. Semiconductor localisation is not a cycle but a two-decade structural realignment: China imports over USD 300 billion of chips annually, domestic manufacturing self-sufficiency remains low, and SMIC is the only node on this chain with scale capacity. Such a position has historically captured excess profit—TSMC grew exactly this way in the 1980s.

But he would press one question: does the excess profit go to the company, or to the state? If prices are regulated, capacity expansion follows state planning, and profit must be reinvested, then "industry success" and "shareholder returns" will diverge for a long time. He bought BYD because he was betting on the product of the entrepreneur and the industry's upside; SMIC's management are not entrepreneurs but executors, which his framework discounts.

His approach is typically to buy the segment where "the industry succeeds and shareholders get paid": wait for the company to start paying dividends, wait for free cash flow to turn positive, wait for capex as a share of revenue to fall from 87% to below 50%. Until those three signals appear, he would concede this is a good company but would not pay 205x P/E for a "national project."

A realistic choice: if you really want to buy, A-shares or H-shares?

All four say "wait," but for those already holding A-shares the question is more concrete: for the same SMIC, the A-share trades at RMB 126.25 against HKD 66.20 (RMB 57.2) for the H-share — a 121% premium. Why not switch into the H-share?

The case for switching is hard-nosed. Same shares, same rights, same financials, same dividend policy (no dividend at all); what the H-share collects each year is a price discount, not compensation for risk. Historically the premium has ranged between 50% and 150%, so "twice as expensive" is the norm rather than a one-off mispricing — but at 121% it sits toward the upper end of the range, with more room down than up.

The case against is equally real. First, liquidity: A-shares often trade RMB 5–10 billion a day versus only HKD 300–500 million for the H-share, so large flows in and out incur slippage. Second, FX: with the renminbi at 6.7367 in 2026, part of the H-share return in RMB terms is eaten by the exchange rate. Third, access: domestic money buying H-shares must go through Stock Connect, which carries quota and eligibility limits.

The compromise: if you can only buy A-shares, write the 121% premium into your expected return as a "cost of holding" — meaning that on identical fundamentals, an A-share investor must wait for 20%–30% more upside than an H-share investor just to break even. This is not a theoretical point; it directly determines that RMB 126 is not an entry point, and that only below RMB 100 does it barely qualify as one.

The neutral scenario already sits below the current price

The probability-weighted expected return across the three scenarios is negative. Applying weights of 30% pessimistic, 50% neutral and 20% optimistic: 0.3×(-30%) + 0.5×(-5%) + 0.2×(+23%) = -6.9%. This is less a precise forecast than a judgment on magnitude — 23% upside against 30% downside, odds below 1:1.3, with the probability distribution clearly skewed to the downside.

The optimistic scenario requires three conditions to hold simultaneously: price increases extending into 2027, depreciation growth peaking in 2027, and the A/H premium not converging. Two of the three lie outside the company's control. The neutral scenario requires only "full-load utilization sustained + 5% ASP growth per year"; even on such mild assumptions the target price sits 5% below the current price, implying the current price already discounts optimism beyond the neutral scenario.

Conclusion

Conclusion: four investors agree on the business, not the price. Duan Yongping's objection is the absence of free cash flow; Buffett's is that ROE does not justify the P/B; Munger notes all three decline paths remain open; Li Lu and others are waiting for industry success to transmit into shareholder returns. They differ only on "what it is worth" — none says "buy now." The triggers are clear: P/B back below 3x (about RMB 60), free cash flow turning positive, or the start of dividends. Should any one of the three appear, the odds must be recalculated rather than continuing to wait.
