Masterclass Close Read: Li Lu — SMIC

Read financial reports to judge whether "industry success will turn into shareholder success"

Core view

Li Lu reads financial reports differently from most: he does not calculate a single quarter's profit. He works out where the industry will sit in China ten years from now, then asks who collects the rent on that position. SMIC's results answer: the industry is succeeding, the rent accrues to capacity, and shareholders get only dilution.

Industry position: the only scaled node

2026Q1 revenue USD 2.506 billion, global pure-play foundry share about 5%, ranking third worldwide and first in mainland China. 12-inch monthly capacity of 1.078 million wafers (8-inch equivalent), four new lines under construction, advanced process N+2 supports Huawei's Ascend and Kirin series.

Together these numbers say one thing: mainland China's chip design industry has grown up, but manufacturing has only one outlet. The advanced or specialty process orders from Huawei, Cambricon, Will Semiconductor, GigaDevice and SG Micro all ultimately land on SMIC's capacity. This "single mandatory node" position has historically generated long-term excess profit in industrial history.

Li Lu would acknowledge this, and would also acknowledge that this is not a matter of one quarter — it is a structural reordering on a twenty-year scale.

Who gets the rent: three pieces of evidence

Evidence one: no dividend.No cash dividend and no bonus shares for 2025, on the grounds that capex takes priority. That is an explicit statement that "profits belong to capacity, not to shareholders." Evidence two: share issuance.The RMB 40.6 billion acquisition of 49% of SMIC North is paid in shares, diluting existing shareholders by 7% — shareholders bear the cost of industrial consolidation. Evidence three: pricing.Price increases cover only categories where demand outstrips supply; handset and panel driver chips "have not raised prices to date," showing that the company does not fully set prices and must yield when customers are struggling.

The three pieces of evidence point to one conclusion: this company's excess profit goes first to national capacity targets, then to customer relationships, and only last to shareholders.This is not a management choice but the inevitable result of the ownership structure and industrial policy.

Li Lu's approach is to wait for the phase when "industrial success transmits through to shareholders": dividends begin, free cash flow turns positive, and capex falls below 50% of revenue. Until those three signals appear, he acknowledges it is a good company but will not buy.

Time horizon: he is willing to wait, but not to pay a premium

Li Lu's holding period is typically more than five years, so he does not care about a single quarter's gross margin. But he does care that "the purchase price takes the benefits of the next decade in advance"—205x PE and 6.3x PB amount to discounting all of the 2028–2030 capacity and price increases back to today.

In his framework, such a price is not "expensive"—it is "wrong." Wrong in mistaking industrial value for shareholder value. Industrial value is real (mainland China needs a TSMC of its own); shareholder value must wait for cash flow to prove it.

Conclusion

Li Lu-style conclusion: Full marks on industry judgment, failing marks on shareholder returns. He would put SMIC on the "track long term, wait for structural change" list—waiting not for the share price to fall, but for three structural indicators to turn: dividend policy, capex intensity, and free cash flow. Before that turn, any price pays a premium for a "national project."
