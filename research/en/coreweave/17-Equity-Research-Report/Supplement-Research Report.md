## I. Company Overview: From Mining Farm to 4.6GW

CoreWeave (NASDAQ: CRWV) was founded in 2017 as an Ethereum mining company; after the coin price crash in 2019, it converted its mining rigs into a GPU cloud — that was the company's true starting point. It IPO'd in March 2025 at USD 40 per share, raising about USD 1.5 billion, with Nvidia as an anchor investor subscribing USD 250 million. **It opened at about USD 57 on its first trading day and was once seen as the "first AI infrastructure stock."**

As of Q3 2026: contracted power 4.6GW, operating 1.7GW, quarterly revenue USD 3.661 billion, market cap about USD 22 billion, enterprise value about USD 83.5 billion. The business is highly concentrated in the US, with customers such as Meta, OpenAI and Microsoft on the buy side. **The company has only one product line: renting GPU compute under contract, billed by usage.**

On ownership, the three founders control super-voting rights through Class B shares, and Nvidia is the largest external strategic shareholder; institutional holdings are mainly growth funds, which means the share price is more sensitive to "narrative shifts" than to financial-report details — the November 2026 decline reflects exactly that sensitivity.

The company discloses no segment data, only one business line, but it does disclose two operating metrics — contracted power and operating power. For this company, those two numbers say more about scale than revenue does, because revenue is "capacity in operation × rent," and capacity is what represents the future.

## II. Industry analysis: demand explodes, the bottleneck shifts to power

Demand-side certainty is high. NVIDIA's FY2027Q3 (November 17) revenue reached USD 115.8 billion, up +103% year over year; the four major North American cloud providers sharply raised 2026 capex, with Microsoft at USD 45.2 billion for the quarter and about USD 170 billion for the full year. **The compute gap for AI training and inference remains unfilled in 2026.**

But supply is expanding in tandem, and the bottleneck has shifted from GPUs to power and grid interconnection. Transformer lead times run 2–3 years, and GW-scale campuses in some US regions face 5–7 year interconnection queues. **The industry sits "mid-expansion, before the shakeout": prices are still rising (a 25% increase in July, a further 10% at quarter-end), but the smartest buyer (Microsoft) has begun pulling back outsourcing**—a classic late-cycle signature.

On valuation transmission, industry momentum and its share price have a non-linear relationship: when demand beats expectations it rises the most (leverage amplification), and when demand peaks it falls first (bonds move first). Judging industry position therefore matters more than judging company operations, and the current industry position is "mid-expansion, buyers beginning to contract."

Implication for investors: the beta opportunity in this industry has passed (2024–2025); 2026–2027 is the phase of "picking stocks and picking position," and upstream and hyperscaler positions are superior to intermediaries—the industry basis for this report not assigning neocloud a Buy rating.

## III. Business model: a contractor with leverage

The model is simple: sign 5-year long-term contracts, use the contracts to raise financing, buy cards and build data centers, collect rent monthly. **It earns the spread among "construction timeline + power price + cost of capital."** It controls neither chips, nor models, nor end customers — a classic intermediary.

The accounting structure dictates how the financials look: GPUs bypass cost and go to depreciation, so adjusted EBITDA margin reaches 62%; after deducting depreciation, GAAP operating margin is only 5.7%; deduct a further 846 million in net interest and the net margin is -19.4%. **Every 1 yuan of revenue requires 2.58 yuan of equipment investment — a ratio that dictates it must keep raising financing.**

An easily overlooked cost item is power. Of the 4.6GW contracted power, the 2.9GW not yet in operation still incurs capacity reservation fees and grid connection fees; these do not go into capex but into operating costs, and will compress gross margin in the form of a "rising share of electricity costs" as capacity is gradually commissioned.

Competitors can replicate this model: Nebius, Crusoe and IREN use the same template, differing only in leverage and customers. This means the "model" itself is no barrier; the only barriers are cost of capital and power resources — and it holds an advantage in neither.

## IV. Financial analysis: growth and cash burn at the same pace

Quality of growth: revenue beat guidance for three consecutive quarters, but adjusted operating margin fell from 15.9% a year earlier to 10.4%, showing expenses growing faster than revenue and no scale effect yet. **The bright spot is GAAP operating profit turning positive for the first time (RMB 207 million), showing genuine improvement at the operating level.**

Cash quality is the weak spot: operating cash flow was RMB 1.975 billion, of which RMB 2.127 billion was customer prepayments, leaving a negative figure after exclusion; capex cash spending was RMB 9.441 billion (RMB 14.5 billion on an accrual basis), and free cash flow was negative RMB 7.466 billion, widening each quarter (-4.71 / -5.74 / -7.47). On debt: RMB 46.873 billion, cost 7.9%, RMB 41.6 billion with recourse, RMB 13.4 billion due within two years, cash of RMB 5.5 billion.

Looking at the three quarters of 2026 together, the trend is clear: revenue of RMB 1.82 → 2.58 → 3.66 billion (the latter two derived), free cash flow of negative RMB 4.71 → 5.74 → 7.47 billion. For every RMB 100 million of additional revenue, the cash shortfall widens by about RMB 140 million — direct evidence of negative operating leverage.

One item deserves separate accounting: customer prepayments are booked as deferred revenue (a liability). The RMB 2.127 billion increase this quarter means equivalent compute must be delivered in future to convert it into revenue; if delivery slips, the money must be refunded or compensated, and with only 1.7GW of 4.6GW in operation, delivery risk is not hypothetical.

## V. Management: score execution and capital allocation separately

The three founders have clear divisions of labor: Michael Intrator (CEO, founder of former energy trading software company GasTech) oversees strategy and external affairs, Brian Venturo (CSO, former hedge fund trader) oversees capital, and Brannin McBee (CDO) oversees site selection, power and contracts. **Delivery speed is the industry benchmark: 900MW of contracts added and 200MW brought online in a single quarter.**

Capital allocation is aggressive: in 2025 it planned to acquire Core Scientific (gaining power and ready-built data centers) with about USD 9 billion in stock, which the target's shareholders rejected in October; it then shifted to leasing power and self-building, and in 2026 is simultaneously pursuing RMB 4.2 billion in convertible bonds, RMB 2.6 billion in delayed-draw loans and a 35 million share follow-on offering. **Dual-class shares and the Nvidia关联三角 (supplier + shareholder + backstop) constitute a governance discount.**

The incentive structure merits attention: management compensation is mainly equity, tightly linking the share price to personal wealth — a positive incentive during expansion, but with the stock -71.4% from its high it could induce "more aggressive financing to sustain growth" — bad for creditors and not necessarily good for shareholders either.

## Six, growth drivers: three legs, each weaker than the last

**First, operating power ramp**: from 1.7GW to 4.6GW, with each GW corresponding to roughly USD 8.6 billion in annual revenue (on a rough Q3 annualized basis) — the most certain driver. **Second, price increases**: +25% in July and +10% at quarter-end, but only on newly signed contracts; existing contracts take effect at renewal. **Third, new customers**: framework contracts of USD 21 billion with Meta and USD 6.5 billion with OpenAI.

All three legs face constraints: the power ramp is limited by grid connection and transformer lead times; pricing depends on supply shortage, and that shortage is easing; new customers are concentrated in a few names, and the contracts are maximum commitment amounts. **The more fundamental constraint is capex intensity of 258% — the faster the growth, the more debt it must raise.**

Another detail on customer mix: the top two customers at 54% of revenue is a 2026Q3 figure, whereas Microsoft alone accounted for 62% in 2024. Concentration has eased slightly, but what that buys is "multiple customers can all build their own" — diversification has not reduced risk, it has merely shifted it from "one customer" to "one industry."

The quality of the growth drivers can be seen in one ratio: new orders of USD 28.5 billion ÷ new cash capex of USD 9.441 billion ≈ 3.0. That implies RMB 0.33 of equipment investment per RMB 1 of orders — not bad on the face of it, but orders are paid in installments over 5 years while equipment is paid in cash up front; the timing mismatch is the problem.

## VII. Competitive landscape: the middleman is in the most awkward position

Four types of players: **NVIDIA** (70%+ gross margin, ecosystem lock-in), **hyperscalers** (4%–5% cost of capital, own customers and grid access), **neoclouds** (CoreWeave, Nebius, Crusoe, IREN — fast delivery but no pricing power), **sovereign and regional clouds** (subsidies, preferential power tariffs).

CoreWeave ranks first among neoclouds by scale, but its size is negligible against Microsoft's single-quarter capex of USD 45.2 billion. **The most dangerous rival is Microsoft: it is simultaneously customer, supplier (through in-house Maia chips) and competitor, and is reviewing USD 80 billion of external compute commitments and cancelling a USD 3.22 billion Nscale order.** Buyers pulling back outsourcing is the most structural blow to an intermediary.

Comparison with Nebius and IREN highlights the difference: the three have similar revenue models, but CoreWeave carries the highest leverage (D/E 16.5), the most concentrated customers (54%) and the most expensive capital (7.9%). In the same sector selloff it fell 71% while the other two drew down around 50% — the gap is the price of leverage.

## VIII. Risk warnings: five types, four related to capital structure

**① Refinancing risk**: RMB 13.4 billion maturing in 2027 against RMB 5.5 billion of cash; unsecured bond yields have risen to about 15%. **② Customer concentration**: top two customers at 54%; contracts are maximum commitment amounts, not guaranteed payments. **③ Depreciation assumptions**: moving from 6 years to 4 years turns GAAP operating profit negative immediately and adds RMB 11.5 billion in annual depreciation.

**④ Equity dilution**: 35 million shares issued (about 6.3%) plus roughly 3% annual equity compensation. **⑤ Circular financing breaks down**: Nvidia is under SEC investigation over its accounting treatment and Jensen Huang has resigned as CEO, weakening the credibility of supply priority and backstop commitments. **The five risks reinforce one another, and their transmission paths all point to "dilution."**

Timeline of the risks: near term (within 6 months), equity dilution is most likely; medium term (12–18 months), rising refinancing costs; long term (beyond 24 months), a re-rating of the business model. Three timelines map to three responses, but for anyone at 0% position, all three mean "stay out."

Risk control hinges not on the company but on the portfolio: cap any single high-volatility name at 2%, and this report views the appropriate position as 0%—because the payoff is "lose 70% to make 9%," and any position above 0 pays for negative expected value.

## Nine, earnings forecast: return to profit in 2027 hinges on one aggressive assumption

2026E: revenue USD 13.15 billion (midpoint of guidance), adjusted EBITDA USD 8.15 billion (62%), net interest USD 3.5 billion, operating cash flow USD 5.5 billion, capex USD 37 billion, free cash flow -USD 29.5 billion. **The company burns nearly USD 30 billion for the full year, more than twice its revenue.**

2027E: revenue USD 25 billion, EBITDA USD 13 billion, interest USD 4.5 billion, operating cash flow USD 11 billion, free cash flow +USD 4 billion — **but this swing to positive depends entirely on capex falling from USD 37 billion to USD 7 billion (from 290% to 28% of revenue)**. If capex stays above USD 15 billion, free cash flow remains -USD 4 billion and the neutral target price drops to USD 25–30.

Forecast sensitivity: every 10% shortfall in 2027 revenue cuts EBITDA by about USD 1.3 billion, which at 7x reduces EV by USD 9.1 billion and equity value by USD 9.1 billion (about -USD 15/share); every additional USD 5 billion of capex adds USD 5 billion of net debt, likewise -USD 8/share. Both directions are asymmetric — downside elasticity far exceeds upside.

Placed on a timeline, these risks converge on 2027 as the key node: USD 13.4 billion maturing, the first GPU cohort entering mid-life depreciation, and Microsoft Maia 300 reaching mass production. Three events colliding in the same year is the combination this report least wants to see, but one with a non-trivial probability.

## 10. Valuation: three scenarios, one conclusion

Method: EV/EBITDA less net debt to back out equity value; share count of 560–620 million shares (including potential issuance). **Bear case USD 12** (credit tightening, equity financing needed, 30% dilution); **Base case USD 43** (7x × 13 billion = EV 91 billion, less net debt 65 billion, divided by 605 million shares); **Bull case USD 97** (8x × 16 billion = 128 billion, less 68 billion, divided by 620 million shares).

No PE — GAAP losses persist, making PE meaningless; no PS either — PS of 2.4x looks cheap, but revenue quality hinges on 258% capex intensity. **The base case is only 9% above the current price, the bear case -70% — the quantitative expression of "risk/reward does not work."** Probability-weighted (35/45/20) expected value USD 43.

Another valuation lens is the asset method: 4.6GW × USD 30 million/MW ≈ USD 138 billion replacement cost, seemingly far above EV of 83.5 billion. But replacement cost is not value — equipment depreciates, power contracts expire, customers leave. Valuing on replacement cost was the most common mistake in the 2000 telecom bubble.

## 11. Investment recommendation: avoid, with no buy price set

The core argument is not that "AI demand will fall" — that probability is very low. The core argument is: **equity is priced off the credit market, not off AI demand.** In mid-November its unsecured bond yield was about 15%; the bond market is already pricing default, while the equity market is still pricing the RMB 132.5 billion order book.

Three conditions for reassessment, all required: **① free cash flow gap narrows for two consecutive quarters, with management giving a specific quarter for turning positive; ② debt duration extended beyond 5 years, or a successful new issue with a coupon below 10%; ③ at least one major customer contract converted to guaranteed payment.** Alternative: express the same view through NVIDIA, TSMC, the three HBM players, or Microsoft.

If a price must be attached to "buy," it can only be recalculated after the three structural conditions are met — not a number picked now. This is also why this report gives no target price range: for a company where equity is 26% of EV, any point valuation is false precision.

For different investor types, the conclusion is the same but framed differently: value investors — the name is screened out at the filter stage; growth investors — the growth is real but equity is a leverage tool, so buy upstream; traders — you can trade the volatility, but know you are betting on the credit window, not on fundamentals.
