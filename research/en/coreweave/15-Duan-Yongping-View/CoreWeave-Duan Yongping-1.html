Core view

Run Duan Yongping's six questions and every answer points the same way: a poor business that earns the spread of a scarcity period, most likely a thin-margin utility in ten years, understandable but not something I want to own, no price at which it is worth buying, and the biggest fear is the credit window closing. His conclusion would not be "wait for it to fall to X and buy" but "this is not my dish."

1. Is this a good business?

Not good. I'm talking about the business, not the demand. Good demand and a good business are two different things. VCDs had great demand back in the day—did a pile of companies actually make money? No.

Strip it back and CoreWeave does this: borrow money to buy machines, rent the machines out, collect the rent. The rent is decent—USD 3.6 billion a quarter. But look at the interest bill—USD 846 million. What it earns doesn't cover the interest, so it has to borrow more. A business that can only keep running by borrowing nonstop isn't a business—it's financing.

I don't like this model. What I like is a business where what you earn this year, you don't have to spend it all next year and still have a little left over. Apple is like that. Moutai is like that. This one is the reverse: it earns USD 3.6 billion and has to spend USD 9.4 billion on equipment.

II. Will it still be around in ten years?

It may still be around, but probably not in its current form. My guess is that this business ends up as the "IDC of compute" — everyone has to use it, no one has pricing power, and it earns hard-won money.

Think about it: in ten years, will Nvidia still dominate GPUs? Not necessarily. Will customers still pay a premium because "you can power up six months earlier than the next guy"? I doubt it. Once everyone has built roughly the same capacity, with power prices, land prices and data centers all public, customers will ask one question: Why should I pay your price?

By then it will still exist, but it will have become a heavy-asset, low-margin company surviving on scale. I generally don't buy companies like that, because their share price won't rise — it will only track interest rates.

III. Can I understand it?

Half of it I understand, half I don't. What I do understand is how it makes money: sign contracts, buy cards, connect power, collect rent. What I don't understand is what that card will be worth in three years.

If I don't understand it, I don't touch it. I stayed away from tech stocks back then not out of stupidity but because I couldn't tell who would win over ten years. Here it's trickier: it isn't that I don't know who wins — it's that I don't know what the cost structure looks like in three years. Depreciation is stated at 6 years; if it's really 3, the entire profit is fake, and that's not something I can judge in advance.

By my standards, it fails at this gate. Not that it's necessarily bad — I just can't judge it, and if I can't judge it, I don't do it. That's called knowing your circle.

IV. At what price would I buy?

Honestly, I can't put a price on it right now. A cheap price won't fix its problems.

Consider: a RMB 22 billion market cap against RMB 46.9 billion of debt — RMB 83.5 billion of enterprise value in total. A 71% decline sounds dramatic, but the debt hasn't shrunk by a cent. If it can't borrow next year, a 10 yuan share price won't help, because this company doesn't belong to shareholders — it belongs to the creditors.

So when would I start looking? Three conditions: first, it can survive without borrowing more; second, it can borrow for more than five years at under 10% interest; third, its customers will sign take-or-pay contracts. Meet all three and I'll work out a price. Fail any one and I won't look at 20 yuan either.

Five, what is there most to fear?

The biggest fear is not that AI demand rolls over—that is unlikely. It is that one morning the company announces "we have completed a USD X billion equity financing."

That would signal two things: the credit market has stopped lending, and shareholders have been diluted. Consider what it is doing simultaneously—convertible bonds, delayed draw term loans and a 35 million share follow-on offering. A company running three financing channels at once knows the window could close at any time.

The second fear is customer flight. Its top two customers account for 54% of revenue; Microsoft is already saying "selling to just one or two customers isn't a business" and has cancelled USD 3.2 billion in orders. If the customers leave, the RMB 132.5 billion order book is just paper.

Six: If you had to make one bet on AI compute, who would you back?

Bet on the shovel sellers, not the shovel buyers. Nvidia's cards sell themselves and the three HBM players are raising prices — they get paid; CoreWeave pays.

Some argue Nvidia now faces questions over circular financing and its CEO is embroiled in trouble, so the risk is high. Yes — but that is a company with pricing power running into trouble; CoreWeave is a company without pricing power running into trouble. Same trouble, but the former can weather it and the latter cannot. In business and in investing alike, first ask which side you stand on — the side getting paid, or the side paying.

That's all I have to say. I understand this company, but I don't want to own it. That's it.

Conclusion

Duan Yongping-style verdict: don't do it. Not because the stock will fall, but because it sits outside his circle of competence — not the business itself, which is easy enough to understand, but the cost structure three years out. And where a business's cost structure has to be guessed at, no price is low enough. His three conditions for revisiting: it can survive without borrowing; if it borrows, it can borrow for five years or more at under 10% interest; customers will sign "pay whether they use it or not." Meet all three, then talk price.
