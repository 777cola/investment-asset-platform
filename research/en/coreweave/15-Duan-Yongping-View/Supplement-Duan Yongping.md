# CoreWeave（CRWV）— Duan Yongping Supplement

- Research date：2026/11/20
- Price：39.51 USD
- Market cap：Approximately USD 22 billion

## Notes

This piece restates the judgment on CoreWeave in Duan Yongping's public style of expression (emphasizing "business model", "benfen", "circle of competence" and "not doing what you don't understand"); the views draw on the methodology in his public interviews and are not his own statements about this company. Financial figures are taken from the 2026Q3 earnings report.

## 1. Is this a good business?

Not good. I'm talking about the business, not the demand. **Good demand and a good business are two different things.** Back when VCDs were a thing, demand was great too—did a pile of companies actually make money? No.

What CoreWeave does, plainly put, is this: borrow money to buy machines, rent the machines out to others, and collect rent. The rent is decent—USD 3.6 billion a quarter. But look at how much interest it pays—USD 846 million. What it earns isn't enough to cover the interest, so it has to borrow more. **A business that can only keep running by borrowing nonstop isn't a business—it's financing.**

I don't like this model. What I like is a business where "what you earn this year, you don't have to spend it all next year and still have a little left over." Apple is like that. Moutai is like that. This one is the reverse: it earns USD 3.6 billion and has to spend USD 9.4 billion on equipment.

## II. Will it still be around in ten years?

It may still be around, but probably not in its current form. **My guess is that this business ends up as the "IDC of compute" — everyone has to use it, but no one has pricing power, and it earns hard-won money.**

Think about it: in ten years, will Nvidia still dominate GPUs? Not necessarily. In ten years, will customers still pay a premium because "you can power up six months earlier than the next guy"? I don't think so. Once everyone has built roughly the same capacity, with power prices, land prices and data centers all public, customers will ask one question: **Why should I pay your price?**

By then it will still exist, but it will have become a heavy-asset, low-margin company surviving on scale. I generally don't buy companies like that, because their share price won't rise — it will only track interest rates.

## III. Can I understand it?

Half of it I understand, half I don't. **What I understand is how it makes money: sign contracts, buy cards, connect power, collect rent. What I don't understand is what that card will be worth in three years.**

If I don't understand it, I don't touch it. Back then I stayed away from tech stocks not because I was stupid, but because I didn't know who would win in ten years. **Here it's trickier — it's not that I don't know who will win, it's that I don't know what a business's cost structure will look like in three years.** The depreciation period is stated as 6 years; if it's actually 3, then all of its profit is fake, and that's something I can't judge in advance.

So by my standards, it fails at this gate. **Not that it's necessarily bad — I just can't judge it, and if I can't judge it, I don't do it.** That's called knowing your circle.

## IV. At what price would I buy?

Honestly, I can't come up with a price right now. **Because a cheap price won't solve its problems.**

Look: market cap of RMB 22 billion, debt of RMB 46.9 billion — enterprise value of RMB 83.5 billion combined. A 71% drop sounds like a lot, but the money you owe hasn't shrunk by a cent. **If it can't borrow next year, a share price of 10 yuan won't help, because the company isn't mine — it belongs to the creditors.**

When will I start looking? Three conditions: **One, it can survive without borrowing more; two, it can borrow for more than five years at under 10% interest; three, its customers will sign contracts that pay whether or not they use the service.** If it meets all three, I'll work out a price. If not, I won't look even at 20 yuan.

## Five, what is there most to fear?

The biggest fear is not AI demand falling off—that is actually unlikely. **The biggest fear is that one morning it issues an announcement saying "we have completed a USD X billion equity financing."**

Because that would mean two things: the credit market has stopped lending, and shareholders have been diluted. Look at what it is doing simultaneously—convertible bonds, delayed draw term loans, and a 35 million share follow-on offering—**a company running three financing channels at once shows it knows the window could close at any time.**

The second fear is customer flight. Its top two customers account for 54% of revenue; Microsoft is already saying "selling to just one or two customers isn't a business" and has cancelled USD 3.2 billion in orders. **If the customers leave, the RMB 132.5 billion order book is just paper.**

## Six: If you had to make one bet on AI compute, who would you bet on?

Bet on the shovel sellers, not the shovel buyers. **Nvidia's cards sell themselves, and the three HBM players are raising prices — these are the ones getting paid; CoreWeave is the one paying.**

Some say Nvidia now faces questions over circular financing and its CEO is embroiled in trouble, so the risk is high. Yes — but that is a company with pricing power running into trouble; CoreWeave is a company without pricing power running into trouble. Same trouble, but the former can weather it and the latter cannot. **Business and investing alike: first look at which side you stand on — the side getting paid, or the side paying.**

That's all I have to say. I understand this company, but I don't want to own it. That's it.
