Core view

Munger read financial reports to "find the page management doesn't want you to see." Three numbers in this report warrant suspicion: the 62% adjusted EBITDA margin, the USD 132.5 billion backlog, and the 6-year GPU depreciation life. All three rest on assumptions, and every one of those assumptions cuts in management's favour.

The first suspicious number: a 62% EBITDA margin

Adjusted EBITDA of RMB 2.256 billion and a 62% margin — a figure that excludes depreciation, interest and share-based compensation, precisely the company's three largest expenses. Munger's test is blunt: "If the metric a company most wants to talk about happens to exclude its biggest costs, you already know the answer."

On a GAAP basis: operating margin 5.7%, net margin -19.4%. Same quarter, same business, three bases telling three stories. Munger would pick the ugliest one, because accounting defaults toward flattery and the ugliest basis sits closest to the truth.

Second suspicious figure: the RMB 132.5 billion backlog

Backlog rose from RMB 104 billion to RMB 132.5 billion, up RMB 28.5 billion in a single quarter. The reconciliation holds; there is no room for fabrication. But Munger would press on quality: how much of these contracts is take-or-pay, and how much is "maximum commitment, pay per actual usage"?

The 10-Q disclosures show that Meta's RMB 21 billion and OpenAI's RMB 6.5 billion both fall into the latter category. A "maximum commitment" is an order in optimistic times and zero in pessimistic times — customers can use less, defer, or shift load back in-house. Valuing the company on a backlog of 36x annual revenue assumes these contracts are 100% performed.

The third suspicious figure: 6-year depreciation

NVIDIA ships a new architecture every year: Hopper, then Blackwell, then Rubin. How far do rental rates for a card fall after a generational change? The company's own data offers a clue: rental rates for Ampere cards, now three generations old, were 30%–50% higher this quarter than in 2026Q2—seemingly good news, but the result of a supply shortage, and once the shortage ends, rental rates for older cards will fall off a cliff.

Munger's inversion: if the depreciation period were changed to 4 years, would the company still make money? A rough calculation: annual depreciation rises by about 50%, and GAAP operating profit swings from RMB 207 million to negative. Its earnings, in other words, rest entirely on an accounting assumption. A company whose "profit depends on assumptions" falls, on his checklist, into the hardest category to value.

Conclusion

Munger-style conclusion: all three numbers flatter the picture, and each props up the others. High EBITDA makes interest coverage look adequate, a large backlog makes the future look certain, and long depreciation schedules make current profits look strong. If reality overturns any one of the three assumptions, the other two collapse with it. His advice would be: don't try to value a company like this—skip it outright.
