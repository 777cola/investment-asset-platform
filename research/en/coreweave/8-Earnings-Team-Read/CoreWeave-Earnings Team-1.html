Core view

Duan Yongping reads financial statements by turning to the cash flow statement first, then the income statement. This report teaches the opposite lesson: operating cash flow of RMB 1.975 billion looks impressive until you open it up and find it is all customer prepayments; investing cash flow shows an outflow of RMB 9.441 billion; financing cash flow brings in over RMB 10 billion. Read the three statements together and they say only one thing—it is using financing money to build assets and prepayment money to fund working capital.

First, look at where the money comes from and where it goes

Line up the three cash flow sections and the picture is clear: operating +RMB 1.975 billion, of which RMB 2.127 billion is customer prepayments; investing -RMB 9.441 billion, almost entirely GPU purchases and data center buildout; and financing, a large net inflow from bond issuance, convertible notes and a follow-on offering. Cash generated from operations covers less than a third of investing, and financing fills the entire gap.

This is not an accounting-technique problem; it is a problem of business quality. Duan Yongping's standard is simple: a good business's operating cash flow should cover capital expenditure, and whatever is left belongs to shareholders. BBK, Apple and Moutai all meet this test. CoreWeave meets none of it—its capital expenditure is 4.8 times operating cash flow.

Depreciation lives are a stroke of management's pen

CoreWeave depreciates GPUs over roughly 6 years, and that assumption drives the entire shape of the income statement. Rough math: for every year the depreciation life is shortened, annual depreciation rises about 20% and operating profit falls by a fifth. GAAP operating profit this quarter was 207 million; shorten the life by two years and it goes to zero.

Duan Yongping would ask a very simple question: if this business is really that good, why not use a slightly more conservative depreciation life? The answer is usually that it wouldn't be profitable if it did. That sentence alone is a verdict on the business.

How to read the RMB 132.5 billion order

Orders were the standout number this quarter, but they are not cash. Duan Yongping's view on orders has always been consistent: "A contract is paper; money received is the business." The RMB 132.5 billion figure is the maximum committed amount, and customers pay for actual usage; if they use less, the company does not receive the full amount, yet the racks, power and debt have already been provisioned for full capacity.

The right way to look at orders is through their conversion into cash flow: new orders this quarter were RMB 28.5 billion, against new capex of RMB 9.441 billion (cash) to RMB 14.5 billion (accrual) — every RMB 1 of new orders requires RMB 0.33–0.51 of equipment investment. Unless this ratio improves over time, the more orders there are, the more the company has to borrow.

Conclusion

Duan Yongping-style conclusion: this is a business stacked up with other people's money, not one that generates money on its own. Put the three statements side by side: operating cash flow covers less than one-third of capex, and debt and customer prepayments fund the gap. He would probably say: "I understand it, but I don't want to own it. Check back when it can expand without borrowing."
