One earnings report, three ways to read it

Core view

This article merges four close readings into one more readable piece: take the same 2026Q3 earnings report — start with the income statement and you get "rapid growth"; start with the cash flow statement and you get "a cash-burning machine"; start with the balance sheet and you get "a leveraged bet". All three readings hold, because the numbers themselves do not change; only the order in which you read them does.

First reading: what the income statement says

CoreWeave's Q3 was genuinely impressive at the operating level. Revenue of USD 3.661 billion came in +168% YoY and +42% QoQ, above the top end of the company's own guidance of USD 3.45–3.6 billion. Adjusted operating income of USD 379 million was a wide beat against guidance of USD 200–260 million, and GAAP operating income of USD 207 million was positive for the first time since listing. Contract backlog rose from USD 104 billion to USD 132.5 billion, an increase of USD 28.5 billion in a single quarter.

Management also stressed pricing power on the call: a roughly 25% price increase in July, a further 10% at quarter-end, and rents for older-generation Ampere and Hopper cards running 30%–50% above Q2 levels. The ability to raise prices shows this is a seller's market. Read this far and the conclusion would be "strong demand, improving operations, worth watching."

The second reading: what the cash flow statement tells us

Read two pages further into the statements and the story changes. Operating cash flow was USD 1.975 billion, which looks healthy—except that USD 2.127 billion of it came from an increase in customer prepayments, prepayments exceeding total operating cash flow. Strip that out and day-to-day operations were a net outflow.

On the investing side, cash paid for fixed assets was USD 9.441 billion (capex on an accrual basis was USD 14.5 billion). Free cash flow was -USD 7.466 billion, and the gap is widening: Q1 -USD 4.711 billion, Q2 -USD 5.743 billion, Q3 -USD 7.466 billion. Three quarters burned USD 17.9 billion, more than its annual revenue.

Third reading: what the balance sheet tells us

The most critical page of this earnings report sits further back. Total debt stands at USD 46.873 billion at an average cost of 7.9%, of which USD 41.6 billion is recourse. About USD 13.4 billion matures over the next two years against roughly USD 5.5 billion of cash on hand—a USD 7.9 billion gap that can only be closed through refinancing.

Interest has already consumed all operating profit: net interest of USD 846 million this quarter is 4.1x GAAP operating profit of USD 207 million and 2.2x adjusted operating profit of USD 379 million. Q4 guidance pushes interest to USD 1.05–1.15 billion. When a company pays more than a quarter of its revenue to creditors, its income statement structurally does not belong to shareholders.

Why did the stock fall 12% on November 20?

Global tech sold off sharply that day: the Nasdaq fell 5.28%, the Philadelphia Semiconductor Index 10.73% and Nvidia 14%. CoreWeave dropped 12.02% to USD 39.51, breaking below its March 2025 IPO price of USD 40 and now down 71.4% from its 52-week high of USD 137.98.

On the surface this looks like systemic risk, but the real signal sits in an inconspicuous place: its unsecured bond yield rose to around 15% in mid-November, entering distressed territory. Bond investors are already pricing the probability of default; equity investors are still pricing the RMB 132.5 billion order book. One company, two markets, two verdicts. Historically, the bond market has usually been right first.

So, is a 71% drop an opportunity?

No. Assessing whether it is cheap requires enterprise value, not market cap. Market cap is USD 22 billion; adding net debt (including leases) of about USD 61 billion gives enterprise value of about USD 83.5 billion. A 71% decline in market cap maps to a far smaller decline in enterprise value—most of the fall sits in the equity layer, and equity was only 26% of enterprise value to begin with.

Put differently, the share price fell from 138 to 39.5, yet the company's debt obligations did not fall by a single cent. What fell was shareholders' claim on future cash flows, not the company's obligation to creditors. That is the structure of leverage: good news is discounted, bad news is amplified.

Under what circumstances is it worth another look

Three conditions, all necessary.First, the free cash flow gap narrows for two consecutive quarters and management specifies the quarter it turns positive—not "it will turn positive in the future," but which year and which quarter.Second, extend debt duration: replace the RMB 13.4 billion maturing within 2 years with debt due in 5 years or more, or successfully issue new bonds with unsecured yields below 10%.Third, improve the quality of customer contracts: at least one major customer changes "maximum commitment amount" to "minimum payment guarantee."

None of these three can be solved by "better AI demand." All are capital structure issues, and capital structure is the company's biggest problem right now.

Conclusion

Conclusion: avoid. This earnings report does not require a view on whether AI keeps growing—USD 132.5 billion in orders has already proven demand. What it does require is a view on whether a company burning USD 7.5 billion a quarter, with interest of USD 846 million exceeding operating profit and debt of USD 46.87 billion, can turn free cash flow positive before the credit markets shut. Until that answer is clear, USD 39.51 is not a low—it is just a price.
