Management: execution A, capital allocation D — CoreWeave

Three founders, three critical choices
Research date 2026/11/20  ·  Price $39.51 (-12.02%)  ·  Market cap Approximately USD 22 billion  ·  P/E (TTM) — (GAAP loss, PE not meaningful)
← Back to the hub

Core view

Michael Intrator, Brian Venturo and Brannin McBee began in 2017 with an Ethereum mining farm; by 2026 they had 4.6GW of contracted power and quarterly revenue of USD 3.661 billion. Delivery execution sets the industry benchmark and deserves full credit. But three key capital-allocation decisions grew more aggressive each time.

Execution: from mine to 4.6GW

They bought cards to mine Ethereum in 2017; when the coin price collapsed in 2018–2019, they converted the mining rigs into a GPU cloud — that pivot is the company's true starting point. The March 2025 IPO priced at USD 40, raising about USD 1.5 billion, with Nvidia subscribing USD 250 million. By 2026Q3 contracted power reached 4.6GW against 1.7GW of operating capacity, with 900MW of contracts added and 200MW brought online in a single quarter.

That pace ranks first among neoclouds and rests on three things: a priority supply relationship with Nvidia, power and campuses locked up in advance, and a willingness to sign long-term contracts with customers. The first two are capability; the third is a gambler's instinct. Doubling operating capacity in 18 months is genuine execution.

Capital allocation: the direction of three choices

First, the March 2025 IPO. Listing at peak AI sentiment secured the cheapest equity capital, with Nvidia anchoring the investment—the call was right. Second, the proposed all-stock acquisition of Core Scientific for about USD 9 billion in 2025 (to gain power and ready-built data centers), rejected by the target's shareholders in October. Right direction, expensive price; the rejection also exposed disagreement between the two sides on valuation.

Third, opening three financing channels simultaneously in 2026: USD 4.2 billion convertible notes, USD 2.6 billion delayed-draw term loan, a plan to issue 35 million shares, and staking the next three months' funding on about USD 9 billion of customer prepayments. Using three instruments at once is defensive financing—it signals management sees the window closing.

Governance: three areas that warrant a discount

Dual-class shares: The founding team controls voting power through Class B shares far exceeding its economic interest, leaving outside shareholders with almost no veto power over equity issuance, M&A or debt expansion. For a company at D/E 16.5, this governance discount should be larger. The related-party triangle: Nvidia is supplier, shareholder and the backstop party under scrutiny; whether related-party disclosures are adequate requires reading the notes yourself.

Metric selection: The most frequent terms in filings and earnings calls are backlog and adjusted EBITDA, both of which strip out the company's largest costs (depreciation, interest). Not a violation, but selective narrative — yet for investors, the two excluded items are precisely what matter most.

Succession: an underpriced single-point risk

The three founders still run the company with a clear division of labour — CEO on strategy and external affairs, CSO on capital, CDO on delivery — and there is no public succession plan. That is not unusual at a USD 3 billion revenue scale, but the company's survival depends heavily on financing capacity, which in turn rests partly on the CEO's personal relationships in the credit market.

Comparison: Microsoft and Oracle fund themselves institutionally (ratings, bond market, banking syndicates), so whoever becomes CEO can still borrow. Part of CoreWeave's financing is "founder credit," and that is the first piece to disappear in a crisis.

A detail worth watching in management

On the call, management spent more than 5x as long on backlog as on free cash flow. That is not deception; it is a allocation of attention—what a company repeatedly emphasises usually signals where it most wants others to look. A genuinely healthy company will proactively discuss its ugliest numbers.

Contrast a truly candid management team: they will state up front in the earnings report, "Our free cash flow is negative, here is why, and here is when it will improve." CoreWeave's disclosure does contain this sentence, but it sits far later, behind 16 pages of backlog and "adjusted" metrics. Read earnings reports to the very end.

Conclusion

Management conclusion: the capability deserves a premium; the capital structure and governance do not. Three founders turned a mining site into a 4.6GW platform, and on delivery execution they earn an A. But three capital allocations, each more aggressive than the last, alongside dual-class shares, a related-party triangle and selective disclosure, turn the "management premium" into a "governance discount." Impact on the decision: no valuation premium, and no credit for management.
