Industry and competition: the bottleneck shifts from chips to power, squeezing out intermediaries — CoreWeave

Profit redistribution along one supply chain

Core view

Through 2024–2025 the industry was short of GPUs: whoever secured the cards made money, and neoclouds enjoyed scarcity pricing as a result. In 2026 the shortage is power and grid interconnection: of CoreWeave's 4.6GW of contracted power capacity, only 1.7GW is in operation, with 2.9GW stuck on transformers, gas turbines and permitting. As the bottleneck shifts, bargaining power moves from "those who have the cards" to "those who have the power" — and the latter are the hyperscalers.

Supply and demand curves: supply is catching up

Demand is not in question: the four major North American cloud providers sharply raised 2026 capex, with Microsoft spending 45.2 billion in a single quarter and about 170 billion for the full year; NVIDIA FY27Q3 revenue was 115.8 billion, up +103% YoY. But supply is expanding in tandem—every neocloud is building, and every hyperscaler is building its own.

To judge whether supply is catching up, watch one indicator: rents for older-generation cards. This quarter, Ampere and Hopper rents ran 30%–50% above the second quarter. That looks like strong demand but is in fact a shortage premium—"not enough new cards, so customers are forced onto old ones." A shortage premium is the most fragile pricing there is—it is negative-feedback: the higher the price, the more incentive customers have to build their own and wait for new cards.

Competitive landscape: four types of players, two outcomes

The first category is NVIDIA: it sells the shovels, runs gross margins above 70% and holds the strongest pricing power when supply is tight. The second is hyperscalers: a 4%–5% cost of capital, their own customers and their own power grids make them the endgame winners. The third is neoclouds (CoreWeave, Nebius, Crusoe, IREN): fast delivery, but no pricing power. The fourth is sovereign clouds and regional operators, surviving on subsidies and electricity prices.

Two outcomes: hyperscalers become compute utilities (stable thin margins, high valuations), while neoclouds become acquisition targets or are cleared out. Every asset-heavy industry in history has walked this path — IDC, fiber, panels, airlines. CoreWeave sits at the "pre-shakeout" stage, carrying USD 46.9 billion of debt.

Circular financing: the market discounts the authenticity of demand

NVIDIA invests in customers → customers buy cards → NVIDIA backstops idle capacity. That chain came under repeated scrutiny in 2026. The issue is not fraud, but that "revenue growth" now includes "capital provided by the seller." If part of the buyer's money comes from the seller, how much of demand is end-market?

The SEC investigation into NVIDIA's accounting treatment on November 17 and Jensen Huang's resignation as CEO forced the issue into the open. The read-across to CoreWeave is twofold: lower supply priority and reduced credibility of the backstop commitment. Its supply-chain advantage—the only passing item in its moat—rests precisely on this chain now under investigation.

Power prices and grid connection, not GPUs, are the key competitive variables

Over the next 24 months, what decides the fate of neoclouds is not whether they can secure GPUs but whether they can secure power. Grid interconnection queues for a GW-scale campus run 5–7 years in parts of the US, while hyperscalers can jump the queue through "investment + tax commitments + self-built power plants."

CoreWeave's response has been to contract 4.6GW of power (including self-supplied gas turbines). The direction is right, but it keeps paying for capacity that is not yet operational. If power prices rise and interconnection slips, that 2.9GW turns from "reserve" into "burden"—and that is exactly what is unfolding in the second half of 2026, with transformer lead times of 2–3 years.

A testable prediction

Absent a cut to 2027 Q1 capex guidance from RMB 37–39 billion, free cash flow breakeven slips beyond 2028, by which point refinancing needs would exceed RMB 20 billion. This forecast can be verified against public disclosures; no insider information is required.

The corollary: if 2027 capex stays above RMB 37 billion, debt will exceed RMB 60 billion by end-2027, at which point annualised interest expense tops RMB 4.5 billion — more than full-year 2026 adjusted operating profit. At that stage the valuation debate becomes moot; only restructuring or dilution remain.

Do the math from the customer's perspective

Take an AI lab that wants 1GW of compute by 2027. Build it yourself: USD 30 million per watt × 1,000MW = USD 30 billion, plus a 3–5 year power-up cycle. Rent from CoreWeave: ready-made 1GW costs about USD 860 million a year, or USD 4.3 billion over 5 years.

Near term, renting is far cheaper, and that is where CoreWeave's business comes from. But after five years the self-build cost is fully amortised, while the rent keeps being paid. The customer's real decision is therefore to rent first to save time while starting to build in parallel—exactly what Microsoft and Meta are doing now. The middleman's window is essentially the construction period of the customer's own build.

Conclusion

Sector view: real demand, mid-cycle, intermediaries losing ground. The bottleneck has shifted from chips to power, and bargaining power from neoclouds to hyperscalers and power-equipment suppliers. Circular financing is under scrutiny, which discounts the credibility of "demand growth." CoreWeave is the most fragile link in this setup: sub-scale versus hyperscalers, the highest cost of capital, and customers now pulling outsourcing back in-house. Own the sector through upstream or hyperscaler exposure.
