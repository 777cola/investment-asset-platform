The nature of the business: the economics of a contractor — CoreWeave

How much revenue can 1 MW of power generate, and how much can be borrowed against it?

Core view

CoreWeave's business boils down to one model: each megawatt of compute = about USD 30 million of equipment capex + about USD 7 million of annual rental revenue + about USD 2.5 million of annual interest. What matters here is not revenue but the "payback period on equipment capex" and the "rigidity of interest". CoreWeave is essentially a contractor: win the job, front the capital, hire the equipment, deliver, get paid.

Unit economics: run the numbers per megawatt

Back-of-envelope on 2026Q3: revenue of RMB 3.661 billion ÷ 1.7GW in operation ≈ USD 21.5 million per MW per quarter, or roughly USD 86 million per MW annualised. Equipment capex at the industry order of magnitude of USD 30 million/MW implies a payback of about 3.5 years, excluding interest and O&M. Looks good — a 3.5-year payback on equipment that lasts 6 years.

Add the cost of capital and the picture changes: USD 30 million/MW at 7.9% interest carries USD 2.37 million of annual interest, 2.75x the USD 86 million of annual revenue. In other words, on a single-megawatt basis, rent does not even cover interest, let alone principal. Hence net interest of RMB 846 million at 2.2x adjusted operating profit — not financial engineering, but a function of unit economics.

Why must it keep expanding?

Because stopping means an immediate loss. A static calculation: operate only the existing 1.7GW, build nothing new. Equipment investment of about USD 51 billion (at USD 30 million/MW), depreciated over 6 years, or 8.5 billion a year; annualized revenue of about USD 14.6 billion, adjusted EBITDA at 62% of about 9.1 billion; annualized net interest of about 3.4 billion. Result: 9.1 − 8.5 − 3.4 = -USD 2.8 billion.

The math points to something uncomfortable: at its current scale, the business cannot yet cover the two rigid costs of depreciation and interest. Only two paths turn it positive—rents keep rising (a bet on shortage), or scale keeps expanding (which requires more debt). And each additional megawatt of expansion requires another USD 30 million of investment and another USD 24 million of debt. Growth is not a choice; it is a necessary condition for keeping the financing flywheel spinning.

That also explains why capex guidance is USD 37–39 billion rather than set "according to cash flow capacity". Once expansion stops, the financials will immediately expose the fact that depreciation + interest > EBITDA, and the financing window will shut the same day. The flywheel stops, and the company stops.

Three red lines of the contractor model

First, customers must not run. The top two customers account for 54%, and Microsoft is pulling back on outsourcing. Second, money must not be expensive. A 7.9% average cost versus Microsoft's 4.85% is a structural disadvantage in a thin-margin industry, and new debt now costs 15%. Third, equipment must not become obsolete too fast. Nvidia refreshes every year, and the 6-year depreciation assumption is the entire source of its profit.

Three red lines, and it is treading on the edge of all of them at once. And the subcontractor's fate is this: the more work it takes on, the more capital it fronts, and the more it cannot do without the client. That is why the bigger its backlog, the less reassured I am — orders are the client's promise, but debt is its own obligation.

A counterexample: why it has held up until now

Because customers need it. OpenAI's training clusters and Meta's inference peaks have no second supplier that can match its delivery speed near term. That explains how it signed RMB 132.5 billion in orders, and why its debt finds buyers—creditors believe "customers won't let it collapse immediately." But "won't collapse immediately" and "shareholders can make money" are two different things.

The reverse also deserves acknowledgment: as long as it can still borrow, the flywheel keeps spinning and revenue keeps rising. So the call that "it will blow up imminently" is wrong—the trouble need not arrive in the winter of 2026; it could drag into some quarter of 2027. For a target with "certain direction, uncertain timing," the best handling is not to participate, rather than to short.

Conclusion

Business essence: a highly leveraged compute contractor. On unit economics, rent does not cover interest. Structurally, new financing has to fund the depreciation and interest on old projects. Positionally, it is squeezed by both upstream (Nvidia) and downstream (hyperscalers). This is not "the early stage of a growth stock" but "the highly leveraged middle stage of a heavy-asset industry."
