Moat dissection: a time window that closes — CoreWeave

Six scores, one passing grade

Core view

A moat is "something others cannot take away." Tested against that standard, CoreWeave clears the bar on only one count: supply chain (6/10), and even that rests on Nvidia's strategic will rather than any capability of CoreWeave's own. The other five—brand 2, cost 3, network effects 1, switching costs 1, resources 4—all fall below the passing line.

First distinguish between the "time window" and the "structural moat"

The time window is "I got there before you"; the structural moat is "you can't take it even once you arrive." The former disappears as competitors arrive; the latter thickens as scale expands. CoreWeave's advantages are almost entirely the former: getting GB300 9 months early, powering up 6 months early, and accumulating 4.6GW of power capacity two years ahead.

The test is simple: ask "if a competitor spent twice the money, could they catch up within 18 months?" For CoreWeave, the answer is yes. Nebius and Crusoe built campuses of the same specification within the same time window; Microsoft's annual capex of RMB 170 billion — shift just 5% of it and that equals CoreWeave's entire scale. What can be caught up to is not a moat.

Why "compute" is inherently not a good business

Three attributes decide it. Homogeneity: with identical GB300 racks, customers see no differentiation and compete on price, delivery and SLA alone. Substitutability: workloads migrate within weeks, with no data lock-in and no ecosystem lock-in. Asset-heavy: every 1 yuan of revenue requires 2.58 yuan of equipment, and exit costs are extremely high.

Stack the three and the outcome is "price set by marginal supply, profit set by cost of capital." That is why the long-lived players in this industry are Microsoft, Amazon and Google — they have the lowest cost of capital; not CoreWeave — it has the highest.

The only real moat: 4.6GW of power, but it will be bought up

Its hardest asset is the 4.6GW of contracted power, since grid-connection quotas are administratively allocated, scarce and exclusive. This item scores 4, the second highest in the table. But that exclusivity lasts only for the contract term, and the quota can be reallocated to a higher bidder (and whose investment would local governments rather have?).

More critically, only 1.7GW is in operation. The remaining 2.9GW has paid reservation fees but generates no revenue, with grid-connection fees, capacity fees and equipment prepayments all consuming cash. A resource is a moat when scarce and a burden when in surplus—and the bottleneck is shifting from chips to power, where the contenders far outnumber those chasing chips.

Another possible moat: do customers want it to die?

One atypical "moat" merits discussion: if CoreWeave collapses, will its customers feel the pain? Meta's USD 21 billion and OpenAI's USD 6.5 billion contracts make the three, near term, a community of shared interest—cut the compute and model training halts.

That logic sounds like "too big to fail," but it has a fatal flaw: customers can build their own capacity, just slowly; creditors cannot extend forbearance, they simply refuse to. So when crisis hits, customers will wait for a restructuring—contracts voided, terms renegotiated—rather than rescue it. Telecom equipment in 2000 and airlines in 2008 bore this out: customers do not pay their suppliers' debts.

A judgment beyond the score

Moat scoring is a tool; judgment is human. My judgment: the moat can hold for 12 months, but the debt takes 24 months to repay. The two time scales do not match, and that is the whole problem.

Two dates make this 12-month vs 24-month mismatch verifiable: the first debt maturity in March 2027, and the capex guidance issued in January–February 2027. If guidance is not cut and the refinancing coupon exceeds 12%, the mismatch becomes a crisis. Both dates are public; no guessing required.

If there is no moat, how has it survived to this day?

It works only because three conditions hold at once: not enough cards, no power, and customers who cannot wait. In 2026 the first is easing as NVIDIA capacity comes online, the second has become the new bottleneck (2–3 year lead times on transformers), and customers are answering the third with "build our own plus squeeze prices." All three expire at the same time.

That is also why its moat cannot be validated by waiting another two years — in two years all three conditions will turn against it. The absence of a moat is not a call on the present but on the endgame: in the compute business, the endgame belongs to whoever has the lowest cost of capital.

Conclusion

Moat verdict: 17/60, no structural barrier. What CoreWeave has is a 9–12 month window and a power quota that a higher bidder will simply buy away. Such companies earn excess profit in an upcycle on scarcity pricing, and are the first to be squeezed in a downcycle, with no pricing power and high leverage. Investment implication: there is no "wait for the moat to be built" path, because the nature of the compute business dictates that no moat can be built.
