## Opening: three numbers

On November 20, CoreWeave fell 12% to close at USD 39.51. That is below its March 2025 IPO price of USD 40 and down 71.4% from its 52-week high of USD 137.98. On the same day, the Nasdaq fell 5.28% and Nvidia fell 14%.

Many people's first reaction is "it has fallen too much — is this an opportunity?" Here are three numbers first; judge for yourself after reading them.

**USD 3.661 billion** — third-quarter revenue, up 168% year on year and 42% quarter on quarter, above the company's own upper guidance. That is one of the fastest growth rates in the US market this year.

**USD 846 million** — net interest expense in the same quarter. Adjusted operating profit was USD 379 million, and interest expense was 2.2 times that.

**USD -7.466 billion** — free cash flow in the same quarter. In other words, it earned USD 3.6 billion while posting a net outflow of USD 7.5 billion in the quarter.

## Part One: What keeps it alive

The business logic is not complicated: sign 5-year contracts with customers like Meta and OpenAI, take those contracts to banks and bond markets to borrow, buy Nvidia chips, pay for power, build data centers, then collect rent monthly.

The problem lies in the "spend first, collect later" rhythm. Cash capital expenditure this quarter was USD 9.441 billion, 2.58 times revenue. The more revenue, the more chips it must buy; the more chips it must buy, the more money it must borrow. It now carries total debt of USD 46.873 billion at an average cost of 7.9%, with USD 13.4 billion due over the next two years, against USD 5.5 billion of cash on hand.

So you see a strange phenomenon: revenue is flying, cash is leaking. **Growth is not organic — it is bought with borrowed money.**

## Part Two: The RMB 132.5 billion order is real, but it is only on paper

Its backlog rose from RMB 104.0 billion to RMB 132.5 billion in one quarter, a single-quarter increase of RMB 28.5 billion. The number is real, and the cross-checks hold up.

But orders and cash are two different things. Most of these contracts are "maximum commitment" agreements — customers pay for actual usage, not "pay whether they use it or not." If customers use less, the company does not collect the full amount, yet the racks, power and debt have all been provisioned for full capacity.

Run the conversion rate and it becomes clear: single-quarter revenue of RMB 3.661 billion ÷ orders of RMB 132.5 billion = 2.8%. If these contracts all run five years, the annualized conversion rate should be around 20%. Actual is only 11%, indicating a large chunk of the backlog consists of projects not yet powered up or started. **The orders are real, but their time horizon is far longer than the interest bill.**

## Part Three: Why a 71% drop still leaves it expensive

This is where most people get it wrong. Cheapness is about enterprise value, not market cap.

Its market cap is now USD 22 billion. But adding debt and lease liabilities, enterprise value is USD 83.5 billion. In other words, **equity is only 26% of this company's enterprise value; the remaining 74% belongs to creditors.**

That structure determines one thing: a 10% fall in enterprise value means a 38% fall in equity. So the stock can double on the way up, and halve twice over on the way down.

The more direct price signal sits in the bond market: its unsecured notes issued in June with a 9.625% coupon were yielding around 15% by mid-November, in distressed territory. Over the same period, AAA borrowers like Microsoft fund at under 5%. **Same industry, same business, and the market charges the two companies a threefold difference to borrow money — that is the answer.**

## Part Four: Why those "positive catalysts" do not matter

The Q3 report does contain good news: GAAP operating profit of USD 207 million, positive for the first time since listing; a 25% price hike in July and a further 10% at quarter-end, leaving rental rates on older-generation cards 30%–50% higher than in Q2.

But neither changes one fact: **its interest expense exceeds its operating profit.** Q4 guidance puts interest at USD 1.05–1.15 billion against adjusted operating profit of roughly USD 400–500 million. Even doubling margins again would not cover the interest.

The same goes for pricing. The ability to raise prices shows cards are scarce now; that scarcity will ease as Nvidia releases capacity and Microsoft and Amazon bring their own builds online. **Pricing power here is a gift from the cycle, not a capability.**

## Closing: what signals I will watch

I would not recommend buying, nor would I recommend shorting. The most appropriate stance for a company like this is "put it on the watchlist and wait for conditions to change." Three conditions, all of which must be met:

- The free cash flow gap narrows for two consecutive quarters, and management can say "which year and which quarter it turns positive"
- Debt duration extends: the RMB 13.4 billion maturing within two years is swapped into 5-year-plus maturities, or new bonds are issued at a coupon below 10%
- At least one major customer changes its contract from a "maximum commitment amount" to "guaranteed minimum payment"

None of these three things can be solved by "better AI demand." They are all about the capital structure.

So my conclusion is simple: **a 71% drop is not a reason to buy. What fell is shareholders' claim on the future, while the money the company owes creditors is not a cent less.**
