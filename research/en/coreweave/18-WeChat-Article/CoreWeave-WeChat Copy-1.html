Opening: three numbers

CoreWeave fell 12% on November 20 to close at USD 39.51, below its March 2025 IPO price of USD 40 and down 71.4% from its 52-week high of USD 137.98. The Nasdaq fell 5.28% and Nvidia 14% the same day.

The reflex reaction is that the stock has fallen too far and must be an opportunity. Before judging, consider three numbers.

USD 3.661 billion — third-quarter revenue, up 168% YoY and 42% QoQ, above the top end of company guidance. That ranks among the fastest growth rates in the US market this year.

USD 846 million — net interest expense in the same quarter, 2.2x adjusted operating profit of USD 379 million.

USD -7.466 billion — free cash flow in the same quarter. It earned USD 3.6 billion and still posted a net outflow of USD 7.5 billion.

Part One: What keeps it alive

The business logic is simple: sign 5-year contracts with customers such as Meta and OpenAI, pledge those contracts to banks and bond markets to raise debt, buy Nvidia chips, pay for power, build data centers, then collect rent monthly.

The problem is the "spend first, collect later" rhythm. Cash capex this quarter was USD 9.441 billion, 2.58 times revenue. More revenue means more chips to buy, and more chips to buy means more money to borrow. Total debt now stands at USD 46.873 billion at an average cost of 7.9%, with USD 13.4 billion due over the next two years against USD 5.5 billion of cash on hand.

Hence a strange phenomenon: revenue is flying, cash is leaking. Growth is not organic — it is bought with borrowed money.

Part Two: The RMB 132.5 billion order is real, but only on paper

Backlog rose from RMB 104.0 billion to RMB 132.5 billion in a single quarter, an increase of RMB 28.5 billion. The number is real, and the cross-checks hold up.

But orders and cash are two different things. Most of these contracts are "maximum commitment" agreements: customers pay for actual usage, not "pay whether they use it or not." If customers use less, the company does not collect the full amount, yet the racks, power and debt have all been provisioned for full capacity.

The conversion rate makes this clear: single-quarter revenue of RMB 3.661 billion ÷ orders of RMB 132.5 billion = 2.8%. If these contracts all run five years, the annualised conversion rate should be around 20%. Actual is only 11%, indicating a large chunk of the backlog consists of projects not yet powered up or started. The orders are real, but their time horizon is far longer than the interest bill.

Part Three: Why a 71% drop still leaves it expensive

This is where most people get it wrong: cheapness is about enterprise value, not market cap.

Market cap is now USD 22 billion, but add debt and lease liabilities and enterprise value reaches USD 83.5 billion. In other words, equity accounts for only 26% of enterprise value; the remaining 74% belongs to creditors.

That structure cuts one way: a 10% fall in enterprise value wipes 38% off equity. The stock can double on the way up and halve twice over on the way down.

The cleaner price signal sits in the bond market. Its unsecured notes, issued in June with a 9.625% coupon, were yielding around 15% by mid-November — distressed territory. Over the same period, AAA borrowers like Microsoft fund at under 5%. Same industry, same business, and the market charges the two a threefold difference to borrow money — that is the answer.

Part Four: Why those "positive catalysts" do not matter

The Q3 report does contain good news: GAAP operating profit of USD 207 million, positive for the first time since listing, and two price hikes—25% in July and a further 10% at quarter-end—that leave rental rates on older-generation cards 30%–50% higher than in Q2.

Neither changes one fact: interest expense exceeds operating profit. Q4 guidance puts interest at USD 1.05–1.15 billion against adjusted operating profit of roughly USD 400–500 million. Even another doubling of margins would not cover it.

Pricing tells the same story. The ability to raise prices shows cards are scarce now, but that scarcity will ease as Nvidia releases capacity and Microsoft and Amazon bring their own builds online. Pricing power here is a gift from the cycle, not a capability.

Closing: what signals I will watch

I would neither buy nor short. For a company like this, the right stance is to put it on the watchlist and wait for conditions to change. Three conditions, all of which must be met:

  • The free cash flow gap narrows for two consecutive quarters, and management can say which year and which quarter it turns positive
  • Debt duration extends: the RMB 13.4 billion maturing within two years is swapped into 5-year-plus maturities, or new bonds are issued at a coupon below 10%
  • At least one major customer changes its contract from a "maximum commitment amount" to "guaranteed minimum payment"

None of these three can be solved by "better AI demand." They are all about the capital structure.

So my conclusion is simple: a 71% drop is not a reason to buy. What fell is shareholders' claim on the future, while the money the company owes creditors is not a cent less.

Conclusion

Conclusion: Revenue is up 168% YoY and the order book stands at USD 132.5 billion, yet the stock is down 71% and remains uninvestable: equity is just 26% of enterprise value, interest expense is 2.2x operating profit, and the company burned USD 7.5 billion of net cash in a single quarter. Demand was never the problem; the capital structure is.
