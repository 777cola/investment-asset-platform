Core thesis

The four masters use different frameworks but reach the same conclusion. Duan Yongping calls it good demand, bad business — users can switch away with a single phone call; Buffett flips through the balance sheet: RMB 46.9 billion of debt against RMB 5.5 billion of cash, and the orders no longer matter; Munger jumps to the worst case — once the credit window shuts, it goes to zero, a structural flaw of leverage; Li Lu concedes AI is real, but demand rising tenfold in the railway era did not mean railway shareholders made money. Three-scenario valuation of USD 12 / 43 / 97, with the neutral case only 9% above the current price and the bear case a 70% loss — the risk-reward does not hold up.

Duan Yongping: This is good demand, not a good business

Duan Yongping draws a very clear distinction: good demand does not equal a good business. He bought Apple because users cannot switch away from iOS, and Moutai because they cannot switch away from the taste — "a moat is what others cannot take from you." CoreWeave's customers are indeed scrambling for compute, but what they are scrambling for is "there is no other choice right now," not "only CoreWeave can do it." Once Nvidia's capacity is released and the hyperscalers finish building their own, rental rates will fall immediately, while its debt and depreciation are rigid.

He would also ask the most basic question: will this business still exist in ten years? GPU cloud has no customer stickiness, no switching costs, no brand premium. For a customer to switch away, all it takes is one phone call, a new contract, and migrating the workload next door to Nebius or its own data center. He does do this kind of business — but only when it is "cheap enough that you don't need to judge the future," not at a market cap of USD 22 billion and net debt of USD 61 billion.

Another line from Duan Yongping cuts deeper: "If the business model is bad, no price is cheap enough." His assessment now would be: I understand it, but I don't want to own it.

Buffett: Read the balance sheet first; the income statement can wait

When Buffett looks at a company like this, the first thing he flips to is the balance sheet: total debt of RMB 46.87 billion, of which RMB 41.6 billion is recourse debt, average cost 7.9%, and about RMB 13.4 billion maturing over the next two years. Then he looks at cash: about RMB 5.5 billion. Put those two numbers together and any talk of an "order book of RMB 132.5 billion" loses its weight — orders are future, debt is present, and creditors rank ahead of shareholders.

He would also point to an accounting fact: depreciation in this kind of business is a real economic cost, because you have to keep buying new cards to replace old ones, so the 62% adjusted EBITDA margin is meaningless. In his words, "depreciation is a real expense, it is just paid a little later." Interest, by contrast, is not paid later — the RMB 846 million this quarter is cash out the door.

As for "it's down 71%, so it's cheap," he would probably invoke the reverse of his 1999 logic: for a company that needs to keep borrowing from others to survive, no amount of share-price decline turns into a "margin of safety" — it only becomes a "cheaper option." Berkshire's insurance float is negative-cost liability; CoreWeave's debt is liability at 7.9% cost. That is the essential difference.

Munger: First ask under what circumstances it goes to zero

Munger's method is to invert. This company does not need AI demand to fall to go to zero; it needs only one thing: the high-yield bond market refuses to refinance the neocloud in a given quarter. At that point it must swap equity for cash, and the equity already sits at USD 39.51, down 71% from its high.

The second trigger is customer concentration. Its top two customers account for 54% of revenue; Meta's USD 21 billion and OpenAI's USD 6.5 billion contracts are "maximum commitment" amounts, not "guaranteed payments." If even one moves its orders back in-house, the backlog shrinks while the debt does not fall by a cent. This is the structural flaw of leverage: good news gets discounted, bad news gets doubled.

He would probably add a line about incentives: management repeatedly stresses backlog and "adjusted" metrics on earnings calls, and those metrics happen to exclude interest and depreciation—"if the metric a company most wants to talk about happens to exclude its two largest costs, you already know the answer." Munger's conclusion would be total non-participation; not even the watchlist.

Li Lu: AI is real, but who captures the profit?

In Li Lu's framework, the money from a technological revolution is usually captured by three types of players: the standard-setters (Nvidia), the largest-scale players (hyperscalers), and those who can turn capex into a long-term moat. CoreWeave belongs to none of the three—it is the contractor in this arms race, earning the spread between construction timelines, power prices and cost of capital.

He would acknowledge the certainty on the demand side: compute is civilization-level infrastructure, and total demand will grow by an order of magnitude over the next decade. But the lesson of the railway era is that tenfold demand growth does not mean railway shareholders made money—US rail mileage multiplied several times over in the 1880s, and so did the number of bankrupt railway companies, because in capital-intensive industries excess profit is often eaten by capex itself.

His prescribed path is "participate in the trend, not the middleman": to bet on AI compute, buy the upstream with pricing power (Nvidia, the three HBM makers) or the application layer with cash flow, not a distributor running leverage to the limit. This is consistent with his style of judging opportunities in China—act only when downside protection is thick enough.

The only disagreement among the four: will AI demand fall?

The four see no disagreement on "this company doesn't work"; the only disagreement is over time horizon. Munger thinks there is no need to judge AI demand — the credit window is enough; Li Lu thinks that even if AI demand grows tenfold over ten years, this company is merely a conduit for traffic, but the trend itself is worth participating in (via upstream); Duan Yongping thinks demand will always be there, but this business simply cannot make money from it; Buffett cannot even be bothered to join the debate — "I don't invest in tech I don't understand, and I don't need to understand it to make money."

Put into action, all four frameworks yield the same move: don't buy. The difference is only "when one might buy" — Duan Yongping wants an extremely low price plus an improved business model, Li Lu wants thicker downside protection, and Buffett and Munger essentially rule it out permanently. This consensus of "same conclusion, different paths" carries far more weight than four people all saying "valuation is too high."

Odds assessment: neutral case yields only 9%, pessimistic case loses 70%

Lay out the probabilities of the three scenarios: bear 35%, base 45%, bull 20%. Expected value = 0.35×12 + 0.45×43 + 0.20×97 = 4.2 + 19.4 + 19.4 = USD 43, 8.8% above the current price. It looks "not expensive," but that rests on a 65% probability of not losing money, while the downside is -70%.

More critically, the asymmetry of the payoff is inverted: a normal good opportunity is "lose 20%, make 100%"; here it is "lose 70%, make 145% (and the bull case requires three things to hold at once: 100% order conversion + continued prepayments + credit markets staying open)." Under this structure, no position should be opened no matter how small.

Another angle is the bonds. In mid-November the unsecured bond yield was about 15%, implying the bond market assigns a not-low implicit default probability; the equity market, meanwhile, is still pricing in RMB 132.5 billion of orders. Two prices for the same company, and the bond one is usually right—because it has priority claim, and those who cannot afford to lose are the most serious.

Conclusion

Conclusion: avoid. The four masters differ only in wording, not in action. Duan Yongping says the business model does not work, Buffett says the balance sheet does not allow it, Munger says leverage will be killed off by the credit window, and Li Lu says the middleman cannot capture the money from the technology revolution. Three scenarios of USD 12 / 43 / 97, expected value USD 43, only 9% above the current price, against 70% downside. Conditions that trigger a re-evaluation: a clear path to positive free cash flow + debt duration extended to more than 5 years + at least one major customer contract converted to guaranteed minimum payment. Only when all three hold does the valuation method shift from "option pricing" back to "enterprise valuation".
