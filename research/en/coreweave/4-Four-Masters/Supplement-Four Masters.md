## Notes

Valuation: EV/EBITDA less net debt to derive equity value. Base case: 2027 revenue of USD 25 billion (roughly double the 2026 guidance of USD 13–13.3 billion), adjusted EBITDA of USD 13 billion (about 52% margin); at 7x, EV of USD 91 billion less net debt of USD 65 billion gives equity of USD 26 billion, or USD 43 per share on 605 million shares. Bull case: 8x × USD 16 billion = EV of USD 128 billion, less net debt of USD 68 billion = USD 60 billion, or USD 97 per share on 620 million shares. Bear case assumes 30% dilution from equity financing required in 2027 and EV/EBITDA compressing to 5x.

## Duan Yongping: This is good demand, not a good business

Duan Yongping draws a sharp distinction: good demand does not equal a good business. He bought Apple because users cannot switch away from iOS, and Moutai because they cannot switch away from the taste — "a moat is what others cannot take from you." CoreWeave's customers are indeed scrambling for compute, but what they are scrambling for is "there is no other choice right now," not "only CoreWeave can do it." Once Nvidia's capacity is released and the hyperscalers finish building their own, rental rates will fall immediately, while its debt and depreciation are rigid.

He would also ask the most basic question: will this business still exist in ten years? GPU cloud has no customer stickiness, no switching costs, no brand premium. All it takes for a customer to switch away is one phone call, a new contract, and migrating the workload next door to Nebius or its own data center. He does do this kind of business — but only when it is "cheap enough that you don't need to judge the future," not at a market cap of USD 22 billion and net debt of USD 61 billion.

Another line from Duan Yongping cuts deeper: "If the business model is bad, no price is cheap enough." His assessment now would be: I understand it, but I don't want to own it.

## Buffett: read the balance sheet first, the income statement can wait

When Buffett looks at a company like this, the first thing he flips to is the balance sheet: total debt of RMB 46.87 billion, of which RMB 41.6 billion is recourse debt at an average cost of 7.9%, with about RMB 13.4 billion maturing over the next two years. Then he looks at cash: about RMB 5.5 billion. Put those two numbers together and any talk of an "order book of RMB 132.5 billion" loses its weight — orders are future, debt is present, and creditors rank ahead of shareholders.

He would also point to an accounting fact: in this kind of business depreciation is a real economic cost, because you have to keep buying new cards to replace old ones, so the 62% adjusted EBITDA margin is meaningless. In his words, "depreciation is a real expense, it is just paid a little later." Interest, by contrast, is not paid later — the RMB 846 million this quarter is cash out the door.

As for "it's down 71%, so it's cheap," he would probably invoke the reverse of his 1999 logic: for a company that needs to keep borrowing from others to survive, no amount of share-price decline turns into a "margin of safety" — it only becomes a "cheaper option." Berkshire's insurance float is negative-cost liability; CoreWeave's debt is liability at 7.9% cost. That is the essential difference.

## Munger: First ask under what circumstances it goes to zero

Munger's method is to invert. This company does not need AI demand to fall to go to zero; it needs only one thing: the high-yield bond market refuses to refinance the neocloud in a given quarter. At that point it must swap equity for cash, and the equity already sits at USD 39.51, down 71% from its high.

Customer concentration is the second trigger. Its top two customers account for 54% of revenue, and Meta's USD 21 billion and OpenAI's USD 6.5 billion contracts are "maximum commitment" amounts, not "guaranteed payments." If even one moves its orders back in-house, the backlog shrinks while the debt does not fall by a cent. This is the structural flaw of leverage: good news gets discounted, bad news gets doubled.

He would probably add a line about incentives: management repeatedly stresses backlog and "adjusted" metrics on earnings calls, and those metrics happen to exclude interest and depreciation—"if the metric a company most wants to talk about happens to exclude its two largest costs, you already know the answer." Munger's conclusion would be total non-participation; not even the watchlist.

## Li Lu: AI is real, but who captures the profit?

In Li Lu's framework, the money from a technological revolution is usually captured by three types of players: the standard-setters (Nvidia), the largest-scale players (hyperscalers), and those who can turn capex into a long-term moat. CoreWeave belongs to none of the three—it is the contractor in this arms race, earning the spread between construction timelines, power prices and cost of capital.

He would acknowledge the certainty on the demand side: compute is civilisation-level infrastructure, and total demand will grow by an order of magnitude over the next decade. But the lesson of the railway era is that tenfold demand growth does not mean railway shareholders made money—US rail mileage multiplied several times over in the 1880s, and so did the number of bankrupt railway companies, because in capital-intensive industries excess profit is often eaten by capex itself.

His prescribed path is "participate in the trend, not the middleman": to bet on AI compute, buy the upstream with pricing power (Nvidia, the three HBM makers) or the application layer with cash flow, not a distributor running leverage to the limit. This is consistent with how he judges opportunities in China—act only when downside protection is thick enough.

## The only disagreement among the four: will AI demand fall?

All four agree the company doesn't work; they differ only on time horizon. Munger sees no need to judge AI demand — the credit window is enough. Li Lu argues that even if AI demand grows tenfold over ten years, the company is merely a conduit for traffic, though the trend itself is worth participating in (via upstream). Duan Yongping accepts demand will always be there, but the business simply cannot make money from it. Buffett cannot even be bothered to join the debate: "I don't invest in tech I don't understand, and I don't need to understand it to make money."

Put into action, all four frameworks yield the same move: don't buy. They differ only on "when one might buy" — Duan Yongping wants an extremely low price plus an improved business model, Li Lu wants thicker downside protection, and Buffett and Munger essentially rule it out permanently. This consensus of "same conclusion, different paths" carries far more weight than four people all saying "valuation is too high."

## Neutral case returns just 9%; bear case loses 70%

Assign probabilities to the three scenarios: bear 35%, base 45%, bull 20%. Expected value = 0.35×12 + 0.45×43 + 0.20×97 = 4.2 + 19.4 + 19.4 = USD 43, 8.8% above the current price. That looks "not expensive", but it rests on a 65% probability of not losing money, against a -70% downside.

More critically, the payoff asymmetry is inverted: a normal good opportunity is "lose 20%, make 100%"; here it is "lose 70%, make 145%" — and the bull case requires three things to hold at once: 100% order conversion, continued prepayments and credit markets staying open. On that structure, no position should be opened, however small.

The bonds offer another angle. In mid-November the unsecured bond yield was about 15%, implying the bond market assigns a not-low implied default probability, while the equity market is still pricing in RMB 132.5 billion of orders. Two prices for the same company, and the bond one is usually right — it has priority claim, and those who cannot afford to lose are the most serious.
