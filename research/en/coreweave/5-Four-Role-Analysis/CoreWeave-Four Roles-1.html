Core view

In one sentence: CoreWeave monetises a timing gap — it secures GPUs and power ahead of its customers, then sells that scarcity window as 5-year contracts. This is not a technology company but an asset-leasing business, with depreciating GPUs on the asset side and debt at a 7.9% cost on the liability side. Duan Yongping's first question would be: how long can this spread last?

Where does the spread come from?

Gross profit breaks down into three sources. The first is scarcity pricing: prices rose about 25% in July and a further 10% at quarter-end, while rents for older-generation Ampere and Hopper cards ran 30%–50% above the second quarter — this is money from insufficient supply, not from capability. The second is delivery speed: 4.6GW of contracted power, 1.7GW already operating, with 900MW of contracts added and 200MW brought online in a single quarter — the fastest pace of any neocloud globally. The third is power prices and site selection: siting data centers where electricity is cheap and grid connection is fast.

Of the three, only the second counts as capability; the first is a gift from the cycle, and the third is a replicable resource. A gift from the cycle disappears once supply catches up, and it disappears fast — the moment Nvidia's capacity is released and Microsoft's and Oracle's self-built projects come online, rents head downhill.

Why customers sign with it instead of building in-house

Time is the answer. Buyers such as OpenAI and Meta will grow 2026 compute demand faster than any self-built project can be energised: a GW-scale data centre takes 3–5 years from site selection to energisation, whereas CoreWeave can fill empty racks in an existing campus with GPUs within 6 months. Customers therefore pay an "acceleration fee."

But that fee holds only while "you are faster than me." On November 18, Nadella said publicly that "selling to just one or two customers is not a business," as Microsoft reviewed over USD 80 billion of external AI compute commitments and cancelled a USD 3.22 billion Nscale order.This is the buyer's choice after running the numbers: where self-built cost per watt is lower, the acceleration fee is no longer worth paying.

The ten-year question for this business

Duan Yongping's test — will it still be here in ten years? The honest answer: it may survive as a company, but probably not as a business with a moat. GPU cloud ends up looking like IDC (data centre hosting): a thin-margin utility competing on scale and cost of capital, where the top few survive but none has pricing power.

Where it stands today: not number one on scale (Microsoft's quarterly capex of USD 45.2 billion is 12 times its quarterly revenue), not lowest on cost of capital (7.9% versus under 5% for Microsoft), and holding no proprietary technology. It leads on none of the three conditions, yet carries USD 46.9 billion of debt — not a "rising leader," but "a leveraged player at the top of the cycle."

Conclusion

Business judgment: good demand, bad business. Revenue growth is real (+168%), but it is driven by supply shortage and customers pulling forward schedules, both of which are fading. The business ultimately becomes a thin-margin computing utility, and it enters that phase with the most expensive capital structure.Duan Yongping-style conclusion: understandable, but I don't want to own it; unless the price is low enough that "you make money without looking at the future," and that requires the stock to halve again while the debt does not blow up.
