Risk Assessment (Li Lu's perspective) — CoreWeave

Five ways it dies, and which is most likely

Core view

CoreWeave's risk is not "AI demand falling short of expectations"—that probability is very low. What would truly kill the equity is four things: the refinancing window closing, customers moving orders back in-house, depreciation lives forced lower, and heavy equity dilution. Three of the four have nothing to do with AI demand; all four are about capital structure.

The most likely way to die: not a demand collapse, but an inability to roll over debt

Put the debt maturity schedule and cash flow side by side and the conclusion is clear: the USD 13.4 billion maturing in 2027 cannot be repaid out of its own cash flow. Free cash flow for the first three quarters of 2026 was roughly negative USD 17.9 billion; even with capex flat in 2027, the shortfall remains in the tens of billions. It must therefore return to the bond market in 2027, and its unsecured debt already yields 15%.

What does a 15% funding cost mean? A rough calculation: borrow another USD 20 billion at 15% and annual interest comes to USD 3 billion—more than half of full-year 2026 adjusted EBITDA. This is a self-reinforcing death spiral: the more cash it lacks, the more expensive it gets; the more expensive it gets, the more cash it lacks.

Underappreciated risk: the contract is a maximum commitment, not a guaranteed payment

Meta's USD 21 billion and OpenAI's USD 6.5 billion are commitments, not take-or-pay. The distinction matters for both accounting and cash: if actual customer usage falls below the commitment, CoreWeave does not collect the full amount, yet the racks, power and debt have already been provisioned at full capacity.

The USD 132.5 billion backlog implies 36x annual revenue coverage, but its quality hinges on two things: whether customers can perform, and whether the contracts carry minimum-payment clauses. The 10-Q discloses little on either point, and that is itself a risk — when you cannot verify the quality of a number, you should discount it for the worst case.

How would Li Lu price this risk?

Li Lu's framework centres on "downside protection": if the worst case is a 70% loss, the expected value must be significantly positive to justify the position. Here the expected value is only +9% (USD 43 vs USD 39.51) against a -70% downside, so the risk-reward is completely unworkable.

His more fundamental point would be this: AI is a civilisation-level opportunity, but how you participate determines your return. Nvidia is the standard-setter; Microsoft is cash flow plus an option; CoreWeave is "the thinnest sliver of equity plus the highest leverage." One trend, three risk-return structures — there is no reason to choose the worst one.

Conclusion

Risk assessment: unacceptable. Four of the five risks are tied to the capital structure and reinforce one another: customer attrition weakens cash flow, weaker cash flow raises refinancing costs, and costlier refinancing forces equity dilution. This is not "high volatility"; it is "structural fragility." Li Lu-style conclusion: stay out. If tracking is a must, watch only two leading indicators: the unsecured bond yield (currently about 15%) and quarterly customer prepayments (RMB 2.127 billion this quarter, Q4 guidance around RMB 9 billion). The former determines whether the company survives; the latter, whether it lives like a normal company.
