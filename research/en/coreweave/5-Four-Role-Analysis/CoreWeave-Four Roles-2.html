Financial analysis (Buffett's perspective) — CoreWeave

The balance sheet tells the truth earlier than the income statement

Core view

The income statement and the balance sheet tell two different stories. Revenue came in at RMB 3.661 billion and adjusted EBITDA at RMB 2.256 billion, yet net interest of RMB 846 million ran at 2.2x adjusted operating profit of RMB 379 million, leaving a net loss of RMB 709 million. The issue is not the P&L but the capital structure: RMB 46.87 billion of debt, RMB 13.4 billion maturing within two years, and RMB 5.5 billion of cash on hand.

Quality of operating cash flow

Operating cash flow was USD 1.975 billion, which looks healthy in isolation. But break down the components: customer prepayments rose by 2.127 billion, more than all of operating cash flow. In other words, strip out prepayments and day-to-day operations were net cash outflow. Prepayments are essentially "future service fees paid in advance by customers," booked as deferred revenue in accounting, and must be delivered in future through data centers that come online on schedule — otherwise they become liabilities.

Buffett's stance on such projects has always been clear: prepayments are a good thing, but parading prepayments as operating cash flow dresses up financing activities as operating activities. True operating cash flow should come from "cash received for services already rendered," not "cash received before services are rendered." This is also why the line in the Q4 guidance — "about USD 9 billion of prepayments over the next three months" — should be read as a financing plan, not an operating result.

The structure of the debt is more frightening than its size

Total debt stands at RMB 46.87 billion at an average cost of 7.9%, of which RMB 41.6 billion is recourse — creditors can reach the parent company directly, rather than relying on non-recourse financing at the project-company level. About RMB 13.4 billion matures over the next two years against roughly RMB 5.5 billion of cash on hand, leaving a RMB 7.9 billion gap to be covered by refinancing or operating cash flow — and operating cash flow itself is propped up by prepayments.

A textbook case of duration mismatch plus cost mismatch: the assets are GPUs depreciated over 6 years and data halls with 15–20 year lives, while the liabilities are debt maturing within 2 years. If the refinancing window shuts for even one quarter, the gap must be filled with equity — and the equity has already fallen from 137.98 to 39.51. The June unsecured bond with a 9.625% coupon yielded 15% in mid-November; the market is already pricing it as distressed debt.

Depreciation is the biggest hidden liability in this business.

CoreWeave depreciates GPUs over roughly 6 years. If the true economic life is 3–4 years—NVIDIA ships a new generation every year, and Hopper rental rates step down once Rubin launches—then the depreciation understated each year becomes future impairment or a profit collapse. A rough calculation: the equipment investment behind 4.6GW of contracted power, at USD 30 million/MW, is about USD 138 billion. Cutting the depreciation life from 6 years to 4 years raises annual depreciation from USD 23 billion to USD 34.5 billion—a gap larger than its adjusted EBITDA for a full year.

This is also the most dangerous part of the "62% EBITDA margin": it packages a number determined by accounting assumptions as operating capability. There is only one real test: what price these cards can still be rented out at in five years.

Conclusion

Financial verdict: a substandard balance sheet and an income statement built on assumptions. Operating progress is real — GAAP operating profit turned positive for the first time at RMB 20.7 million — but RMB 84.6 million of interest swallowed it, and customer prepayments of RMB 212.7 million plus new debt covered the remaining gap. Buffett-style conclusion: do not invest. His logic on such companies: "If you have to depend on someone else continuing to lend you money to survive, you are not running a business, you are running a financing." Only two metrics to watch: whether the quarterly free cash flow gap narrows, and whether unsecured bond yields fall back below 10%.
