Core thesis

Three founders (CEO Michael Intrator, CSO Brian Venturo, CDO Brannin McBee) went from an Ethereum mining farm in 2017 to a GPU cloud with 4.6GW of contracted power in 2026. Their execution ranks first among neoclouds, and that must be acknowledged. The problem is capital allocation: after rejecting Core Scientific's stock-for-stock acquisition, they turned to expansion funded by RMB 4.69 billion of debt and continuous equity issuance; dual-class shares let the founding team control the company with a minority stake; and Nvidia is simultaneously supplier, shareholder and potential customer, a complex related-party structure.

Capital allocation track record: three key decisions

First, the March 2025 IPO, priced at USD 40, raising about USD 1.5 billion. Listing at peak AI sentiment, with Nvidia as an anchor investor subscribing USD 250 million — the right move, securing the cheapest equity capital.

Second, the all-stock acquisition of Core Scientific proposed in 2025 (about USD 9 billion), rejected by its shareholders in October. The direction was right (acquiring power and ready-built data centers), but the cost was using overvalued stock to buy assets, and the rejection exposed disagreement between the two sides on valuation. The company then pivoted to "leased power plus self-build," with all capex running through the balance sheet and lease liabilities.

Third, three funding channels opened simultaneously in 2026: USD 4.2 billion convertible notes, a USD 2.6 billion delayed-draw term loan, and a plan to issue 35 million shares. This is the classic "raise to the full while the window is open" strategy — the right thing to do if the credit window closes next quarter; but it also signals management believes the window is closing. Using all three instruments together is defensive financing, not offensive financing.

Said versus done

Looking back at the past four quarters of earnings calls, three types of management commitments are worth noting. Delivery commitments were met well: contracted power rose from about 2.5GW at end-2025 to 4.6GW, and operating capacity from just over 1GW to 1.7GW, broadly in line with quarterly targets. Profitability commitments were met so-so: adjusted operating margin fell from 15.9% (year-ago period) to 10.4%; Q3 exceeded the upper end of guidance, but the full-year trend is downward. Financing commitments were the loosest: the line "prepayments and deposits will cover most capex" recurred across three quarters, while debt in fact rose from the RMB 30 billion level to RMB 46.87 billion.

This combination says one thing: it is a reliable contractor on engineering and delivery, and an increasingly leveraged borrower in its capital structure. Management should be scored separately on these two counts.

Integrity and governance: three areas that warrant a discount

Dual-class structure.The founding team controls voting power through Class B shares far exceeding its economic interest, leaving outside shareholders with almost no veto over major decisions (equity issuance, M&A, debt expansion). Common at tech companies, but for a utility-type company with 16x leverage, the governance discount should be larger.

The triangle of related parties.NVIDIA is supplier (GPUs), shareholder (anchor IPO investor), and the party questioned as backstop (supporting idle capacity). The SEC investigation into NVIDIA's accounting treatment on November 17 turned this triangle from "strategic synergy" into "risk contagion." Whether related-party disclosures are adequate is something you have to check yourself in the 10-K footnotes.

Metric selection.The most frequent terms on earnings calls and in press releases are backlog and adjusted EBITDA, both of which exclude this company's largest costs (depreciation, interest).This is not a violation, it is selective narrative—but for investors, the two excluded items are exactly what matter most.

Successor: no Plan B

The company is still led by its three founders: the CEO oversees strategy and external affairs, the CSO oversees capital, and the CDO oversees delivery. The division of labor is clear, but there is no publicly disclosed succession plan. That is not unusual for a company with USD 3 billion in revenue, but given that its survival depends heavily on its ability to raise financing, and that ability in turn depends heavily on the CEO's personal relationships in the credit markets, this is an underappreciated single-point risk.

For comparison: financing at rivals such as Microsoft and Oracle is an institutional affair (ratings, bond markets, bank syndicates), and whoever serves as CEO can borrow money. Part of CoreWeave's financing rests on "founder credit" — and that part is the most fragile in a crisis.

Conclusion

Conclusion: management execution deserves a premium; capital allocation and governance do not. Three founders built a mining site into a 4.6GW computing platform, with delivery speed that sets the industry benchmark — that earns high marks. But after refusing a stock-for-stock acquisition they pivoted to all-debt expansion, running three financing instruments simultaneously, alongside dual-class shares + a triangular web of related-party dealings + selective disclosure — all of which turn the "management premium" into a "governance discount." Impact on the investment decision: not selling because of management's demerits (the position was never held), but refusing to grant any valuation premium because of the governance discount.
