## Capital allocation track record: three key decisions

First, the March 2025 IPO at USD 40, raising about USD 1.5 billion. Listing into peak AI sentiment with Nvidia as anchor investor, subscribing USD 250 million, was the right call — it secured the cheapest equity capital.

Second, the all-stock acquisition of Core Scientific proposed in 2025 (about USD 9 billion), rejected by shareholders in October. The direction was right — acquiring power and ready-built data centers — but the cost meant using overvalued stock to buy assets, and the rejection exposed disagreement between the two sides on valuation. The company then pivoted to "leased power plus self-build," with all capex running through the balance sheet and lease liabilities.

Third, three funding channels opened simultaneously in 2026: USD 4.2 billion convertible notes, a USD 2.6 billion delayed-draw term loan, and a plan to issue 35 million shares. This is the classic "raise to the full while the window is open" strategy — the right thing to do if the credit window closes next quarter, but it also signals management believes the window is closing. Using all three instruments together is defensive financing, not offensive financing.

## Said versus done

Across the past four quarters of earnings calls, three categories of management commitment stand out. Delivery commitments were met: contracted power rose from about 2.5GW at end-2025 to 4.6GW and operating capacity from just over 1GW to 1.7GW, broadly in line with quarterly targets. Profitability commitments were met only in part: adjusted operating margin fell from 15.9% a year earlier to 10.4%; Q3 beat the upper end of guidance, but the full-year trend is downward. Financing commitments were the weakest: the line that "prepayments and deposits will cover most capex" recurred across three quarters, yet debt rose from the RMB 30 billion level to RMB 46.87 billion.

The pattern points to one conclusion: a reliable contractor on engineering and delivery, but an increasingly leveraged borrower in its capital structure. Management should be scored separately on each count.

## Integrity and governance: three areas that warrant a discount

Dual-class structure.The founding team's Class B shares carry voting power far in excess of its economic interest, leaving outside shareholders with almost no veto over major decisions (equity issuance, M&A, debt expansion). This is common at tech companies, but for a utility-type business running 16x leverage, the governance discount should be wider.

The triangle of related parties.NVIDIA is supplier (GPUs), shareholder (anchor IPO investor) and the party whose backstop role is in question (supporting idle capacity). The SEC investigation into NVIDIA's accounting treatment on November 17 turned this triangle from "strategic synergy" into "risk contagion." Whether related-party disclosures are adequate is a question investors must answer themselves in the 10-K footnotes.

Metric selection.Backlog and adjusted EBITDA dominate earnings calls and press releases, yet both exclude this company's largest costs (depreciation, interest).This is not a violation but selective narrative—and for investors, the two excluded items are precisely what matter most.

## Successor: no Plan B

The three founders still run the company: the CEO oversees strategy and external affairs, the CSO capital, and the CDO delivery. The split is clear, but no succession plan has been disclosed. That is not unusual for a company with USD 3 billion in revenue, but given that its survival depends heavily on its ability to raise financing, and that ability in turn depends heavily on the CEO's personal relationships in the credit markets, this is an underappreciated single-point risk.

By contrast, financing at rivals such as Microsoft and Oracle is an institutional affair (ratings, bond markets, bank syndicates), and whoever serves as CEO can borrow money. Part of CoreWeave's financing rests on "founder credit" — and that part is the most fragile in a crisis.
