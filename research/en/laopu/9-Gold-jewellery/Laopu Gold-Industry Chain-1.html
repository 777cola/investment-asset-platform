Core call: the most profitable link in the chain is also the one most at the mercy of the gold price

In the gold jewelry chain, value distribution is clear: upstream miners earn resource money, midstream processors earn hard-earned money, brands earn symbolic money, and malls earn location money. Laopu sits in the "brand" link, with a gross margin of 41.3%, the fattest segment of the chain; but it simultaneously carries RMB 19 billion in inventory, absorbing the upstream price risk.

Its position in the chain determines both its ceiling and its soft spot. It has zero bargaining power over upstream (Shanghai Gold Exchange, banks, smelters) — the gold price is given; it has bargaining power over downstream (consumers) through per-piece pricing; it is weak against malls (SKP, MixC, Hang Lung) — commission rates and locations are set by them. Its profit = brand labor fee − mall rent − inventory cost; the first two are relatively stable, the third jumps with the gold price.

Upstream: the gold price is a given, not something to be negotiated

Gold is a standardized commodity. Prices are set by London gold and the Shanghai Gold Exchange, and any jewelry company is a price taker. Laopu's procurement cost = the gold price + a minimal processing fee, with no room for negotiation.

This determines its cost structure: gold materials account for about 60% of operating costs and are uncontrollable. Spot gold stood at USD 5,419/oz in January 2026 and fell below 4,000 in June, with domestic pure-gold retail prices cut by more than 25% in tandem. Upstream prices moved 25%, while downstream selling-price adjustments always lag — this is why the gross margin was squeezed to 37.6% in 2025.

Compare the upstream: for companies like Zijin Mining, the stock rises when gold rises and falls when gold falls, but their costs (mining, smelting) are relatively fixed, so the gross margin amplifies gold-price moves. Laopu is the opposite — it absorbs gold-price volatility into inventory and releases it gradually through selling prices. At the same gold price, the upstream benefits on the revenue side and Laopu benefits on the asset side; the mechanisms are entirely different.

Mid- and downstream: it earns "symbol money" and pays "location rent"

Of the RMB 19.79 billion in revenue, gold material costs account for about RMB 11.6 billion, and the remaining RMB 8.2 billion is labor fees, design and brand premium. That RMB 8.2 billion is the real profit pool.

But to capture that RMB 8.2 billion, two payments come first: one to the malls (15%–25% commission), one to inventory (financing cost on RMB 19 billion tied up). Rough math: 45 stores, annualized sales per store of RMB 1.01 billion, at a 20% commission, about RMB 9 billion a year goes to the malls — more than its entire gross profit of RMB 8.174 billion. (Note: commissions are typically calculated on sales, not directly comparable to gross profit; this only illustrates the order of magnitude of rent.)

So the truth of this business is: brand premium goes to the company, location premium goes to the malls, gold-price swings go to shareholders. Three parties split the proceeds; the company takes the middle slice, the most stable and the most easily squeezed. The 2026 National Day "price cuts without volume" shows brand premium is being squeezed; gold falling from 5,419 to 4,300 shows gold-price swings are hurting shareholders.

Cycle position: mid-cycle high-level volatility, not a bottom

Three coordinates locate the industry cycle.

Gold price: peaked at USD 5,419 on 5 January 2026, broke below 4,000 in June, and sat near 4,300 in early October. That is down 21% from the peak but still above the 2024 average. This is the middle stage of a retreat from highs.

Demand: China's gold jewellery consumption was 360 tonnes in 2025, down 25% year on year, while spending reached RMB 281.4 billion, up 8%. Q1 2026 volume was 85 tonnes, down 32%, with spending of RMB 92.8 billion. Volume is falling, value is rising; price increases are propping up the top line.

Inventory: Laopu holds RMB 19 billion, or 271 days. Chow Tai Fook closed nearly a thousand stores in fiscal 2026 to clear inventory. Shuibei merchants queued to buy gold over the National Day holiday, buying raw material rather than jewellery, which shows the downstream is betting on a rebound.

Together, the three coordinates show an industry locked in a "high price, shrinking volume, high inventory" combination, the classic middle-to-late stage of a cycle. Clearing usually takes four to six quarters and runs through store closures, price cuts and inventory clearance. Laopu's current strategy, optimizing 10–12 stores and holding list prices while discounting only iterated products, follows the cycle. The direction is right, but the cuts are not deep enough.

An overlooked policy variable: VAT

From November 2025, adjusted VAT policy on gold jewellery took effect, raising end-market costs across the industry — one reason gold jewellery consumption fell 32% year on year in the first quarter of 2026 (both the World Gold Council and brokers list the new policy as a demand drag).

For a per-piece-priced, high-gross-margin brand like Laopu, the policy impact is relatively small — tax as a share of its selling price is diluted by brand premium; for per-gram sellers the impact is large, because end prices are more sensitive to tax. This accelerates the industry's shift toward "branding" concentration — a near-term drag on demand, a medium- to long-term positive for leaders.

But note: the policy is a one-off cost increase, not a sustained demand stimulus. It will not change the direction of the cycle, only speed up divergence.

Conclusion

Industry-chain view: Laopu occupies the most profitable segment of the chain, but it has taken on the risk it is least equipped to bear. A 41.3% gross margin proves the brand has pricing power; RMB 19 billion of inventory proves it has swapped that pricing power for gold-price exposure. The industry cycle is in its mid-to-late stage, and consolidation will take another four to six quarters. Its current direction of "optimizing stores + holding prices" is right, but if gold prices fall another leg, what gives way first will be its inventory, not its brand.
