It is not his own view. ## Question 1: Is this a good business?

A "good business" meets just three criteria: the product is differentiated, users can't do without it, and it doesn't require constant reinvestment.

Criterion one: pass. Laopu makes traditional-method gold differently from everyone else. It raised prices 20%–30% in February and customers still bought — that is differentiation. A 41.3% gross margin that no one else in the industry achieves is not luck.

Criterion two: half pass. Buyers recognise the brand, but what they recognise is "this is what's popular now," not "it has to be this one." A 9.0% discount over the 2026 National Day holiday drew no queues — proof that this "can't do without it" is conditional.

Criterion three: fail. For every extra yuan of profit, it must first push RMB 4.5 of gold into inventory. That is not a good business; it is a heavy business. A good business is one like Moutai — the liquor grows more valuable with age, and cash comes in before goods ship.

So my answer is: a good brand, not a good business. The two are often conflated, but they are separate things.

## Question 2: Will it still be around in ten years?

Gold will certainly still be here in ten years; it has survived three thousand years. Whether the "Laopu" brand will is another question.

I ran companies for many years and know a rule: a brand that rises on a single category usually does not outlive that category. VCDs, MP3 players, feature phones—all the same. Ancient-method gold is a category. It took off in 2023 and is now in its fourth year. Whether consumers will still wear ancient-method gold in ten years, I don't know, and neither does anyone else.

But it has one advantage over others: what it sells is not "gold jewelry" but "a symbol of Chinese culture." Symbols outlive functions. Hanfu has been popular for over a decade and still is; guochao is still around too. If it can upgrade "ancient-method gold" into "Eastern aesthetics," it has a chance to outlive the category; if it can only cling to ancient-method gold, it will go wherever the category goes.

As things stand, the upgrade has not begun. 45 stores, RMB 27.3 billion in revenue, and what it still sells is engraved bangles and filigree necklaces.

## Question three: Can you understand it?

This is the most critical question. My answer: I understand the business; I do not understand the price.

The business itself is clear: 45 stores, RMB 1 billion of sales per store, a 41% gross margin and a 21% net margin — all of which I can calculate. I also know why it makes money: good locations, a strong brand, and selling by piece rather than by gram.

But a large chunk of its profit comes from the gold price. Gold was USD 5,419 in January, USD 4,000 in June and USD 4,300 in October. I do not understand this, and neither does anyone else. I do not touch what I do not understand.

Some will say: didn't you buy Apple, and you don't understand Apple's share price either? That is different. For Apple's business I can estimate roughly how much it will earn five years out, with no great margin of error; for Laopu's business, a move in the gold price can halve profit. This is not a question of ability but of the nature of the business.

So my answer is: half understood. What is only half understood can only be held in a small position, never a heavy one.

## Question 4: At what price to buy?

For the buy price, I don't look at P/E; I look at "how many years it takes me to get my money back." That's my old method.

On sustainable profit, it earns RMB 4.5 billion to 5.7 billion a year. The current market cap is HKD 59.6 billion, or RMB 54.5 billion, which equals 9.5–12x. At this price, I get my money back in ten years. That's acceptable, but not "so cheap you don't have to think."

What counts as not having to think? When I bought Apple, it was a bit over 10x after deducting net cash, and that company could still buy back 3%–4% of its shares every year. Laopu has no buyback, and its 75% dividend payout draws on existing cash, so the quality is a notch lower.

So my price is: 5–6x sustainable profit, which is HKD 140–170. That corresponds to a market cap of HKD 25 billion–30 billion, close to its asset value (RMB 19 billion in gold + 45 locations). Only at that level would I feel "it can't fall much further."

It's now HKD 337, still half again away. No rush; good companies also have cheap moments.

## Question 5: What worries you most?

The real fear is not a falling gold price. A falling gold price is plain for all to see: the market already knows, and it is partly in the price.

The real fear is "it becomes a company that sells by the gram." What does that mean? Consumers stop paying up for the symbol and recognise only the value of the gold itself. At that point the 41.3% gross margin falls below 20%, the RMB 19 billion of inventory becomes pure dead weight, and the rent on 45 stores becomes a fixed cost. If all three land together, this company is no longer worth much.

Are there signs? Yes. During the 2026 National Day holiday it discounted iterative products by 10% (9.0 off) for the first time, with VIPs able to stack another discount, and still no one queued. Over the same period, shoppers queued in Shuibei, Shenzhen to buy plain gold at low fabrication fees. On one side, "buying by the gram is a good deal"; on the other, "buying by the piece is not worth it." Consumers' choice is clear.

If this trend persists for two years, it will no longer be "China's Tiffany" but "a pricier Chow Tai Fook."

## Question 6: What should existing holders do?

Ask yourself one question first: did you buy it because "gold prices will rise," or because "this brand will keep growing"?

If it was the former, your thesis is gone — gold peaked in January and broke below 4,000 in June — and you should exit. If it was the latter, look at same-store sales: in Q2 2026 they already turned negative, and National Day discounting failed to drive volume, so that thesis is weakening too.

I don't recommend "holding on because it has fallen a lot." Falling a lot is not a reason; it only means it was previously expensive. One standard for buying and holding: if you were flat today, would you still buy at this price? If not, you should sell.

As for me, what would I do at HKD 337? Nothing. Add it to the watchlist and wait for HKD 140–170, or wait for it to prove it can grow without relying on gold prices. If either happens, I'll take another look.
