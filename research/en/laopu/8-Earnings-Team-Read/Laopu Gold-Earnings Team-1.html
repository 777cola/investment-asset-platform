Core view

Duan Yongping asks only two questions when reading financial statements: where the money comes from, and how much must be invested to keep earning. Laopu's answers are one good, one bad. The money comes from per-piece pricing premiums—good. To keep earning, it must first sink RMB 19 billion into inventory—bad.

Where the money comes from: half from fabrication fees, half from financial attributes

Revenue of RMB 19.808 billion splits into two parts: gold material cost of about RMB 11.6 billion (backed out from a 41.3% gross margin) and roughly RMB 8.2 billion of labour, design and brand premium. In other words, of every RMB 100 sold, RMB 41 is the "non-gold" portion. That money does not come from the gold price; it comes from the brand.

How much of that RMB 41, in turn, comes from "scarce locations"? All 45 Laopu stores sit in top-tier malls, with average ticket sizes of RMB 30,000–100,000. The same bangle sells for RMB 20,000 at Chow Tai Fook and RMB 35,000 at Laopu; the RMB 15,000 gap is location rent converted into gross profit. Duan Yongping would accept this point: this is not a hollow premium; consumers voted for it with their money.

But he would immediately ask a second question: can this premium be charged indefinitely? No — Q2 2026 already provided a counterexample. After price hikes of 20%–30%, Q2 revenue fell to RMB 2.3–3.95 billion, showing the premium has a ceiling and volume drops once it is crossed.

Every additional RMB 1 of revenue requires RMB 4.5 of inventory to be funded upfront

This is the line Duan Yongping cares about most: RMB 19.02 billion of inventory against RMB 4.27 billion of half-year net profit — inventory/net profit of 4.5. Moutai's inventory is also growing, but that is base liquor, which appreciates with age and does not depreciate; Laopu's inventory is gold, and its price moves every day.

The inventory structure determines the nature of the business. In 2025 the company stocked up aggressively, driving operating cash flow to -RMB 6.85 billion, propped up by debt and financing; in the first half of 2026 it bought a little less, and cash flow only just turned positive at +RMB 2.01 billion. In other words, whether this company's cash flow is good or bad depends mainly on whether it "buys goods," not on whether it "sells well."

What he likes are businesses that "can grow without putting money in" — Apple, Moutai, Gree. Laopu is not one. For Laopu to grow, it must first push gold into the warehouse; to stock up, it must have capital; to have capital, it must answer to the banks and the gold price. In this loop, the company holds no initiative.

What does a 75% dividend payout mean?

Duan Yongping would give this section extra points. It earned RMB 4.267 billion and paid out RMB 3.185 billion, a payout ratio of 74.6%, and it is still in its growth phase. His logic is simple: management willing to hand out cash usually will not waste it.

One detail, though: dividends of RMB 3.185 billion > operating cash flow of RMB 2.008 billion, with the gap covered by cash on the balance sheet. The dividend therefore signals not "so much cash there is nowhere to put it" but "management wants to send the market a signal." The signal itself is not bad, just unsustainable — if profit falls to RMB 4.5 billion in 2027, the same 75% would be RMB 3.375 billion, and cash cannot support it.

His judgment would probably be: this is a company with "a very good brand and a mediocre business model," suitable for buying a little at extremely low valuations, not suitable as a core holding. What counts as extremely low? On sustainable profit of RMB 4.5 billion and 5x P/E, market cap is RMB 22.5 billion, about HKD 24.6 billion, corresponding to a share price of HKD 139. That price would likely appear only if gold prices collapse.

Conclusion

Duan Yongping-style conclusion: a brand worth respecting, a business not worth a heavy position. He would only look seriously below HKD 140; at HKD 337 today, it is out of range. The judgment rests not on whether the valuation is high or low, but on whether the business needs the gold price to go its way in order to make money — if it does, he does not buy.
