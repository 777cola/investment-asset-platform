Core call: good brand, bad business, wrong price anchor

The four converged quickly: this is a good brand, but not a good business. Duan Yongping would say pricing power exists but is capped by the gold price; Buffett would call it a cyclical stock and read P/E at the earnings trough, not the peak; Munger would walk you through a 15% fall in gold, net profit halving and the P/E doubling on its own; Li Lu would concede that cultural confidence is a long-term trend, but brands have life cycles, and forty-five stores cannot yet support a luxury valuation.

Three valuation scenarios: bear case HKD 222 (-34%), base case HKD 352 (+4%), bull case HKD 595 (+77%). A 2:1 payoff looks attractive, but the odds skew toward the bear case — direction is set by the gold price, and the gold price is not up to management. The base case sits almost exactly at the current price, which tells you the market has not mispriced the stock; it is simply waiting for an answer.

Duan Yongping: It can raise prices, but the ceiling on price increases is the gold price

Duan Yongping asks just two questions: can it raise prices, and does it need heavy reinvestment?

The first passes halfway. Prices rose 20%–30% across all channels in late February 2026, with some products above RMB 2,500 per gram, and gross margin still climbed from 38.1% to 41.3% after the increase—evidence that consumers are paying for the brand rather than being bought with discounts. Three increases—August 2025, October 2025 and February 2026—came in at 10%–15%, 20%–30% and 20%–30%, a steady cadence. But he would immediately add: it sells gold, raw material is 60% of cost, and pricing power is hostage to the gold-price cycle; it cannot do what See's Candies does—"raise prices with inflation every year, cost up 2%, price up 6%."

The second fails. RMB 19 billion of inventory and 271 days of turnover make this a heavy-asset business. What he likes is "asset-light, high turnover, good cash flow," like Moutai—advance receipts arrive first, inventory gains value the longer it sits, and capex is almost nil. Laopu is the reverse: inventory ties up RMB 19 billion, operating cash flow has only just turned positive at RMB 2 billion, and it still has to pay out RMB 3.185 billion in dividends.

So his verdict would be roughly this: a good brand, but not a good business. The brand buys you a few points of gross margin; the business underneath is still the gold price. He would look at it, but not buy at the top of the cycle—because "I can't read the gold price" means, in his book, it sits outside his circle.

Buffett: look at the P/E of cyclicals at the earnings trough

Buffett would compare Laopu with See's Candies. See's average ticket is a few dozen dollars, sugar accounts for less than 20% of cost, and brand power turns USD 1 of sugar into USD 3 of sales, leaving it largely insulated from swings in gold and sugar prices. Raw materials make up 60% of Laopu's costs, and its brand premium shows up in labor and design, so gross margin jumps whenever the gold price moves.

This is not a moat that is too narrow; it is the physics of the business. He would turn to the 2025 page: gold prices rose fast, the company raised prices three times and still could not keep up with costs, and gross margin fell from 41.2% to 37.6%.Whether a company can pass on costs is judged in the year costs rise fastest, not the year it raises prices most smoothly.

On margin of safety, 7.9x (recalculated basis) looks cheap at first glance. But he has bought too many low-P/E cyclicals, and the lesson is written in Berkshire's annual reports: P/E for cyclicals must be read at the earnings trough, not the peak. Of the RMB 4.267 billion net profit in the first half of 2026, Q1 accounted for more than RMB 3.5 billion; annualizing Q2's RMB 510–760 million gives a P/E of 20–30x.A P/E calculated on peak profit is not a margin of safety; it is a trap.

He would set one condition: wait until the gold price has been flat for two quarters and same-store sales have returned to positive growth before discussing price. If P/E is still around 10x then, that is the level at which he would be willing to act.

Munger: Invert—what would make it fall by another half?

Munger does not guess direction; he works only the reverse case.

Lay out the scenario: gold falls from USD 4,300 in early October to USD 3,600, a 16% decline. Against inventory of RMB 19.018 billion, assuming 60% is gold raw material, the theoretical impairment is about RMB 1.8 billion; gross margin falls from 41.3% to 37%; same-store sales turn negative 10%. If all three hit at once, 2027 net profit could be only RMB 4.5 billion, half of full-year 2026 (about RMB 8.5 billion annualized from the first half).

At that point P/E goes from 7.9x to 13x—the share price does not move, yet the valuation doubles on its own. That is the mechanism of the low-P/E trap: the denominator shrinks faster than the price falls.

He would also point to a more hidden mechanism: price hikes, inventory stockpiling and store openings reinforce each other in an upcycle and backfire together in a downcycle. Price hikes pull forward customer spending (that is where Q1's RMB 19 billion including tax came from), stockpiling turns inventory into a source of impairment, and store openings make fixed costs rigid. Munger calls this the lollapalooza effect—multiple factors stacking in the same direction. On the way up it makes results look too good to be true; on the way down, too bad to be true.

Li Lu: cultural trends are real, and so are brand life cycles

Li Lu takes the longest time horizon. He would acknowledge one fact: Chinese consumers' willingness to pay for "cultural identity" is rising. Ancient-method gold is a successful attempt to turn traditional culture into a wearable product; the craft set of chiseling, filigree and enameling carries explicit intangible-cultural-heritage endorsement, and the customer base has expanded from wedding-driven necessity to self-reward. The trend is not confined to China: with a store opened at Marina Bay Sands in Singapore in June 2025, overseas revenue reached RMB 3.32 billion in the first half of 2026, up 107.8% YoY, or roughly RMB 1 billion annualized per store—evidence that overseas Chinese and local customers also recognise this symbol.

But he would flag two things. First, brands have life cycles. The luxury industry has rotated through several leaders over seventy years, and fewer than one in ten brands survives beyond thirty years. Laopu opened its first store in 2009 and has a seventeen-year brand history; it has not yet lived through a full down-cycle in the gold price, let alone a generational shift in consumer taste.

Second, its current position is "the number one in high-end gold jewelry," not "luxury." Luxury rests on "you cannot buy an equivalent status symbol anywhere else"; Laopu rests on the middle ground of "pricier than Chow Tai Fook, cheaper than Cartier." That position is valuable—a 41.3% gross margin proves it—but it should not be valued as luxury, and 13x P/E is already a reasonable ceiling for this position.

His conclusion would be milder: this is a company worth tracking over the long term, but now is an "observation period," not an "entry period."

Four-person panel split: gross margin is the misread piece

Gross margin was the only point of contention. The working paper framed it as "a timing gap on low-cost inventory," but the longer series tells a different story: 41.2% in 2021, 41.9% in 2022, 41.9% in 2023, 41.2% in 2024, 37.6% in 2025 and 41.3% in 2026H1. 41%–42% is the company's norm; 37.6% in 2025 is the outlier.

That correction trims the bear case slightly and adds modestly to the bull case, but does not change the conclusion. The real peak sits not in gross margin but in revenue: Q1 tax-inclusive sales of about RMB 19 billion, a single quarter running at twice the whole of 2024. That demand was pulled forward, not incremental. Gross margin can normalise; revenue cannot.

On this the four agree: a valuation can be built on normal gross margin, not on peak revenue. Laopu's current P/E has peak revenue times normal gross margin as its denominator, and one of those two is bound to fall.

Conclusion

All four landed on the same call: do not buy, but keep it on the watchlist. A 2:1 payoff (34% downside, 77% upside) clears the bar for value investing; the issue is probability. Weighted 40% pessimistic, 40% neutral and 20% optimistic, the expected price is HKD 348.6, just +3.4% above the current price — not worth carrying -34% downside. The triggers are fixed: gold holds in a USD 4,000–4,300 range for a quarter with same-store sales turning positive, or the share price falls below HKD 250 (12% above the HKD 222 pessimistic case). Recalculate if either occurs.
