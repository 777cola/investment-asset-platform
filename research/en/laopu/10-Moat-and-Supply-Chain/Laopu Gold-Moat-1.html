Moat — Laopu Gold

Laopu Gold (06181) | Moat

Core call: the real moat is 45 mall locations, not traditional gold craftsmanship

Score the moat across five components: brand 7, craftsmanship 7, channel 8, supply chain 5 and pricing power 6, for a weighted score of 6.6 — adequate rather than deep. The truly hard thing to replicate is the location of 45 stores, not the ancient-method gold craftsmanship. The craftsmanship carries no patents, and Shuibei can ship in a week. Ground-floor space in top-tier malls is in limited supply, and whoever occupies it first is hard to displace.

Supply chain scores 5, the only negative: RMB 19 billion of inventory and 271 days of turnover make it a source of profit when gold prices rise and a burden when they fall. The moat here protects the customer base, not the gold price.

Position in the chain: stuck at the "brand" link, squeezed from both sides

Upstream is gold material from the Shanghai Gold Exchange at a given price, with zero bargaining power. Downstream is floor space in top-tier malls, with a 15%–25% commission and location allocated by the mall. Further downstream are consumers, who pay per piece and do have bargaining power, but are swayed by gold price expectations. Laopu sits in the middle, earning the processing fee and brand premium from "turning gold material into a symbol."

The upside of this position is asset-light operations (all stores leased, no owned mines), high gross margin (41.3%) and replicability (validated by the Singapore store). The downside is that neither end is set by the company: it does not set the gold price, it does not set the location, and the only thing it does set is "whether consumers are willing to pay more for this symbol." That variable gave a negative answer in the second quarter of 2026.

Put another way: it is a company that monetizes a long gold position through retail channels, and the brand is the distribution tool for that position. That sentence is unflattering, but it explains why gross margin swings between 37.6% and 41.3% while net margin can jump from 17.3% to 21.5%.

In-house workshops safeguard quality rather than lower cost

Laopu keeps engraving, filigree and inlay work in-house rather than outsourcing to Shuibei. That buys two things: quality stays controllable and craft techniques do not leak out. The trade-off is capacity and unit cost—both run above those of contract manufacturing.

It therefore cannot fight a price war on supply-chain efficiency; in a real fight, Shuibei's costs are half its own. The premium can only rest on "others cannot produce this effect." Hence the 2–3 price increases a year: the hikes are not just a hedge against the gold price, but also a way to sustain the "scarcity" narrative.

Supply chain scores 5 points; the deduction is not for the workshop but for inventory. RMB 19 billion of inventory leaves gold-price exposure at several times net assets, and that exposure carries no hedging instrument (the company has not disclosed use of gold leasing or futures hedging). This is a deliberate choice, not an oversight—hedging would lock out upside gains, and management clearly prefers to bet on direction.

Competitive comparison: smallest scale, highest efficiency

Line the five companies up in one table and Laopu's profile is unmistakable: fewest stores, highest revenue per store, highest gross margin, fastest growth and the heaviest dependence on the gold price.

The store-level efficiency gap is the most striking. Laopu generates RMB 27.3 billion of revenue from 45 stores, or roughly RMB 600 million annualised per store; Chow Tai Fook's 5,305 mainland stores generate about RMB 86 billion, or about RMB 16 million per store; Lao Feng Xiang's RMB 52.8 billion spread across thousands of outlets works out to about RMB 10 million per store. Laopu's store-level efficiency is four to six times theirs.

High efficiency, however, does not equal a deep moat. These brands are not really competing on the same battlefield: Chow Tai Fook sits in Wanda plazas and pedestrian streets, Lao Feng Xiang in community retail districts, Laopu in SKP and MixC. They are not fighting for the same money from the same customers, but for first-floor space in the same malls. Chow Tai Fook can push its "Heritage" collection of ancient-method gold across 5,305 stores at once and spread marketing costs over scale; Laopu can only go deep in 45 locations. Scale advantages become more visible in a downturn — closing stores, clearing inventory and fighting for foot traffic all depend on store density.

The real threat isn't Chow Tai Fook — it's the gold price

Chow Tai Fook's "Heritage" collection is genuinely gaining mindshare in traditional gold craft. In fiscal 2026 it lifted net profit 52.2% to HKD 9.004 billion on a 32.3% gross margin by closing nearly a thousand stores, raising prices and upgrading product. But Chow Tai Fook is winning volume in the mid-price band, not Laopu's core customer: Laopu's average ticket is RMB 30,000–100,000, versus RMB 5,000–30,000 for Chow Tai Fook's mainstay.

The real threat to this business is the gold price. As gold fell from 5,419 to 4,000, three things hit at once — inventory impairment, gross margin compression and negative same-store sales — and management controls none of them. The second-biggest danger is aesthetic fatigue: traditional gold craft has been hot for three years, and no one knows what the next trend will be.

A third competitor is easily overlooked: recyclers. Gold jewellery has a second-hand market, and recycling is priced by weight. A traditional gold bangle bought for RMB 35,000 is worth only its gold value on recycling (about RMB 20,000); brand premium goes to zero in the second-hand market. A consumption downgrade amplifies this — consumers start asking whether it holds its value, and the answer is no. This is the hardest soft spot in Laopu's luxury narrative.

Conclusion

Moat assessment: 6.6 points — adequate but not deep. Channel position protects the customer base and brand premium protects gross margin, but supply chain and gold-price exposure are structural weaknesses. Assigning a premium to the locations of 45 stores is reasonable; assigning one on the view that "the brand can withstand the gold price" is not. That is why the valuation deserves 10x, not 20x.
