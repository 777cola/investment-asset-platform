03 Moat anatomy: the wall is in the channel, not the IP — Pop Mart

676 stores · 82.44 million members · 51.6% repurchase rate · 37/60 points
Research date 2026/09/07  ·  Price HK$156.20 (+1.43%)  ·  Market cap About HKD 209 billion  ·  P/E (TTM) Approximately 14.2x

Core view

Break the moat into six parts: channel, membership, IP, scale, brand, supply chain. Total score 37/60. The conclusion is clear: the wall lies in channel and membership (9 points, 8 points), not in IP and brand (7 points, 6 points). Defensiveness therefore rests on the "retail infrastructure," not on "consumers having no alternative."

Channel: 676 stores, built up over a decade

676 company-operated stores, 221 overseas (90 in Asia-Pacific, 86 in the Americas, 45 in Europe and elsewhere), and 2,827 robot vending machines. The value of this network lies not in its size but in its locations — prime retail space in core commercial districts is in limited supply, and whoever occupies it first is not easily displaced.

What would it take to replicate this network? A rough estimate: fit-out, deposits and initial inventory for one store run about RMB 3–5 million, so 676 stores would cost RMB 2–3.4 billion; add site-selection teams, local operations and lease negotiations, and it would take at least five years. Money can solve half of it; time solves the other half. That is the reason for the 9 score.

But the channel has a weakness: it can move goods, but it cannot create demand. The more stores there are, the higher inventory piles up if the goods do not sell. In the first half of 2026, stores rose by 46 and inventory turnover lengthened by 78 days — a live demonstration of this logic.

Members: 82.44 million and 92.9% of sales

The membership system is the channel's "software layer": 82.44 million registered domestic members, 92.9% of mainland revenue from members and a 51.6% repurchase rate form a closed loop—store acquisition → App retention → blind-box machine repurchase → limited-edition activation.

The moat sits in the data. The company knows which IPs each member has bought, which categories they prefer, how often they buy and what prices they are sensitive to. That data enables "precision launches"—pushing new IPs first to the people most likely to want them, which lowers the failure rate. Pure offline retailers cannot replicate this.

An 8 rather than a 10, because members are loyal to the IP, not the platform. Two consecutive quarters without a good IP and members go silent without cancelling—"silent churn" is more dangerous than cancellation because it never shows up in the data.

IP and brand: why only 7 and 6 points

IP scores 7 because the matrix is real: 11 IPs exceed RMB 100 million, 6 surpass RMB 1 billion, Twinkle Twinkle goes from RMB 389 million to RMB 2.65 billion in a year, and MOLLY at 20 still does RMB 900 million. Only a handful of toy companies worldwide have this incubation capability.

But no higher, because "incubation capability" is not "asset duration." Labubu went from breakout to negative growth in 18 months; MOLLY is down -33.6% YoY. Its IPs are consumables, not assets. Assets appreciate; consumables run out.

Brand scores 6 because it is the absolute leader within designer toys and fails outside that circle. Ask a 40-year-old Chinese consumer "Do you know Pop Mart?" — most likely yes; ask "Do you like it?" — most likely no. That is the difference between awareness and brand power. Lego, Disney and Jellycat cut across ages; Pop Mart does not yet.

The truth about moats: enough to hold for three years, not ten

On a three-year view, Pop Mart is near-invincible: 676 stores, 100 million members, a 69.7% gross margin and RMB 12.4 billion in cash. No new entrant could replicate that within three years.

On a ten-year view, it is not fundamentally different from Funko in 2015. Both depend on hit products, both depend on channels, and both carry high gross margins. Funko was worth USD 1.2 billion in 2018 and fell to USD 300 million in 2023; revenue barely declined, but profit vanished. The cause: licensed IP expiring, plus consumer fatigue with the aesthetic. Pop Mart owns its IP, so the first risk is small — but the second is the same.

The moat verdict: it can keep the company alive, but cannot guarantee sustained high growth. The former is worth 12–15x P/E, the latter 25x. The market currently prices it between the two.

Appendix: How it falls short of Lego

Lego is the only company in the global toy industry to have survived 90 years, generating 2025 revenue of about DKK 90 billion at a net margin of around 20%. Set against Pop Mart, it illustrates the difference between a "brand" and a "hit-making machine."

First, Lego sells a "system"; Pop Mart sells "characters." Lego bricks connect to one another—the more you buy, the more fun it gets, a genuine network effect. Pop Mart's figures are standalone; buying ten of them doesn't let you snap them together.

Second, Lego has "generational inheritance"; Pop Mart does not. Parents play with Lego and so do their children—the same set of bricks passes through two generations. Pop Mart's customers cluster in the 15–30 age range and churn once they age out.

Third, Lego's IP runs on two engines, "in-house + licensed"; Pop Mart is "mostly in-house." Lego combines proprietary lines such as City and Technic with licences such as Star Wars and Harry Potter, giving it stronger risk resilience.

The gap is not in gross margin (Pop Mart's 69.7% is higher) but in "asset duration." Lego's brand is a hundred-year asset; Pop Mart's IP is a three-year consumable. That is the difference between 15x and 25x P/E.

Conclusion

Moat 37/60: Distribution 9 and membership 8 are genuine walls; IP 7 and scale 7 are operating advantages; brand 6 and supply chain 6 can be bypassed at any time. Such walls can defend the existing business, but not "a decade of high growth." Investment implication: own it as a consumer stock, not a growth stock.
