01 Cognitive reset: Pop Mart is not a toy company but an "attention arbitrage" play — Pop Mart

Core view

The market holds three misconceptions about Pop Mart: that it is a toy company, that it is China's Disney, and that its growth depends on Labubu. All three are wrong. Pop Mart is an "attention arbitrage" business: it produces characters cheaply to bet on high-probability social virality, then converts that virality into retail revenue. Only on that reading does it make sense that the stock can double in a year and just as easily halve in a year.

Misconception 1: It is not a toy company

Toy companies run gross margins of 40%–50% (Hasbro about 48%, Mattel about 47% in 2025), built on IP licensing plus channel distribution; they earn the money of "manufacturing + distribution." Pop Mart's 69.7% gross margin, 20 percentage points higher, shows it is not earning manufacturing money.

It earns the money of "pricing power." A blind box sells for RMB 59 to 69 and costs less than RMB 20; a plush sells for RMB 199 to 599 and costs less than RMB 60. Of what consumers pay, 70% is "I like this character." That premium is not manufactured; it is manufactured by social spread.

So its competitors are not toy factories but "everything competing for young people's attention" — short video, games, milk tea, concerts. It does not sell toys; it sells "a moment you can post to Moments."

Misconception 2: It is not a "Chinese Disney"

Disney's core asset is "stories that can be retold": Mickey Mouse is 90 years old, the princesses 80, Marvel 60. A story can be remade, sequelised, turned into a theme park and merchandised into derivatives — the same IP monetised over and over.

Pop Mart's IPs have no story. Labubu has no film, no lines, no world-building; it is simply a figure that "looks a little bad and a little cute." A figure's life cycle depends on the attention cycle of social media, not on narrative. These are two entirely different asset durations.

Management knows this, which is why it is filling the gap: developing a live-action/animated LABUBU film with Sony Pictures, opening four new zones at its City Park, and moving into desserts and accessories. All are attempts to "turn a figure into a story" — the right direction, but it will take five to ten years. Calling it China's Disney today mistakes an option for an asset.

Misconception 3: growth depends not on Labubu but on the "hit-product probability"

2025 revenue reached RMB 37.12 billion, up 184.7% YoY, with THE MONSTERS (Labubu) contributing over RMB 10 billion. Hence the claim that "Pop Mart lives on Labubu." That is half right—2025 did depend on it, yet in 1H26 THE MONSTERS fell 7.5% while total revenue still grew 23.8%.

The real growth mechanism: launch several hundred SKUs a year, 3–5 of which become hits; revenue from the hits covers the cost of every failed SKU and leaves a large surplus. This is a probability business, not a single-product business. Pop Mart's skill is not "making Labubu" but "producing Labubu-caliber luck every year."

The mechanism has a mathematical signature: revenue swings with the number of hits, but gross margin stays stable. Failed SKUs cost little (design fees plus sampling fees), while successful SKUs earn extremely high profit. So revenue surges and slumps while gross margin holds at 69%–70%. Grasp this and you will not infer from "Labubu has cooled" that "the company is finished."

After the cognitive reset, what questions should be asked?

If you don't treat it as a toy company, what should you look at? Three metrics.

First, hit output rate. In the first half, 11 IPs exceeded RMB 100 million and 6 broke RMB 1 billion, versus 8 exceeding RMB 100 million last year. The number is rising, so the output rate is intact. Second, output per store. Annualised RMB 50.8 million, down 14% from RMB 58.92 million last year. The number is falling, so efficiency in converting hits into revenue is declining. Third, member repurchase. 51.6%, with 92.9% of domestic sales from members. The number is holding steady, so the core base is intact.

Of the three metrics, two are fine and one is deteriorating. That is the quantitative version of "good company, bad timing."

Appendix: Three halvings, three different causes

In six years as a listed company it has weathered three sharp selloffs, each triggered by a completely different cause, and all three are worth remembering.

The first, 2021–2022. It listed at HKD 38.5, surged above HKD 100 in 2021, then fell back to around HKD 10 on fading blind-box hype, tighter regulation and pandemic store closures. The market woke up from the "first stock of designer toys" narrative and realised it was just a toy retailer with high gross margins. The lesson: a narrative can carry you for a year, not three.

The second, 2023. Domestic same-store sales declined, overseas was only just starting, and the market worried that growth had peaked. The stock traded sideways around HKD 20 for more than a year. Labubu rescued it—a breakout in Southeast Asia in 2024, then globally in 2025.

The third, 2026. From HKD 288.2 in February to HKD 156.2 in September, -45.8%. The cause was not a collapse of the narrative but a genuine slowdown in results: overseas -11.1%, net profit +10.1%, inventory at 201 days.

What the three have in common: when the stock falls, the market says "this time is different"; when it rises, the market says the same. In reality every time is the same—its valuation swings with hit-product expectations, and hit products never arrive on schedule.

Conclusion

Cognitive reset, concluded: this is a company that "monetizes attention through a retail network." The retail network is the asset; attention is the traffic. Assets can compound; traffic cannot be controlled. Valuation should therefore be framed as "consumer stock plus a traffic option," not as a "growth stock." 14x TTM is the price of a consumer stock; 17x 2026E is the price with the option added—and the latter is no longer cheap.
