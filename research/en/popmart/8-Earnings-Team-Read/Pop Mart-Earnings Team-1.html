Core view

I pay little attention to quarterly figures, but two things in this interim report stand out: first, the domestic business is still +47.3%, which tells us the franchise is intact; second, overseas is -11.1%, which tells us part of last year's strength was luck. Wang Ning himself said "one good thing covers a hundred flaws," and that line matters more than any number in the report.

Look at the business first, then the numbers

The first page of the earnings report shows revenue of RMB 17.17 billion, up 23.8% YoY. Many fixate on this number and conclude that growth has fallen off. That view is wrong. Last year's 184.7% growth was never the norm—it came from one Labubu character going viral globally, which was luck. This year's 23.8% was carried by 47.3% growth in China, and that is the business.

Business quality comes down to two things: gross margin and repeat purchases. Gross margin was 69.7%, down only 0.6pp, which shows pricing power remains; 82.44 million domestic members contributed 92.9% of revenue, with a 51.6% repurchase rate, which shows existing customers are still buying. With these two in place, the business is intact.

But output per store fell 14% (rough calculation: annualised RMB 58.92 million per store in 2025 versus RMB 50.80 million annualised in the first half of 2026). That number shows growth was bought by opening stores, not by existing stores selling more. How long can store openings continue? They stop once locations run out of foot traffic. This is the first thing I will watch.

Ignore the 10.1% profit figure; understand what ate into it

Net profit grew just 10.1%, 13.7pp slower than revenue. The notes to the financials explain why: a RMB 720 million FX loss against a RMB 120 million gain last year, an RMB 840 million swing in and out. None of this reflects the underlying business — it is the book impact of money moving across borders. Strip it out and profit growth is 28%, ahead of revenue.

The expense lines that actually matter are elsewhere: rent +43.3%, employee compensation +45.7%, with the sales team expanding from 6,219 to 9,734 people. This is deliberate spending, not a loss of control. Opening stores requires leasing space and hiring staff; cash goes out first and revenue follows. Whether the math works hinges on whether these stores can reach RMB 50 million in unit output within two years. If they do, it is a good investment; if not, the money is wasted.

What I don't like is the decision not to pay an interim dividend. The company holds RMB 12.44 billion in cash, zero borrowings, and spent HKD 1.74 billion on buybacks in the first half — it has the money. No dividend means it wants to keep the cash to open stores — not wrong in itself, but the stores it opens need to make money.

The last line: Wang Ning's "benfen"

Duan Yongping has talked about "benfen" for twenty years. It does not mean honesty; it means "do the right things, do things right," and above all "stop doing the wrong things."

What Wang Ning did this time is stop doing the wrong things. Last year, chasing the demand surge, stores queued every day and sold out, and the supply chain broke down — what he calls "one good thing covering a hundred ugly ones." This year he has voluntarily cut the growth target from 30% to 20%, saying "boosting sales is not the primary goal," and wants to shore up the organisation, supply chain and cross-border management. A founder willing to say "let's slow down first" when the share price has halved — that is benfen.

But benfen cannot be used as a valuation. Good business, reliable people — the price still has to be right. HKD 156 is not cheap; below HKD 120 is negotiable.

Conclusion

Duan Yongping would give this earnings report an 8: the business is intact, management is doing the right things, and the balance sheet has no shortage of cash. Points are deducted for declining output per store and negative overseas growth—both show that "luck" has run out and skill must carry the day from here. His move would be "keep holding, no additions," and reconsider below HKD 120.
