Masterclass Close Read (Buffett): A pretty income statement, an honest cash flow statement — Pop Mart

Core view

Buffett's order for reading financial statements is: cash flow statement first, then balance sheet, and income statement last. Reading Pop Mart's interim report in that order leads to a different conclusion from those who look only at the income statement — the profit is real, but the cash has not kept up.

Cash flow statement: RMB 5 billion profit, only RMB 3.5 billion cash

First-half net profit was RMB 5.038 billion, but cash fell from RMB 13.78 billion to RMB 12.44 billion. Dividends of RMB 3.15 billion and buybacks of about RMB 1.55 billion in the same period totaled RMB 4.7 billion. Rough math: 5.038 - 4.7 = RMB 340 million, so cash should have risen slightly; instead it fell by RMB 1.34 billion. The roughly RMB 1.7 billion gap sits in inventory and working capital. Net cash flow to net profit is about 0.7x.

This is not fraud; it is the cost of expansion. Inventory turnover went from 123 days to 201 days, which, on first-half operating cost of RMB 5.2 billion, ties up about RMB 1.1 billion of extra goods. Overseas stores grew from 185 to 221; new stores need stocking and payment terms, pushing up receivables and prepayments.

But Buffett would press one question: is this cost one-off, or does it recur every year? If every year's store openings require two months of extra inventory, the company's free cash flow deserves a permanent 15% haircut. The test is whether inventory turnover can return to 150 days. If it can, the cost is one-off; if not, it is structural.

Income statement: what sustains the 29.3% net margin?

Net margin of 29.3% is top-tier among consumer goods. Breaking it down: gross margin 69.7%, selling expense ratio around 30% (derived), with G&A and finance costs consuming the remainder. This structure shows its money is spent mainly on "selling," not on "managing"—store rent, staff salaries, and online ad spend are all customer acquisition costs.

Buffett would like this: a business with a 70% gross margin signals product premium; a 30% selling expense ratio means that premium must be sustained through continuous marketing. The former is the moat; the latter is the moat's maintenance cost. The higher the maintenance cost, the shallower the moat. Coca-Cola's selling expense ratio is about 25%, Moutai under 10%, Pop Mart 30%—making its premium the most fragile.

ROE roughly 35% (RMB 12.776 billion net profit / about RMB 36.5 billion net assets). That is a high number, but part of it comes from the leverage effect of "zero debt + high dividends," not purely operating returns.

Balance sheet: the cleanest statement in the whole set

Cash of RMB 12.44 billion, zero bank borrowings, debt-to-asset ratio of 24.9%, with liabilities mainly contract liabilities (prepaid blind-box payments) and accounts payable. What this structure means: it does business with customers' money and suppliers' money first, and keeps its own cash. This is the best liability structure for a consumer goods company.

The only problem is inventory. 201 days of goods, at cost roughly RMB 5.7 billion (rough calculation: half-year cost of sales RMB 5.2 billion × 201/180). If this inventory is cleared at a discount, at a 30% markdown the loss would be RMB 1.7 billion, equivalent to one-third of half-year profit. This is the real risk behind the "low PE."

Buffett would give this balance sheet a 9, the income statement a 7, and the cash flow statement a 6. Overall 7 — a good company, but not one you can buy with your eyes closed.

Conclusion

Buffett's conclusion: this is a company with "real profits, clean assets, and cash at a discount." He would not buy just because of a 14x PE, since the denominator is peak earnings; nor would he sell because of the overseas decline, since the balance sheet can hold up. He would wait—until inventory turnover falls back, the net cash ratio returns to 1x, or the price drops below 12x.
