Core call: Pop Mart can defend the store, but not the reason to walk in

The six dimensions score 37/60 in total. Channel at 9 and membership at 8 are genuine moats; brand at 6, IP at 7, supply chain at 6 and scale at 7 qualify only as operating advantages. The distinction matters: channel and membership take a decade and several billion to replicate, whereas brand and IP can be bypassed with a single hit product.

The most dangerous rivals are not Bloks or TOP TOY but Jellycat and "the next Labubu" — the former contests mindshare in plush (57.2% of revenue), the latter contests attention.

Position in the value chain: not a "brand owner" but a hybrid of "IP operator + retailer"

Viewed along the industry chain, Pop Mart does three things at once: signing IP (upstream), managing categories (midstream) and opening stores (downstream). These map to three numbers in the financials: artist IP revenue of RMB 15.29 billion, a gross margin of 69.7% and 676 stores.

The advantage of this "full-chain" structure is that it captures all the profit — from design to retail, no one else takes a cut. The drawback is that it must build capabilities at every link: signing IP takes judgment, managing categories takes supply chain, opening stores takes site selection and local marketing.Of the three, only store openings can be bought with money; signing IP cannot.

Its industry-chain position is therefore "strong in channels, weak upstream." To contract manufacturers it is the client (large orders); to top artists it is the counterparty (IP is scarce); to malls it is the client (foot traffic); to consumers it is "one of several substitutable options" (if they don't like the figure, they don't buy).

Supply chain: the flexibility of the OEM model became a liability in the plush era

In the figurine era, outsourcing was the perfect model: vinyl production was standardised, hundreds of factories across Guangdong could handle it, and the work went to the lowest bidder. Pop Mart avoided capex and still ran a 70% gross margin.

By 2026 the mix had shifted to plush (57.2% of the total), and the dynamics changed. Plush involves more process steps, lower yields and lead times 30–50% longer, and few factories can absorb large orders. In the first half, inventory turnover stretched from 123 days to 201 days, driven partly by stockpiling and partly by the fact that "plush capacity is fully booked."

Quality control is the bigger issue. Plush is a "feel business": a slightly off stitch, filling or fabric hand-feel is immediately obvious to consumers on touch. Contract manufacturers' yields are unstable, and the brand absorbs the returns and reputational damage. This is the price of "asset-light": you truly save when times are good, and you truly get hit when things go wrong.

The most dangerous rival: Jellycat, and every "next Labubu"

Start with Jellycat, a British plush brand with far deeper mindshare than Pop Mart in Western markets. Jellycat buyers do not think they are buying a trendy toy; they think they are buying "a companion." Plush already accounts for 57.2% of Pop Mart's revenue, and this is the front line.

Jellycat's advantage is that it has "no IP cycle." It does not depend on any single character going viral; it rests on a whole aesthetic system of "soft, quirky, cute," releasing new styles every year without retiring old ones. Pop Mart depends on "a character suddenly going viral." The two business models differ: one is a brand, the other a hit-making machine.

Then there are the intangible rivals: any character that suddenly goes viral on TikTok can replicate Labubu's path within three months. Trendy toys have no capacity barrier, no patent barrier, no channel exclusivity — malls welcome new brands, and contract manufacturers welcome new orders. The only barrier is attention, and attention cannot be inherited.

Domestic rivals are less dangerous. Bloks, TOP TOY and 52TOYS all compete on "cheaper"; they capture price-sensitive customers, not Pop Mart's core members. What could truly kill Pop Mart is a company better than it at manufacturing hits — none has appeared yet, but the bar is not high.

Moat verdict: defensible for three years, not ten

The six scores sum to 37/60, above the passing line but short of a "wide moat." Buffett's three-tier framework: wide moat (Coca-Cola, Moody's), narrow moat (See's Candies), no moat (most retailers). Pop Mart sits between "narrow" and "none," leaning narrow.

Where is the narrow part? Channels and members. 676 stores and 100 million members are not something rivals can replicate even by spending RMB 5 billion over three years. Management knows this, so in 2026 the money goes to store openings and member operations, not advertising.

Where is it not wide enough? Consumers walk into a store not for "Pop Mart" but because "this figure looks good." However strong the channel, it can only get "good-looking figures" onto shelves faster; if the figures are not good-looking, the stronger the channel, the higher the inventory. The 201-day inventory turnover in the first half of 2026 is proof of this logic.

One more quantifiable test: the moat's "maintenance cost." Pop Mart's selling expense ratio is about 30%, of which rent is +43.3% and compensation +45.7%; both are necessary spending to maintain channels and members. If it ever has to raise the selling expense ratio to 35% to sustain revenue, that means the moat is leaking. At 30% it is currently healthy, because stores are still in an expansion phase and the ratio will be diluted by new stores.

Conclusion

Moat 37/60: enough to defend for three years, not enough for ten. Over three years, channels and membership can sustain performance; over ten, durability hinges on two things it cannot control—when the next hit product arrives and where consumer attention goes. That is why it is worth 14x rather than 25x.
