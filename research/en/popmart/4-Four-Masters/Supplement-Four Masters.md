## Duan Yongping: already the second-largest shareholder, but he bought what he understands, not what will rise

Duan Yongping would start with one question: will this business still be here in ten years? Pop Mart's answer: the designer toy category will be here; this company may not. Blind boxes have been around for twenty years. Japan's gashapon, America's Funko and China's designer toy stores have all survived, so demand is real. But "which company survives" is a separate question. Funko has been listed for eight years; its share price has fallen from USD 30 to USD 5. Revenue is still there; profit is gone.

Interestingly, Duan Yongping himself is Pop Mart's second-largest shareholder. Hong Kong Stock Exchange disclosures show that H&H International, which he manages, raised its stake from 6.85% to 7.65% on July 6, holding 102 million shares. After the interim results came out on August 20, someone asked him on an investment platform whether overseas was too weak, and his answer was "as a shareholder, I actually think it's pretty good."

That sentence needs unpacking. By "pretty good" he means the business itself is not broken — gross margin of 69.7%, RMB 12.4 billion of cash on the books, and domestic still at +47%. He never said "this price is worth buying." In Duan Yongping's framework, "good company" and "good price" are two independent questions, and he only acts on the second. His July add-on was at the HKD 150–190 range, which is his own cost judgment, not advice for anyone else.

## Buffett: pricing power has migrated to the secondary market — and that is the crux

Buffett looks at consumer stocks for one thing only: whether they can raise prices without losing customers. See's Candies raised prices dozens of times between 1972 and 2007 with volumes barely falling; Coca-Cola has pushed prices above inflation and held its share. Pop Mart cannot, for a very specific reason — pricing power does not sit with the company; it sits with the resale market.

The economics of blind boxes work like this: you pay RMB 59 or 69 for a box, and if you pull a hidden-edition figure you can sell it on Xianyu for several hundred to over a thousand yuan. That premium sustains the gambling appeal of opening boxes; once the hidden-edition premium disappears, a full-price box becomes "RMB 59 for a plastic figurine" and demand drops immediately. In the first half of 2026, Labubu resale premiums narrowed markedly and THE MONSTERS revenue fell 7.5% over the same period — the two curves describe the same thing.

He would acknowledge the value of the channel and the membership base: 676 directly operated stores, 221 overseas, 100 million members, 92.9% of sales from members — real retail infrastructure. But Buffett's bar is higher: infrastructure is not a moat, because rivals can open stores, sign designers and buy traffic. A true moat means "consumers will accept no substitute," and trend-toy consumers' loyalty attaches to the character, not the company. Lego took sixty years to build "no substitute"; Pop Mart has had ten.

## Munger: invert the thinking — all three paths could halve it again

Munger would invert the question: what would have to happen for Pop Mart to fall from HKD 156 to HKD 80?

Path one: overseas revenue turns negative for four consecutive quarters. That is already under way. Q2 revenue turned negative YoY, with JPMorgan estimating -11% — the first single-quarter decline since it began covering the stock; Asia-Pacific online -39.8%, Americas online -45.6%, Shopee -62.1%. Online is a traffic business, and traffic that ebbs does not come back on its own.

Path two: domestic same-store sales turn negative. Not yet. Domestic revenue +47.3%, online +62.7%, the box-drawing machine app alone at RMB 2.06 billion. But domestic growth fell from 135.2% a year earlier to 47.3% in just four quarters. At that slope, it reaches single digits within another two quarters.

Path three: new IP incubation fails. Partly offset by Twinkle Twinkle — RMB 2.65 billion, +580.6%, moving from the fringe to the second-largest IP. But 580% growth itself signals an extremely low base; this time next year it will be lapping a RMB 2.65 billion base.

Munger's conclusion would not be "avoid," but "this price leaves no room for the worst case." The neutral case of HKD 150 already sits below the current price, meaning the market has taken "everything goes smoothly" as its base assumption.

## Li Lu: emotional consumption is a civilisation-level trend, but profit pools will shift between participants

In Li Lu's framework, "paying for emotion" is the most genuine offshoot of consumption upgrading. Once per-capita GDP passes USD 10,000, people stop buying function and start buying identity, companionship and surprise. The trend is unfolding in China, Southeast Asia and Latin America at the same time, so the industry should expand over the long run: Frost & Sullivan forecasts a 20.9% CAGR for China's designer toy market in 2024–2030, with retail sales of RMB 110.1 billion in 2026. The trend is real; there is no dispute on that point.

The dispute is over who captures the profit. Li Lu would benchmark against Disney, whose core asset is "characters that can be retold indefinitely." Mickey Mouse has been told for ninety years and every re-release is new revenue; theme parks, films and merchandise are repeated monetisation of the same IP. Pop Mart's characters have no story, only an image — Labubu has no film, no dialogue, no worldview, and its life cycle depends on the attention cycle of social media.

Pop Mart is filling this gap: a live-action/animated LABUBU film developed with Sony Pictures, LABUBU becoming the first Chinese original IP invited to the World Cup in its history, and four new zones at its city park built around THE MONSTERS and DIMOO. These moves point in the right direction, but the validation period is five to ten years, not two quarters.

So Li Lu would reach a very sober judgment: a good industry, a decent company, a valuation in a reasonable range — but "China's Disney" is currently only an option, not an asset. Paying full price for an option is the most common mistake in investing.

## Risk/reward: 36% downside, 44% upside, with a further 4% decline in between

I assign the three scenarios the following probabilities: pessimistic 30%, neutral 45%, optimistic 25%. Expected value = 100×0.3 + 150×0.45 + 225×0.25 = HKD 148.75, 4.8% below the current price of 156.2. In other words, this is a negative-expectation bet.

The problem is not that the optimistic scenario is too low (+44% is not low); it is that it requires two conditions to hold at once: Xingxingren turning RMB 2.65 billion into more than RMB 4 billion, while same-store output at the 86 stores in the Americas stops declining. The correlation between the two is very low—one depends on IP operation, the other on localized retail—and the probability of both succeeding is no more than 1/4.

From another angle: if the stock falls to HKD 120, the neutral scenario of HKD 150 offers 25% upside, the pessimistic HKD 100 is only -17%, and the expected value of 148.75 versus HKD 120 is +24%. Same company, same judgment, a 23% price difference flips the odds. That is the whole meaning of "wait for a pullback"—not bearish, but waiting for the price to compensate for the probabilities.

One more point on probabilities: if the neutral scenario's probability were 55% rather than 45% (that is, a higher probability of "overseas stabilization"), the expected value would rise to HKD 158, exactly equal to the current price. In other words, the current price implies the assumption that "the neutral scenario has a probability of 55% or more." That is where the market is more optimistic than you, and where you should not follow the optimism.
