Investment view: good company, bad timing — wait for price or wait for evidence

Pop Mart reported H1 2026 revenue of RMB 17.173 billion (+23.8%) and net profit attributable to shareholders of RMB 5.038 billion (+10.1%), both below consensus. Overseas revenue fell 11.1%, its first decline, and the Labubu family was down 7.5%, marking the end of the single-product breakout phase. At HKD 156.2 the stock trades on 14.2x P/E (TTM), which looks like a consumer-stock multiple, but the denominator captures peak profit; on 2026E it is about 17x.

Our three-scenario valuation of HKD 100 / 150 / 225 implies an expected value of HKD 148.75, below the current price. Rating: "Watch, wait for pullback," entry point HKD 120.

I. Company Overview: From Grocery Distributor to IP Operator

Pop Mart was founded in 2010 and listed on the Hong Kong Stock Exchange in December 2020. Its model is "IP operation + direct retail": it signs artists, develops characters, turns them into blind boxes, figures and plush toys, and sells through directly operated stores, Roboshops and online blind-box machines. As of June 2026, it operated 676 stores globally (221 overseas) and 2,827 Roboshops across more than 20 countries and regions.

2025 revenue was RMB 37.12 billion (+184.7%) and net profit RMB 12.776 billion (+308.8%), a peak year driven by Labubu's global breakout. First-half 2026 revenue was RMB 17.173 billion and net profit RMB 5.038 billion, with growth slowing to 23.8% and 10.1%. Founder Wang Ning holds 49.14%.

On ownership, Wang Ning holds 49.14% in aggregate through trusts and holding platforms, Duan Yongping's H&H International 7.65% and southbound capital 24.46%, with the remainder held by institutions and retail investors. This structure shapes two features of corporate governance: fast decision-making and weak external checks. Good for long-term shareholders, a risk for minority shareholders.

By product mix, plush already accounts for 57.2% and figures 30.2%, with the rest MEGA, derivatives and licensing. This shift in mix is the single most important thing in 2026—it means Pop Mart has moved from "desktop ornaments" into the "cushion doll" market, and its rival has changed from 52TOYS to Jellycat.

II. Industry analysis: a RMB 110.1 billion pool as growth shifts gears

China's designer toy retail sales are forecast to reach RMB 110.1 billion in 2026, implying a 2024–2030 CAGR of 20.9% (Frost & Sullivan); the global art toy market will reach USD 6.94 billion in 2026, a CAGR of 11.6% (CMI). The industry is still growing, but the driver has shifted from penetration gains to IP rotation.

The landscape is one dominant player and several strong challengers: Pop Mart at RMB 17.173 billion in the first half, Bloks at roughly RMB 2.4 billion annualized, TOP TOY at RMB 985 million, 52TOYS at about RMB 600 million, and Kayou monopolizing cards at 71.1%.In 2026 everyone decelerates: Pop Mart +23.8%, Bloks +2.7%, TOP TOY +32.7% but loss-making. Competition shifts from who opens stores fastest to whose IP has more staying power.

Splitting the industry into three segments makes it clearer. Upstream is IP and design, where global supply is increasing—independent artists, licensing companies and film and television IP are all piling in; midstream is manufacturing, and China has the world's most complete toy OEM system, so capacity is not scarce; downstream is channels, where online traffic has peaked and offline store rents are rising.Of the three, only upstream top-tier IP is scarce; everything else is in oversupply. Profit will therefore keep concentrating upstream, while downstream retailers survive on scale and efficiency.

Slower industry growth does not mean demand is disappearing—it reflects a high base. The whole industry surged in 2025 on Labubu, so 2026 is up against the same month that sold explosively last year.On a two-year CAGR basis the industry still grows around 20%, and that is the true demand growth rate. The call on the industry is therefore a gear shift, not a peak.

III. Business model: what is inside a RMB 59 box

Unit economics: retail price RMB 59–69 against cost below RMB 20 (contract manufacturing 12–15, design 2–3, packaging and logistics 4), a 70% gross margin. The premium consumers pay rests on three things — the uncertainty of opening the box, the urge to complete the set, and the social currency of a WeChat Moments post.

The key to this business is not "making good-looking figures" but "controlling the rhythm of scarcity." Hidden-figure probability is 1/144, and secondary-market premiums support primary demand; release volume and the premium disappears, taking demand with it. In 2025 the company released volume to capture global virality, at the cost of 201 days of inventory turnover in 2026.

Channels are self-operated, so retail price equals ex-factory price with no distributor margin. 82.44 million members, contributing 92.9% of mainland China revenue at a 51.6% repurchase rate, form a closed loop of "store acquisition → App retention → box-draw machine repurchase."

The fragility of this model lies in "probability." A series has 12 figures, and the company does not know which one will take off; of several hundred SKUs a year, only 3 to 5 become hits. Revenue from hits must cover the cost of all failed SKUs and still leave a 70% gross margin — which means a hit must be a "super hit." Labubu sold more than RMB 10 billion in 2025, a sample of exactly this "super hit." The problem is that super hits cannot be replicated, only waited for.

One layer further: plush RMB 9.825 billion (+60%), figures RMB 5.192 billion (+0.3%). Figures have stalled, showing that the "classic blind box" format is saturated; plush has exploded, showing that consumers want something "huggable, postable, giftable." The company's ability to shift capacity and shelf space within six months reflects organizational capability.

IV. Financial analysis: real profit, weak cash, high inventory

Revenue: domestic RMB 12.201 billion (+47.3%) held up; overseas RMB 4.972 billion (-11.1%) was the drag, with Asia-Pacific -9.7%, the Americas -16.5% and Europe +5.9%. Online was the weakest channel: Asia-Pacific online -39.8%, the Americas -45.6%, Shopee -62.1%.

Profit: gross margin 69.7% (-0.6pct), net margin 29.3% (-3.7pct). An FX loss of RMB 720 million (prior year +RMB 120 million) was the largest single item; lease costs +43.3% and compensation +45.7% reflected deliberate investment. Excluding FX, profit grew about 28%.

Cash: cash fell from RMB 13.78 billion to RMB 12.44 billion, after RMB 3.15 billion of dividends and about RMB 1.55 billion of buybacks; net cash ratio about 0.7x. Inventory turnover lengthened from 123 to 201 days, tying up roughly RMB 1.1 billion on a rough estimate.Across all three statements: the company is not about to die and remains a good business, but its cash flow deserves a discount.

One more angle: combining 2025 and the first half of 2026 gives two-year compound revenue growth of 62%. That is a fairer figure than the 23.8% standalone number — it strips out the base effect and shows the company is still expanding fast, just no longer doubling every year.On valuation, 62% two-year compound growth against 17x P/E implies a PEG of about 0.3, which looks cheap; but growth in the second half of 2026 could turn negative, and the PEG denominator would instantly fail.That is the difficulty of valuing cyclical stocks: which growth rate you use as the denominator changes the conclusion entirely.

A note on the cash flow basis: this report does not break out the change in contract liabilities, because the interim summary does not disclose the detail.But contract liabilities are key to understanding this company's cash flow — blind-box machines collect payment before shipping, so a rise in advance receipts lifts operating cash flow. If advance receipts recover in the second half, the net cash ratio will repair itself; if they fall, it means even the willingness to pay upfront is weakening.

V. Management: Wang Ning's "Benfen" and one-share dominance

Wang Ning, born in 1987, founded the company in 2010 and signed MOLLY in 2016, completing the shift from channel to IP. In 2026 he voluntarily cut the growth target from 30% to no less than 20%, acknowledging that last year "one good thing covered up a hundred flaws," and announced an HKD 2–5 billion buyback. Speaking honestly after the share price has halved is the hardest evidence of management quality.

Capital allocation: 2025 final dividend of RMB 3.15 billion (payout ratio 24.6%); H1 2026 buyback of 11.22 million shares for HKD 1.74 billion (HKD 140.9–194.9); new-format investment of RMB 1–2 billion a year. No major M&A and no equity raising at the highs.

Governance: a 49.14% stake, no AB shares and no counterbalancing force. The upside is fast decisions; the downside is that strategic correction rests on one person. We found no financial restatements, regulatory penalties or major shareholder selldowns.

Scoring management within a "capital allocation" framework: dividends (24.6% payout) 8 points, buyback (first large-scale programme, imprecise on price) 7 points, M&A (zero) 9 points, new-business investment (restrained) 7 points, organisational adjustment (proactive) 8 points. Composite 7.8, upper tier among Hong Kong-listed new consumer names. The main deduction is buyback timing — the portion bought at HKD 190 is now 20% underwater, showing management's read on "expensive or not" is no better than the market's.

One more governance detail: the company has no AB shares and no concert-party agreement, so Wang Ning's control rests entirely on his shareholding. If he trims below 30% in future, control becomes a variable. There is no selldown on record — he is buying back instead — so this risk need not be priced for now.

Six, growth drivers: four legs, three running

Domestic stores: 676 stores, 455 in China, with room to expand into lower-tier cities. Domestic sales rose 47.3% in the first half — the most certain growth driver. Overseas stores: 221, with 86 in the Americas, 45 in Europe and 90 in Asia-Pacific; the European headquarters is in London, with flagship stores planned for Milan, London, Paris and New York. The direction is right, but sales per store are falling. New IP: Twinkle Twinkle RMB 2.65 billion (+580.6%), CRYBABY RMB 1.633 billion (+34%); the matrix is broadening. New formats: theme parks, desserts, accessories and film, RMB 553 million (+111.7%), 3.2% of revenue.

Of the four legs, domestic stores and new IP are running, overseas stores are shifting gears, and new formats are still climbing. Growth in 2027 hinges mainly on whether overseas can halt its decline — at 29% of revenue, it is the biggest swing factor.

Returns on capital differ sharply across the four legs. Domestic stores: RMB 3–5 million investment per store, RMB 50 million annual sales, payback in 12–18 months — the best business. Overseas stores: higher investment per store (fit-out + local compliance + initial inventory), annual sales of only RMB 45 million (rough estimate), payback in 24–36 months, returns halved. New IP: near-zero marginal cost, the highest return, but success is uncontrollable. New formats: unit economics unproven, effectively an option. Capital should therefore go first to domestic stores and new IP; overseas should slow.

VII. Competitive landscape: the moat lies in channels, not IP

Moat score 37/60: channel 9, membership 8, IP 7, scale 7, brand 6, supply chain 6. Channel and membership are genuine barriers (676 stores, 100 million members); IP and brand are merely operational advantages. The distinction: channel takes a decade and billions to replicate, whereas IP can be leapfrogged by a single hit product.

Competitors: Bloks (building sets, licensed IP, 55% gross margin), TOP TOY (multi-brand stores, 40% gross margin, loss-making), 52TOYS (hardcore, 4.7% net margin), Kayou (cards 71.1%), Jellycat (top of mind in plush). Domestic rivals are chasing price-sensitive customers; the real threat is Jellycat and "the next Labubu."

View the moat on a timeline: within three years, channel and membership can hold off all Chinese competitors; within five years, Jellycat and Lego will erode its brand premium in Europe and the US; within ten years, its fate hinges on one question — "can it keep an IP alive for ten years?" MOLLY has lived 20 years, the only good evidence; Labubu has lived 18 months, the only bad evidence. Too few samples to conclude — hence the score of 6, not 8.

One further cross-reference: Pop Mart's gross margin is 69.7%, above Hasbro (about 48%), Mattel (about 47%), Bloks (about 55%) and MINISO (about 40%), and below Moutai (91%). That positioning places its pricing power "above toys, below luxury." The corresponding fair P/E should likewise sit "between toys (12x) and luxury (25x)."

8. Risk warning: two already materialized, four on the way

Already visible: ① IP lifecycle — THE MONSTERS -7.5%, MOLLY -33.6%; ② overseas localisation — revenue -11.1%, overseas operating margin down 14pct to 30%.

Under way: ③ inventory impairment (201 days, RMB 5.7 billion of inventory value); ④ FX (first half -RMB 720 million); ⑤ regulation (US blind-box legislation advancing); ⑥ valuation (2026E 17x vs 10% growth).

Three data points would prove us wrong: domestic same-store sales turn negative, gross margin falls below 66%, or overseas declines exceed 20% for two consecutive quarters. Trigger any one, and we exit unconditionally.

One more risk that rarely gets mentioned: blockbuster self-cannibalisation. When a company launches hundreds of SKUs a year, new IPs siphon sales from old ones. In the first half, THE MONSTERS fell 7.5% and MOLLY 33.6% — part natural fade, part shelf space and budget ceded to Hirono and CRYBABY. That cannibalisation stays invisible while total revenue grows; once total revenue stalls, it becomes a stampede.

Nine, earnings forecast: net profit of RMB 11bn in 2026

Assumptions: H2 2026 revenue -22% (Q3 -35%, Q4 -20%, referencing JPMorgan's view but assuming the company controls costs); full-year revenue RMB 35.2 billion (-5.2%); gross margin 69.5%; net margin 31.3% (recovering in the H2 peak season); net profit RMB 11.0 billion (-13.9%).

2027 assumes overseas stops declining and domestic +15%, revenue RMB 38.2 billion (+8.5%), net margin 31.4%, net profit RMB 12.0 billion (+9.1%). The key variable in this forecast is overseas, not domestic — domestic growth of 47.3%, even if it falls to 15%, can still hold up revenue; if overseas continues to decline by double digits, RMB 11.0 billion cannot be defended.

Sensitivity: every 5% change in revenue moves net profit by about 8% (operating leverage of about 1.6x); every 1 percentage point change in gross margin moves net profit by about 6%. In other words, if H2 2026 revenue is another 10% worse, net profit would fall 16%, turning RMB 11.0 billion into RMB 9.2 billion and P/E from 17.4x to 20.7x. This is why "growth" matters more than "margin" — it is the fulcrum of the leverage.

One more probability judgment: the probability that 2026 net profit lands in the RMB 9.5–11.0 billion range is about 60%, the probability of below RMB 9.5 billion is 25%, and the probability of above RMB 11.0 billion is 15%. This distribution is "right-skewed" — downside risk exceeds upside potential, because the overseas decline is certain while a new IP breakout is uncertain. The point of forecasting is not precision, but turning "possible" into "probability."

10. Valuation: 14x is an illusion, 17x is the reality

Market cap HKD 209bn (HKD 156.2 × 1.342bn shares), TTM net profit RMB 13.474bn, P/E 14.2x. Note the TTM base includes the 2025 H2 profit peak of RMB 8.436bn; on 2026E net profit of RMB 11bn, P/E is 17.4x.

Comparables: Miniso c.9x, Bloks c.21x, Hong Kong-listed consumer leaders 15–20x. At 17x Pop Mart sits mid-range among consumer leaders — neither cheap nor expensive. With growth of only 10%, PEG is 1.7, on the high side.

Three scenarios: bear case HKD 100 (2027 RMB 10bn × 12x), base case HKD 150 (RMB 12bn × 15x), bull case HKD 225 (RMB 14bn × 20x). Expected value HKD 148.75, 4.8% below the current price.

Cross-check on EV/EBITDA: market cap HKD 209bn plus zero net debt = HKD 209bn, or RMB 191bn; 2026E net profit of RMB 11bn, adding back D&A and taxes, gives EBITDA of roughly RMB 15bn, so EV/EBITDA is about 12.7x. The global consumer-leader EV/EBITDA centre is 10–14x, and Pop Mart sits mid-range. Both methods point the same way: not cheap, not expensive.

11. Investment recommendation: Watch, wait for a pullback

Rating: "Watch, wait for pullback"; 12-month target price HKD 150 (base case); entry point HKD 120.

Three reasons: ① Excellent business model (gross margin 69.7%, net margin 29.3%, zero debt) — not worth shorting; ② Growth is normalizing (overseas -11.1%, profit +10.1%) — not worth chasing; ③ The current price implies 17x 2026E and a PEG of 1.7, and all four major bank target prices sit below the current price — risk/reward insufficient.

Triggers: share price below HKD 120, or Q3 results showing overseas revenue stabilizing sequentially plus inventory back to 150 days. If either trigger appears, upgrade to "Buy (3% position)".

Position sizing: build 3% at HKD 120, add 1% at HKD 110, add 1% at HKD 100, cap 8%. Don't act at HKD 130 — that level is merely "from very expensive to expensive". If Q3 results confirm overseas stabilization, add 3% directly without waiting for price, because "evidence" is worth more than "price".

Conclusion

Watch; wait for a pullback. Pop Mart is the only name in designer toys worth tracking long term: 676 stores, 100 million members, a 69.7% gross margin and RMB 12.4 billion of cash are not luck. Still, its 2026 interim results show growth leans heavily on hit products, and hits are unpredictable. At HKD 156.2 the stock trades on 17x 2026E with a PEG of 1.7 — risk-reward is insufficient. Wait for HKD 120, or for overseas sales to stop falling in the Q3 report.
