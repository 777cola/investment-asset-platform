Core view

In Li Lu's framework, risk is not "how far the share price may fall" but "whether the investment thesis will be falsified." Pop Mart rests on a single thesis: it can keep creating characters people want to buy. That thesis holds only in part today — Twinkle Twinkle absorbed demand, but Labubu slipped. Of the six risk categories, the first two are the principal contradiction; the remaining four are secondary.

Risk one: IP lifecycle risk is a matter of "when", not "whether"

Li Lu would put this first because it is structural, not cyclical. Social-media attention sets the life cycle of a designer-toy IP, and the half-life of that attention is shortening: in 2015 a character could stay hot for three years; by 2026 it may last only 18 months.

The Labubu data is a specimen: RMB 4.814 billion in the first half of 2025, 34.7% of the total; RMB 4.454 billion in the first half of 2026, 26.0%. The absolute amount fell only 7.5%, but the share fell 8.7 percentage points, because the denominator is rising. Labubu has not "collapsed"—it has "stopped growing and been caught by others."

MOLLY is more alarming: -33.6%. A 20-year-old IP, 10 years into productization, still generating RMB 900 million in revenue in a half-year sounds like proof of vitality; but -33.6% YoY shows that old IPs can also fall suddenly, and fall faster than new IPs. Pop Mart has no "perpetual IP," only an "IP portfolio."

Second risk: overseas stores are asset-heavy and the single-store model remains unproven

Overseas stores rose from 185 at end-2025 to 221, a net increase of 36 in six months, yet overseas revenue fell 11.1% over the same period. The implication: new stores are ramping, old stores are sliding, and the two offset each other into negative territory.

JPMorgan estimates the overseas operating margin fell 14 percentage points YoY to 30%. That number matters—a 30% operating margin is still high, but the direction is down. If it drops to 20% in 2027, overseas goes from "growth engine" to "profit drag."

Li Lu would ask a longer-horizon question: when a Chinese brand sells "Chinese aesthetics" overseas, where is the ceiling? Japan's MUJI and Korea's Amorepacific have both shown that cultural products face a ceiling abroad, typically "ethnic Asian populations plus young people well disposed toward East Asian culture." Is Pop Mart now inside that circle, or breaking out into the mainstream? The 1H26 data say it is meeting resistance in the breakout process.

Risks 3 to 6: none is fatal, but together they weigh on the valuation

Inventory: 201 days. Designer toys do not go out of season, so the impairment burden is lighter than for apparel; still, inventory that "won't sell" feeds through to gross margin via discounting, clearance and channel stuffing. This is the easiest item to overlook, because it does not sit in the top three lines of the income statement.

FX: Overseas is 29% of the mix, and currency swings move results by RMB 80 million a year. This is not an operating issue, but it makes the "profit growth" figure incomparable — investors see +10.1%, while underlying operations grew +28%.The market usually prices off reported figures, not off figures after exclusions.

Regulation: Domestic blind box rules are already in force (no sales to minors under 8, disclosure of draw probabilities), with no sign of further tightening. The risk lies overseas — the US is advancing consumer-protection legislation on "blind-box-style random goods"; if it extends to designer toys, the model for the 86 stores in the Americas would have to be redone.

Valuation: 14.2x TTM is not expensive, but it is calculated on peak profit. If net profit is RMB 10 billion in 2026 and RMB 11 billion in 2027, the current price corresponds to 19x and 17x.For a company with 10% profit growth, 17x is a reasonable ceiling, not a floor.

Li Lu at the Giving Pledge event: real demand and strong companies, but "the winner will change"

In his framework, consumption upgrading is a civilisation-level trend, and so is the globalisation of Chinese brands. Both hold. What Pop Mart is doing therefore has long-term value — that is not in doubt.

But "the industry has value" and "this company can keep earning excess profits" are two different things. Designer toys have low entry barriers (sign designers, find contract manufacturers, open stores) and low exit costs, so profits periodically shift from the leader to new entrants. Today Pop Mart is the beneficiary of that shift; tomorrow it may be the one losing out.

He would therefore put the company in the "worth researching, worth tracking, but not suitable for a heavy position" bucket. The position cap depends on price: if the price is low enough that "you don't lose even if the winner changes," you can buy; if it implies "it will always be the winner," you are gambling.

Conclusion

Six risk categories, of which two are the core contradictions: IP lifecycle (already materialised) and overseas localisation (in progress). The four secondary risks will not kill the thesis on their own, but together they compress the valuation. Li Lu's conclusion is not "avoid" but rather "the company's certainty is insufficient to support a heavy position, yet sufficient to justify watching". Price is the only antidote.
