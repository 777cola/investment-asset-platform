Core view

The essence of this business: use an artist's image to manufacture a moment of "anticipation before unboxing," then sell that anticipation for RMB 59 to 599. What it sells is not plastic but emotion; not function but social currency. Only by understanding this can you understand why gross margin reaches 69.7%, and why it can fall just as fast.

The business breaks down into three layers: IP, product and channel

The first layer is IP. Pop Mart signs artists — either buying out the rights or sharing royalties — and turns the characters into replicable SKUs. The economics are "one-time investment, infinite replication": the designer's fee is fixed, and unit cost is virtually the same whether you sell 10,000 units or 1 million. This is where the 70% gross margin comes from.

The second layer is product. In the first half, plush toys generated RMB 9.825 billion, or 57.2%, and figures RMB 5.192 billion, or 30.2%. The category shifted from "small figures displayed on a desk" to "plush toys held in your arms" — a change defined by consumers, not a choice the company made. Plush is more complex to manufacture, has longer lead times and higher unit prices, and carries a slightly lower gross margin than figures, so overall gross margin fell from 70.3% to 69.7%.

The third layer is channel: 676 self-operated stores, 2,827 robot stores, and online blind-box machines (mainland online RMB 4.78 billion, +62.7%; the blind-box machine app alone RMB 2.06 billion). Self-operated means no distributor takes a cut, so the retail price is the ex-factory price — the second reason for the high gross margin.

Unit economics: how does a single store generate RMB 50 million a year?

A rough per-store calculation: RMB 17.173 billion of revenue across 676 stores in H1 2026 works out to RMB 25.40 million per store per half-year, or RMB 50.80 million annualised. In 2025, RMB 37.12 billion across 630 stores implied RMB 58.92 million annualised per store. That is a 14% decline in one year.

What does RMB 50 million of annual store productivity mean? Uniqlo China runs at about RMB 30 million per store and Starbucks about RMB 5 million, so Pop Mart sits above Uniqlo. That explains two things: why top-tier malls are willing to hand over a floor's worth of space, and why the company dares to open in core overseas retail districts—the single-store model supports high rent.

But how much of that RMB 50 million is "Labubu premium"? It cannot be separated out. Membership can: 82.44 million registered members in China, contributing 92.9% of mainland revenue, with a 51.6% repurchase rate. That means more than half of sales come from existing customers, not one-off visits. This is the most solid part of the business, and what separates it from a "trendy shop".

Sources of growth: new stores, price increases or a wider buyer base

Three sources, and they break out cleanly. Store openings: 46 net new stores and 190 robot stores in the half, store count +7.3%, contributing part of the growth. Price increases: plush carries a higher unit price than figures, so the mix shift delivers a "hidden price increase" — not a deliberate price hike. Same-store: this is the key. Overseas stores rose by 36 net while revenue fell 11.1%, implying overseas same-store sales declined by double digits; domestic revenue rose 47.3% with store count also rising, so same-store there should be positive, but the company did not disclose the figure.

The real source of growth, then, is "domestic same-store plus global store openings", with overseas same-store negative. This is a business still expanding, but with falling output per unit. Duan Yongping would make the call here: how long expansion lasts depends on the payback period of new stores; if new-store payback stretches from 12 months to 24 months, expansion will stop on its own.

Conclusion

The business itself scores 8: high gross margin, asset-light, cash collected upfront, and members driving more than half of repeat purchases. It loses points on one count only — demand is attention-driven, and attention is not something the company controls. That is not "bad", it is "unpredictable". By Duan Yongping's standard, an unpredictable business is not unbuyable; it simply has to be bought at a lower price.
