Title and lead

Pop Mart Halves as Wang Ning Says Three Blunt Truths

Pop Mart reported 2026 interim results on August 20: revenue of RMB 17.17 billion, up 23.8% YoY, and net profit of RMB 5.04 billion, up 10.1%. The numbers are not ugly. Yet the stock closed at HKD 153.7 that day, down 46% from HKD 288.2 in February this year.

What really rattled the market was not these two figures, but three things Wang Ning said at the results briefing.

Last year a single standout performance masked widespread weakness.

The subtext: 2025's numbers look good, but one bright spot is masking a hundred problems — customers walk into a store and either the product is sold out or there is a queue, and that should not be the norm.

In other words: last year's 184.7% growth was luck brought by a single Labubu character, not the company's baseline capability.

The data tells the same story. THE MONSTERS, the family LABUBU belongs to, posted first-half revenue of RMB 4.454 billion, down 7.5% YoY, with its share falling from 34.7% to 26.0% — its first half-year decline since going viral. The 20-year-old MOLLY fared worse: RMB 900 million, down 33.6%.

Taking over is Twinkle Twinkle: RMB 2.65 billion, up 580.6%, vaulting from RMB 389 million a year earlier to become the second-largest IP. Add CRYBABY at RMB 1.63 billion, DIMOO at RMB 1.62 billion and SKULLPANDA at RMB 1.55 billion, and 11 IPs exceed RMB 100 million, with 6 topping RMB 1 billion.

So the accurate statement is not "Labubu retreated, the company is finished" but "Labubu retreated, and the company rotated a new batch in." The former is collapse; the latter is rotation.

New overseas users are only here to grab LABUBU.

That is how COO Si De put it. His actual words were blunter: last year's overseas growth was driven mainly by LABUBU, and many new users had no awareness of designer toy culture — "they just came to grab LABUBU."

The upshot: 1H overseas revenue of RMB 4.972 billion, down 11.1% YoY, with its share of the total falling from 40.3% to 29.0%. Asia-Pacific fell 9.7%, the Americas 16.5%, and only Europe — the smallest region — grew, up 5.9%.

Break it down, though, and the decline is entirely online: Asia-Pacific online -39.8%, the Americas online -45.6%, Europe online -59.0%, Shopee -62.1%. Over the same period, overseas stores added a net 36 — the Americas went from 41 to 86 stores, Europe from 18 to 45.

The read-across: overseas demand has not disappeared — the "order while scrolling short videos" route has run its course, and it now comes down to "walking into a store to buy." The former is renting traffic; the latter is buying location. Renting traffic is fast, asset-light and high-margin; buying location is slow, heavy and long-payback. These are two entirely different businesses, and should carry different valuations.

"The 30% target most likely will not be met this year."

Of the 30% growth target set at the start of the year, Wang Ning himself said it most likely will not be met, and guidance was cut to no less than 20%. The company simultaneously announced a HKD 2 billion to 5 billion buyback over the next 6 months — the first time a buyback of this scale has been disclosed at an earnings briefing since listing.

Backing off and buying back are not contradictory. Backing off is honesty about performance; the buyback is a statement about value. With RMB 12.44 billion in cash, zero bank borrowings and a debt-to-asset ratio of 24.9%, a HKD 5 billion buyback poses no financial strain.

But a buyback is not growth. It can support the share price, not profit. In the first half the company already repurchased 11.22 million shares for HKD 1.74 billion at HKD 140.9 to 194.9, an average of about HKD 155, roughly the current level — management itself bought halfway up the hill.

One more number matters more than the three above.

Net profit came in at RMB 5.038 billion, yet cash fell from RMB 13.78 billion at the start of the year to RMB 12.44 billion — a decrease of RMB 1.34 billion. Over the same period, dividends were RMB 3.15 billion and buybacks about RMB 1.55 billion.

Rough math: 5.038 - 4.7 = RMB 340 million, so cash should have edged up; instead it fell RMB 1.34 billion, a gap of about RMB 1.7 billion. Where did the money go? Inventory. Inventory turnover days stretched from 123 to 201; on first-half operating cost of RMB 5.2 billion, that tied up roughly RMB 1.1 billion more in goods.

Management blames overseas pre-stocking — Americas stores grew from 41 to 86, and new stores do need inventory. But the other half of the explanation does not hold: stores are increasing, revenue is falling, and inventory is still rising. When these three move in different directions, the problem is usually on the demand side.

This is not fraud. It is the cost of expansion: half the profit earned turned into goods in the warehouse rather than cash on the books. The net cash ratio is about 0.7x, below the healthy threshold of 1.

So, is a 14x PE cheap?

At HKD 156.2, the market cap is about HKD 209 billion and the stock trades at 14.2x P/E (TTM). On the face of it, a consumer company with a 69.7% gross margin at 14x looks absurdly cheap.

Look closely at the denominator, though. TTM net profit of RMB 13.47 billion is built as full-year 2025 of RMB 13.012 billion plus first-half 2026 of RMB 5.038 billion less first-half 2025 of RMB 4.576 billion. Broken out, that means the second half of 2025 alone earned RMB 8.436 billion — the half-year when Labubu went viral globally and stores had queues every day.

On 2026 net profit of RMB 11 billion, the P/E is 17.4x. HSBC wrote in its August 20 report that the current price equates to 16.4x 2026 forecast P/E. JPMorgan is harsher, forecasting third-quarter revenue -35% and fourth-quarter -20%, cutting its rating straight to "Underweight" with a target price of HKD 120.

Four major banks cut in unison on August 20-21: JPMorgan 120 (Underweight), CMS International 121 (Sell), HSBC 136.5, BofA 153 (Neutral). Not one rates it "Buy"; all four target prices sit below the current price.

So it is not 14x, it is around 17x. For a company with 10% profit growth and overseas still declining, 17x is reasonable, not cheap.

Why doesn't Duan Yongping sell?

The most widely circulated rumour: Duan Yongping is the second-largest shareholder, having added to his position in July, lifting his stake from 6.85% to 7.65%, or 102 million shares. After the interim results, someone asked him whether overseas was too weak, and he replied, "As a shareholder, I actually think it's quite good."

Many read this as "the big name is bullish." But he did not say this price is worth buying. He built his position in 2024 and 2025 at a cost far below HKD 156; the July add was "topping up after a drop," not "the current value is worth buying."

Shareholder and buyer are two identities: a shareholder asks "will the company still be here in ten years," a buyer asks "is today's price a good deal." His cost may be HKD 80, yours is HKD 156 — the same company, but not the same investment.

Conclusion: wait for two signals

The question here is not whether the company is good, but whether it is expensive. The business model, management and balance sheet are strengths; the sustainability of growth and cash conversion are weaknesses.

Wait for one of two signals before re-rating: 1) Q3 overseas revenue stops falling QoQ and inventory turnover returns to within 150 days; 2) the share price falls below HKD 120, equivalent to about 13x 2026 net profit of RMB 11 billion.

Until then, the rating is "watch, wait for a pullback" — not for lack of conviction, but because the price has yet to factor in the bad news.

In one sentence: the RMB 5 billion profit is real, but three sets of numbers are in conflict — stores are rising while revenue is falling; gross margin is stable while net profit is falling; profit is rising while cash is falling. All three contradictions point to one conclusion: the quality of growth is deteriorating.
