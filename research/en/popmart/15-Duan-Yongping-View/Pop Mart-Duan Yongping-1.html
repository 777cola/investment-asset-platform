Duan Yongping — Pop Mart

Pop Mart (09992) | Duan Yongping
Research date 2026/09/07  ·  Price HK$156.20 (+1.43%)  ·  Market cap About HKD 209 billion  ·  P/E (TTM) Approximately 14.2x

Core call: he buys "people + business," not "this quarter's numbers"

Run Pop Mart through Duan Yongping's framework and six questions follow. His answer would most likely be: a good business, the right people, but a price that has not yet reached the point where "even a rough estimate makes it a bargain." He is already the second-largest shareholder (7.65%) — not evidence of "bullishness" but the result of a "low cost basis."

Question 1: Is this a good business?

Good — and good on three counts.

First, a 69.7% gross margin. For a company selling small toys at a few dozen yuan apiece, holding that margin shows it is not selling plastic; it is selling "liking." Liking is cheap to produce and has very low price elasticity. That is what a good business looks like — no need to fight price wars.

Second, cash comes in before goods go out. With 82.44 million members, the online blind-box draw takes payment before the box is opened, so cash flow is positive. Inventories are tied up for 201 days this year, but that is an expansion problem, not a model problem.

Third, expansion does not require heavy spending. Stores are leased and factories belong to others; the company puts up only design and rent. Reaching RMB 37.1 billion in revenue on "renting space + outsourcing manufacturing" — that is a very good model.

Where does it fall short? Demand is "want," not "need." What you want today, you may not want tomorrow — unlike Moutai or Coca-Cola.

Question 2: Will it still be around in ten years?

The company will most likely still be around, and so will the business, but whether it remains "the leader" I cannot say.

Designer toys exist as a category in Japan, the US and China and have survived twenty years, so demand will not disappear. Look at Funko, the US designer-toy leader: it was hot at IPO, and the stock has since fallen from USD 30 to USD 5. Revenue is still there; profit is gone. The category survives; the company may not. The company survives; the profit may not.

Pop Mart's edge over Funko is proprietary IP (artist IP RMB 15.29 billion) rather than licensing. Proprietary IP carries higher gross margin and a longer life cycle. But it shares Funko's problem: it must keep releasing new characters to sustain heat, and momentum drops the moment it stops.

So my answer is: in ten years it will most likely still be here, but I cannot be sure it will still be this profitable in ten years. That "uncertainty" is the discount I demand on the price.

Question three: Can you understand it?

The business is understandable; the earnings are not. Keep the two separate.

The business is easy to grasp: sign artists, make products, open stores, sell to young people. That chain is legible at a glance, with no need to understand technology or policy.

The earnings are not. When does the next hit arrive? When does Labubu fade? When do overseas sales stop falling? Wang Ning himself cannot answer these three questions. He has said as much: "many of this year's difficulties were unforeseen."

Not understanding the earnings does not mean you cannot invest. If you understand the business, you can buy when "the price is low enough," using price to hedge what you cannot understand. But if the price is not low, not understanding is a reason not to buy.

Question 4: At what price to buy?

Rough estimate: 2026 net profit of about RMB 11 billion; 2027, if overseas stops declining, possibly RMB 12 billion; if it does not, RMB 10 billion. What is this company worth? At 15x as a consumer stock, that is RMB 150 billion to 180 billion, or HKD 164 billion to 197 billion, corresponding to a share price of HKD 122 to 147.

So above HKD 147, I would have no interest; below HKD 122, it starts to get interesting; below HKD 100, it is "clearly cheap."

The current price is HKD 156.2, 6% above my ceiling. It is not that you cannot buy it, it is that after buying there is no margin of safety. My habit when buying stocks is "not to panic even if it falls 30% after I buy." Buying at HKD 156, a 30% drop takes it to HKD 109, and at that point I would panic, because I am not sure whether it is worth 109 or 80.

Question 5: What worries you most?

Two things worry me most.

First, the risk that it turns into a "distribution company." If it ever stops creating its own IP and instead licenses other companies' characters and sells goods through stores, it shifts from an "IP company" to a "retail company": gross margin falls from 70% to 40% and the valuation multiple de-rates from 15x to 8x. It has not reached that point yet (artist IP accounts for 89%), but the risk of straying is highest when overseas expansion moves too fast.

Second, the risk that Wang Ning loses discipline. Not that he lacks it now — he voluntarily slowed growth, spoke plainly and bought back stock, all of which show discipline. The fear is that one day he starts "telling stories": the metaverse, AI, the ecosystem. Pop Mart does not need these stories; it only needs to create a few characters each year that people want to buy. When a company starts telling stories unrelated to its own business, it is usually a signal that the core business is in trouble.

Question 6: You are already the second-largest shareholder, so why not buy more?

His answer would probably be: it was cheap when I bought it.

HKEX disclosures show H&H lifted its stake from 6.85% to 7.65%, or 102 million shares, on 6 July. Note the timing — he had been buying since 2024 at a cost far below HKD 156. The July add was "topping up after a fall", not "the current value is worth buying".

Asked after the interim results whether overseas was too weak, he said: "As a shareholder, I actually think it's quite good." That is not a call that the share price will rise; it is a call that the business is not broken. Shareholder and buyer are two identities: the shareholder asks whether the company will still be here in ten years; the buyer asks whether today's price is a good deal.

So do not read my holding as a buy signal. My cost may be HKD 80 and yours HKD 156 — we own the same company, but not the same investment.

Conclusion

The six answers add up to this: a good business, with the right people doing the right things, but a price that has not yet reached the point where "even a rough estimate makes it a bargain." His move would be to "hold what he already owns and not add anything new," waiting for HKD 122 or below. This framework contains no "forecasts" — only "waiting" and "crunching the numbers."
