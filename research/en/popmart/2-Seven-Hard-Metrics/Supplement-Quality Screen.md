# Pop Mart（09992）— Quality Screen Supplement

- Research date：2026/09/07
- Price：156.2 HKD
- Market cap：About HKD 209 billion

## Four criteria cleared — top-tier among consumer goods, no doubt about it.

What does a 69.7% gross margin mean? Kweichow Moutai runs at 91%, Hermès around 70%, Nike 44% and MINISO just over 40%. Pop Mart sits in Hermès's bracket, yet its average selling price is only tens to a few hundred yuan. Two layers stack to produce this: IP premium plus direct-operated channels — the company designs its own products and sets its own retail prices, with no distributor taking a cut in between.

Net margin of 29.3% fell 3.7 percentage points YoY, driven mainly not by gross profit (down only 0.6pct) but by expenses: distribution and selling expenses rose 23.1% YoY, of which rental costs rose 43.3% and employee compensation rose 45.7%, with the sales team expanding from 6,219 to 9,734 people. Store-opening costs came first; revenue has not caught up yet.

A debt-to-asset ratio of 24.9%, zero bank borrowings and RMB 12.44 billion in cash — this is the most solid line item. Designer toys are a cash business: collect payment first, ship later, so in theory no borrowing is needed to expand. On this point it beats most consumer companies.

## The standout metric is 201 days of inventory.

Inventory days have risen from 123 last year to 201 this year — 78 days more, or nearly two and a half months of goods sitting in warehouses and in transit. Management attributes this to overseas pre-stocking: Americas stores grew from 41 to 86 and Europe from 18 to 45, and new stores need inventory. That explanation is only half valid.

The half that does not hold: overseas revenue is falling even as stores are added and stocking accelerates. Asia-Pacific revenue fell 9.7% and the Americas 16.5%, while net store additions in the period were 36. When store count rises, revenue falls and inventory builds — three diverging directions — the problem usually sits on the demand side, not in supply-chain pacing.

At 201 days, inventory is not fatal for a company with a 70% gross margin — designer toys do not go out of season, rot, or face much pressure to be cleared at a discount. It is, however, a signal on profit quality: if overseas destocking falls short of expectations in the second half, impairments will hit gross profit directly. On this item I grade it "fail but fixable," with a two-quarter observation window.

## But two points stand out: net profit growth lags, and cash flow has not kept pace with profit

Net profit +10.1% against revenue +23.8% — a 13.7 percentage point gap. Management attributes it to FX: an H1 FX loss of RMB 720 million versus a RMB 120 million gain last year, an RMB 840 million swing. Ex-FX, profit growth is about 28%, in line with revenue. The explanation is genuine, but FX is not a one-off — overseas revenue is 29% of the total, and as long as the RMB keeps moving against the USD and Southeast Asian currencies, this line item will recur every year.

Cash conversion is the bigger worry. Backing out from the change in cash, H1 operating cash flow was about RMB 3.5 billion, only 0.7x net profit. Where did the money go? Inventory build (+78 days turnover), receivables (overseas channels), and RMB 3.15 billion of dividends plus RMB 1.55 billion of buybacks. The first two are working capital; the last two are deliberate choices.

Verdict: failing these three tests is not a failure of accounting fraud — it is the failure of a growth model shifting from asset-light to operations-heavy. Stores, inventory and headcount are upfront heavy-asset investment; the returns come later. If Q3 revenue recovers and inventory falls, all three repair at once; if revenue keeps sliding, all three deteriorate together. That is why the valuation cannot carry a growth-stock multiple.

## But is it fatal? Not fatal, but it caps the valuation.

The seven hard metrics are a weed-out tool, not a pick-the-best tool: their job is to exclude companies that look cheap but are in fact leaking cash. Pop Mart does not fall into that bucket — it is cash-rich, debt-free and runs very high gross margins, so the worst case is profit reverting to 2024 levels, not bankruptcy.

Failing these three metrics, however, caps the valuation. A company with 201 days of inventory turnover, 10% net profit growth and a 0.7 net cash ratio will not command 25x P/E from the market. 14x TTM and 17x 2026E is the fair price for this state of affairs. A re-rating requires fixing all three first: inventory turnover back to 150 days, net profit growth back above 15%, and the net cash ratio back to 1x.
