Business essence: four revenue lines, four economics — Kuaishou

Start with where the money comes from and what the costs are, then judge whether it is good or bad

Core view

Kuaishou's revenue breaks into four lines with distinct economics: advertising is a bidding business, live streaming a revenue-sharing business, e-commerce a commission business and Kling a compute business. Aggregating them creates the illusion that "+1.4% total revenue is not bad"; taken apart, three are retreating and one is surging.

Advertising: auction-based pricing, with marginal cost trending toward zero

RMB 20.6 billion, 58.0% of total, +4.4% YoY. Advertising economics are the simplest in the model: inventory = user time × load rate, price = auction bids, cost ≈ bandwidth. Each incremental ad slot carries almost no cost, making this the most profitable line.

But it comes with a hard constraint: price is set by advertisers' ROI, not by the platform. A merchant seeing higher conversion on Douyin will bid more for Douyin. Kuaishou can only lift prices by lifting conversion — and that depends on audience spending power and ad product capability, neither of which can change in the short term.

So what does the 4.4% in 2026Q2 tell us? That advertisers ranked Kuaishou lower when allocating incremental budget. This is not "intense competition" — it is "losing on price."

Livestreaming: a revenue-sharing model with diseconomies of scale

RMB 8.7 billion, 24.5% of total, down 13.5% YoY. Livestreaming economics run in reverse: of every RMB 100 in revenue, RMB 40–50 goes to streamers and agencies, and the platform keeps the rest. The more users and the stronger the top streamers, the higher the revenue-share ratio.

This is the only segment where bigger means less profitable. That explains why Kuaishou's livestreaming gross margin sits below advertising, and why the company is willing to let it shrink—a loss-making business falling away is actually good for the income statement.

But it is shrinking too fast. With two consecutive years of negative growth and the industry contracting overall (regulation + consumption downgrade + traffic shifting toward e-commerce), this segment will most likely keep declining until it stabilizes at RMB 6–7 billion per quarter.

E-commerce: a commission business, but the full picture is no longer visible

E-commerce commission sits within "other services" at RMB 6.2 billion, blended with Kling's RMB 850 million. From 2026 the company will stop disclosing quarterly GMV and report only merchant count.

Splitting the line on industry convention, 6.2 - 0.85 = RMB 5.35 billion is e-commerce and other revenue, which at a 2%–5% commission rate implies trillion-level GMV. This is an order-of-magnitude estimate only; the company has provided no definition.

E-commerce is essentially "trading traffic for commission", yet it requires fulfilment, customer service and after-sales support—none of which Kuaishou controls. Its e-commerce will therefore never match rivals that own logistics and supply chains; it can only occupy the "trust e-commerce" niche.

Management has already flagged pressure on commission revenue in the second half. The issue with this line is not growth but transparency: when a company stops disclosing the scale of a business, it is usually because that business is deteriorating.

Kling: a compute business with a fundamentally different gross-margin structure

RMB 850 million, or 2.4% of revenue, +200% YoY. Kling's economics are "compute for revenue": every generation incurs inference cost, and at scale gross margin hinges on in-house chips and inference optimisation.

The upside is that subscription revenue compounds: once customers embed the workflow, renewal rates become visible. The downside is that marginal cost does not trend to zero, and a price war would crush gross margin overnight.

Video-generation API prices fell sharply within a year in 2026, a textbook signal of price war. A cash-burning business running into a track already at price war is the worst combination. Kling 4.0 was only released at the end of September, while ByteDance's Seedance already leads on multiple third-party benchmarks.

Three fatal characteristics

Feature one: no pricing power.Of the four lines, only livestreaming tipping is partly influenced by the platform (through recommendation slots); the other three are market-priced.

Feature two: no switching costs.Users switch apps at zero cost, advertisers shift budgets by changing a single back-end parameter, and creators post the same content on another platform. None of the three sides is locked in.

Feature three: no exclusive content.Short dramas, livestreaming and short videos are all portable formats; nothing exists that "only Kuaishou can show." Exclusive content requires long-term investment (the Netflix model), and Kuaishou's cash flow does not support it.

Taken together, the conclusion is:this is a business that earns money through scale and efficiency, not through moats.Both scale and efficiency will be crushed by larger rivals.

Unit economics: RMB 86 per quarter

Ad revenue per DAU is RMB 50 a quarter, livestreaming RMB 21 and other RMB 15, for a total of RMB 86, or RMB 344 annualised. Against Douyin, where industry estimates put ad revenue per user above RMB 500 a year, Kuaishou runs at roughly 60-70%.

The gap splits roughly in half: user spending power (lower-tier cities, lower ticket sizes) and ad product capability (conversion attribution, creative generation, campaign tools). The former is a given; the latter can be fixed — but it takes a team still fighting.

Right now the best people on that team have been reassigned to Kling. That is the organisational reason behind the 1.4% growth.

Conclusion

Business mix conclusion: Kuaishou is four businesses with entirely different economics bolted together, and the most profitable of them (advertising) is losing pricing power. Kling is the only business with better economics, but it is too small, too expensive and too uncertain. This is not a business that deserves a high multiple.
