Management: right strategy, wrong capital allocation — Kuaishou

One person's judgment, and one company's pricing power
Research date 2026/10/12  ·  Price HK$29.26 (-2.79%)  ·  Market cap About HKD 126.7 billion  ·  PE(TTM) About 10.5x

Core view

Assessing management comes down to two columns: strategic judgment and capital allocation. Cheng Yixiao scores high on the first — betting on video generation in 2024 and building a USD 18 billion asset in two years. He scores low on the second: buying back stock at HKD 45, paying no dividend for five years and steadily shrinking disclosure.

Cheng Yixiao: product background, with a track record of validated judgment

He built GIF Kuaishou in 2011, merged teams with Su Hua in 2013 and for years oversaw product and algorithm. By the time he took over as CEO in October 2021, the company was caught between stalling growth and widening losses.

He did three things: ① cut marketing to protect profit (the marketing expense ratio compressed from 50%+ to just over 30%, and adjusted net profit turned positive in 2024); ② bet on video generation (launched Kling in June 2024, half a year ahead of most peers); ③ spin off Kling (raised USD 3 billion in July 2026 at a post-money valuation of USD 18 billion). All three were right.

But all three share one trait: they are "subtraction" and "options", not "growth". Cutting marketing is subtraction, betting on Kling is buying an option, spinning it off is cashing that option. None of them rebuilt the core business.

That reflects choice, not capability. In a market where ad growth is 4%, rebuilding the core business takes not judgment but money—and that money has to be reserved for AI.

Su Hua: a clean exit

Co-founder and former CEO (2013–2021), a Tsinghua dropout and former Google/Baidu engineer, he led Kuaishou's transition from a tool to a short-video community. After stepping down in October 2021, he gradually withdrew from day-to-day operations.

A founder's exit typically takes two to three years to show its impact: strategic continuity remains, but the "feel for the company" disappears. Kuaishou's product iteration has slowed markedly since 2024—it followed short dramas well, but has built nothing in social, search or local life.

That is not Su Hua's fault but an organisational one: a company's product capability is often bound to its earliest cohort of people.

Capital allocation: three major moves, two mispricings

2021 IPO: HKD 115 issue price, HKD 41.3 billion raised, market cap peaking at HKD 1.7 trillion. Priced wrong — a windfall for old shareholders, a disaster for new ones.

2026Q2 buyback: HKD 880 million for 19.6 million shares at an average HKD 44.9. Against the current HKD 29.26, that is a 35% unrealized loss. Bought at the wrong price.

July 2026 spin-off: USD 18 billion post-money, 16.67% dilution. The one move priced correctly — the AI assets valued separately at their most expensive.

Two of three moves mispriced, one right. That record does not support trusting this management's capital allocation.

Governance: concentrated control, no checks and balances

Dual-class structure: Class A shares carry 10 votes each, giving the founders control. The upside is that long-term strategy can be executed — an investment like Kling that loses money for three years would long ago have drawn opposition from ordinary shareholders. The downside is that minority shareholders have no counterbalancing tool.

Disclosure is the bigger issue: from 2026 the company will stop disclosing e-commerce GMV, Kling will not report profit and loss separately, and the livestreaming revenue-share ratio has never been disclosed. Three of the most critical operating variables, each vaguer than the last.

Declining transparency is usually not a conspiracy but a defence — the company is unwilling to be graded quarter by quarter on the areas where it is deteriorating. The result is the same: less verifiable information means the valuation should carry a discount.

Incentives: change the KPI, change the behavior

Before 2024, the core KPIs were DAU and revenue; from 2025, the heaviest metrics became "AI revenue share" and "Kling ARR." When KPIs change, resource allocation follows: the same algorithm team that once optimised ad CTR now optimises video-generation usability, and the same budget that once bought traffic now buys compute.

This explains a counterintuitive phenomenon: in the year the company invested most aggressively in AI, core business revenue growth fell to 1.4%. The best people left the main battlefield, and the main battlefield is losing.

Conclusion

Management verdict: trustworthy on strategy, not on capital allocation. The first makes us willing to buy at the right price; the second makes us unwilling to buy at the current one. A good captain can still overpay for the ticket—that line fits here.
