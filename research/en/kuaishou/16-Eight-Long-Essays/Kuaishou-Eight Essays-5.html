Industry and competition: the third-place player in a mature market — Kuaishou

The industry hasn't collapsed; Kuaishou's relative position is collapsing

Core view

The keyword for China's short-video industry in 2026 is "stock": user scale has peaked, advertising growth has fallen to single digits, livestreaming is shrinking, and e-commerce growth is slowing.A stock market follows one iron rule — the leader's share holds, the runner-up's share is eaten first, and the third player can only play defense.

Industry size: three curves, two trending down

Short-video advertising: growth slowed from over 40% in 2021 to single digits in 2026, with the industry at about 6%. Kuaishou grew 4.4%, underperforming. Live-streaming tips: the whole industry is shrinking — regulation restricts tipping by minors, consumption downgrading squeezes discretionary spending, and platforms steer traffic toward e-commerce. Kuaishou fell 13.5%, amplifying the industry direction. Live-streaming e-commerce: growth slowed from over 30% to around 10%, and platforms are shifting traffic from live rooms to shelf-based commerce — shelf commissions are higher but content requirements are lower, narrowing platform differentiation.

Of the three curves, only AI video is rising. But it is 2.4% of Kuaishou's revenue and an even smaller share of the industry's. A new track worth 2% of the industry cannot save the old tracks that make up 98%.

Competitive landscape: four layers of pressure, each taking a slice

Douyin is cutting into budgets. For the same advertiser, Douyin delivers higher ROI, more expensive audiences and better conversion. Kuaishou can only win the same budget by cutting prices. WeChat Channels is cutting into scenarios. WeChat's social graph means older users do not need to "scroll" — they see content in group chats. This is the layer Kuaishou can least defend against, because it does not compete for "scroll" time; it bypasses it entirely.

Hongguo is cutting into time spent. Free short dramas capture "time-killing" through a completely different product format, with revenue sharing more aggressive than Kuaishou's. Xiaohongshu is cutting into seeding. It is the decision gateway for high-ticket female users, and its ad unit prices are several times Kuaishou's.

What the four-layer pincer has in common: none of them attacks Kuaishou's core demographic head-on; they fragment that demographic's time from four directions. That is harder to defend against than a frontal assault.

AI video sector: Kling's position

The first tier of global video generation in 2026 comprises four players: ByteDance Seedance, Kuaishou Kling, Google Veo and Runway, plus Alibaba's Tongyi Wanxiang in China. Kling consistently ranks in the first tier in third-party evaluations, but is not number one.

The economics of this track are "compute in exchange for revenue": every generation carries inference cost, and gross margin depends on chips and inference optimization. A price war has already begun—API unit prices have fallen sharply within a year.In a track where marginal cost is non-zero and prices are declining, first-mover advantage gets erased quickly.

Kling's real advantage is not the model but the scenario: it is embedded in Kuaishou's short-video ecosystem, with abundant real creative demand and data feedback. Whether that advantage disappears after the spin-off is an underappreciated question.

Endgame scenarios: three possibilities

Scenario one: standalone survival (40% probability). Advertising holds steady at 3%–4%, Keling reaches RMB 10 billion annualized, and the company becomes a dual engine of "advertising + tools." Valuation could support 14–16x, implying a share price of HKD 35–40.

Scenario two: absorbed (35% probability). Advertising turns negative, Keling's valuation is marked down, and the company becomes a platform with stable cash flow but no growth, eventually absorbed by a larger platform or capital. Valuation at 7–8x, implying HKD 18–20.

Scenario three: transition to a tools company (25% probability). Keling lists separately, the parent becomes a holding platform, and the core business keeps shrinking. Valuation depends on Keling's public-market pricing, with a 30%–40% discount at the parent. The share price could range between HKD 25–35 in this case, but with extreme volatility.

The probability-weighted expectation across the three scenarios is roughly HKD 27 — slightly below the current price. This is the quantitative basis for "avoid."

The nature of competition: not a product war, but a cash-flow war

Competition in 2026 is no longer about "whose product is better" but "who can hold out longer." Douyin sits behind ByteDance, Video Accounts behind Tencent, and Hongguo also behind ByteDance. Kuaishou's rivals all have thicker cash flows than it does.

Kuaishou's operating cash flow in Q2 2026 (rough estimate) was about RMB 1.2 billion in free cash flow, with RMB 24.27 billion on the books. ByteDance's annual capex runs in the hundreds of billions. In a track built on subsidies and compute, a cash-flow gap translates directly into a share gap.

That is also why Kuaishou chose to spin off Kling — not for valuation, but to let Kling raise its own financing and fight its own battles without dragging down the parent's financials. The choice is rational, but it also amounts to an admission: the parent company cannot afford to fight this war.

Conclusion

Industry and competition conclusion: The industry has not collapsed; Kuaishou's relative position is collapsing. In a mature market, the fate of the number three player is slow share loss, margins suppressed by competition, and a valuation converging toward a utility. Unless Kling takes the top spot, that fate cannot change.
