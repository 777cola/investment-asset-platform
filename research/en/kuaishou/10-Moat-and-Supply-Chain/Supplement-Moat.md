## Position in the value chain: wholesaling attention, retailing it to advertisers

Kuaishou's business model can be summed up in one sentence: it buys attention upstream (creator content and streamer performances) and wholesales it downstream to advertisers and merchants. It does not produce content, hold inventory or handle fulfilment—all three sit with third parties. It does one thing only: slice the time of 400 million people into tags and sell it to the highest bidder.

The upside of this position is that it is asset-light: capex is mainly servers (RMB 5.9 billion per quarter), with no inventory, no stores and no riders. The downside is low substitutability: content can migrate to other platforms, and so can advertising budgets.

Its only heavy-asset investment is algorithms and compute. This was once a moat (recommendation systems need years of data accumulation); it is now becoming an arms race (large-model training costs several billion a year). The moat has turned into a cost—the change platform companies least want to see.

## Moat by item: none of the five is widening

Network effect 6/10. The two-sided creator-consumer network holds, and 412 million DAU is a hard asset. But network effects cut both ways: creators follow the users. Douyin has more of them, so creators go to Douyin; Hongguo pays a higher revenue share, so short-drama teams go to Hongguo. Kuaishou's network is playing defence, not offence.

Content ecosystem 6/10. The laotie culture, short-drama supply and livestream supply are each distinctive — above all the "authenticity" of lower-tier markets, a tone Douyin cannot replicate. But tone is not a moat: users can watch two apps at once.

Cost advantage 4/10, switching cost 3/10, licenses 5/10. Bandwidth has scale effects; revenue sharing does not. Switching costs are close to zero, and licenses are no barrier to existing rivals. Together the three total just 12/30 — the thinnest part of the moat.

## The most dangerous rival: not Douyin, but Hongguo

Douyin has already won, and the winner's playbook is predictable: take brand budgets, take merchants, take AI talent. Kuaishou can see all of this coming, and can defend against it.

Hongguo is a different threat. It uses free short dramas — an entirely different product form — to capture "time-killing" demand within the same user base. It does not need to take Kuaishou's advertisers; it only needs to take those two hours of users' time. Once that time is carved away, ad inventory shrinks and eCPM falls. That chain is more lethal than directly grabbing budgets, because it is irreversible.

And Hongguo's parent ByteDance has unlimited ammunition. Kuaishou cannot afford a price war here and can only lean on content differentiation — which is precisely why its gross margin fell 4.1pct this year.

## The flip side of a moat: what can breach it

A moat is rarely breached head-on; it is bypassed. For Kuaishou, all three bypass routes are realistic: 1) A generational shift in content format — if AI generation turns "watching others" into "generating your own," the platform's content library becomes worthless; 2) Transfer of distribution power — if phone makers or super apps take over the recommendation entry point (WeChat Search, the phone's negative-one screen), Kuaishou degrades into a content supplier; 3) Regulatory reshaping — tipping caps, algorithm filing, minor protection: any one of these tightening directly cuts off a slice of revenue.

Of the three, the first is the most likely: it requires neither policy nor a rival's push — only the technology curve continuing downward. And Kuaishou itself is pushing that curve (Kling). This is a cruel paradox: it is personally accelerating the technology that could replace it.

## If only one moat were left, which would it be?

The answer is not network effects but "laotie relationships" — the trust built through long-term interaction between streamers and fans. That delivers two things rivals struggle to replicate: extremely high tipping conversion (Kuaishou's live-streaming ARPU has long outpaced peers) and an extremely low customer acquisition cost (referrals and private-domain groups).

Yet this moat monetises least efficiently: the ad revenue a trust relationship can generate falls far short of what a precise audience tag delivers. Kuaishou therefore faces an awkward choice — either keep the human touch and earn less, or chase efficiency and lose its differentiation. Over the past three years it chose the latter, and both gross margin and DAU have delivered their verdict.
