## Path: three declines, one failed rebound

Leg 1 (7/16–8/14): 46.92 → 40.06, -14.6%. The trigger was the spin-off news losing momentum: the stock jumped to 46.92 on the July 2 announcement, then drifted lower for a month. No bad news in this leg — simply "the good news was all sold."

Leg 2 (8/18–8/20): 39.38 → 33.64, -14.6%. Interim results and guidance, fully priced in over two days. The only decline with a clear cause, and the only one worth reviewing.

Leg 3 (8/21–10/12): 33.64 → 29.26, -13.0%. A slow bleed, with two rebounds along the way (September 18 +2.67%, October 9 +5.47%), neither of which held. The failed rebounds point to position restructuring rather than event-driven selling — institutions are cutting exposure, not waiting for news.

## Three genuine signals

Signal 1: Interim revenue mix. Total revenue +1.4%, advertising +4.4%, livestreaming -13.5% — all incremental growth came from Kling. The market met the print with a two-day drop of -14.6%, rejecting the narrative that AI growth masks a stalling core business.

Signal 2: Management's second-half guidance. The line "e-commerce marketing services and commission revenue under year-on-year pressure" did more damage than the numbers, because it stretched Q2's weakness from "one quarter" to "at least two quarters."

Signal 3: Valuation reconstruction after spin-off pricing. At the July spin-off the market treated Kuaishou as the "Kling parent" and bid it up to 46.92; the August results showed the core business retreating, so the market re-cast it as a "declining ad platform" and drove it down to 33. Neither pricing reflected core cash flow — evidence the valuation anchor is unstable.

## Four noise items, not signals

The -7.76% on 7/17 came with no announcement and was profit-taking on spin-off speculation. The -9.6% from 8/11 to 8/17 was position adjustment ahead of results — right direction, wrong reason: not someone knowing the numbers in advance, but someone unwilling to bear uncertainty. The September drift lower was mainly Hang Seng Tech beta (Hang Seng Tech fell more than 25% in 2026). The new low on 10/8 and the rebound on 10/9 were two sides of the same news item and carry no fundamental meaning.

Only one criterion separates signal from noise: whether an event changes estimates of future cash flows. Of the seven events, just three — the interim results, the guidance and the spin-off pricing — changed estimates; the other four changed only sentiment.

## A counterfactual: what would go wrong with buying at 46.92

The 16 July high marked peak sentiment after the spin-off announcement. The bull case then: "Kling at USD 18 billion, an 83.3% stake worth HKD 116.3 billion, with the core business thrown in for free." The logic sounded airtight but was wrong on two counts.

First, it treated a primary-market valuation as realisable value. The 83.3% stake cannot be sold; the only cash flows available are dividends and future selldown windows. Second, it ignored that the spin-off itself depresses the parent's valuation. Once the asset is carved out, the remaining business gets priced as a declining asset and its valuation centre must shift lower — something that should have been obvious on the day of the spin-off announcement, not learned from the interim report.

The lesson extends to every sum-of-the-parts opportunity: when a company's most valuable asset is priced separately, the market usually discounts the remainder rather than paying the additive value you calculate.

## Attribution: how much of the decline is self-inflicted

Over the same period the Hang Seng Tech Index fell more than 25% (year-to-date 2026), while Kuaishou fell 37.6% in three months. A rough decomposition: the sector accounts for about 10–12 points, the company itself about 25 points. Within the company-specific portion, results and guidance account for the bulk, and the unwinding of spin-off speculation a small part.

This decomposition matters because it sets the conditions for a rebound: a sector recovery can deliver only 10 points of upside; the remaining 25 points must come from the company itself—that is, advertising growth.
