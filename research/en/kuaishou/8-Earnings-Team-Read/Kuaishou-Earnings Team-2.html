Earnings Deep Dive (Buffett) — Kuaishou

Cash flow is more honest than profit, but whose cash is it?

Core view

Profit can be adjusted; cash flow cannot. Adjusted net profit fell 30.3% to RMB 3.913 billion this quarter, while adjusted EBITDA was down just 7.7% at RMB 7.1 billion—a RMB 3.2 billion gap between the two, reflecting depreciation, amortisation and share-based compensation, which shows that part of the profit decline costs no cash.

EBITDA RMB 7.1 billion, capex RMB 5.9 billion

This is the single most telling line item to calculate in the entire earnings report. 71 - 59 = RMB 1.2 billion, a rough free cash flow figure. It deducts neither taxes nor working capital changes, so it is an upper bound. I estimate the real figure at RMB 2 billion–4 billion, because Kuaishou's prepayment model generates positive working capital.

Why does this number matter? Because it is evidence the company can still sustain itself. A company whose net profit fell 30% but whose free cash flow remains positive will not be forced to raise capital or sell assets, and can move at its own pace. Kuaishou's RMB 24.27 billion in cash gives it even more room.

The ownership of cash: book value is not shareholder value

RMB 24.27 billion sounds like a lot, but three questions need asking: where does it sit, can it be repatriated, and does it have matching liabilities?

Most of Kuaishou's cash sits in onshore operating entities and is subject to foreign exchange controls. Part of it corresponds to merchant deposits and unsettled creator payouts — other people's money — and part is regulatory reserve requirements.Only perhaps half is genuinely free to deploy.

That is why I do not treat "net cash at 21% of market cap" as a direct margin of safety. It is a buffer, not dividend capacity.

Buybacks: the only honest statement of valuation

In Q2 the company spent HKD 880 million buying back 19.60 million shares at an average price of HKD 44.9, putting real money behind management's claim that "we are worth HKD 45." The stock now trades at 29.26. Getting it wrong is forgivable; buying back at nearly 20x forward P/E is not. That isn't value investing — it's market-cap management.

Buffett's sole criterion for buybacks is a price below conservatively estimated intrinsic value. Against our neutral valuation of HKD 26.6, HKD 45 represents a 69% premium. Shareholders would have been better off had that capital been retained for dividends.

Two off-balance-sheet commitments

Two items are invisible on the balance sheet. First, content procurement commitments: short dramas and copyrights are typically locked into long-term contracts, with cash payable over the coming years sitting off-balance-sheet. Second, dilution from equity incentives: Kuaishou issues shares for incentives each year equal to roughly 1%–2% of total share capital — no cash flow impact, but a drag on earnings per share.

Together, the two erode per-share value by roughly 3%–5% a year. For a company whose profit is declining, that leakage cannot be ignored: even with net profit flat, earnings per share will drift lower.

Conclusion

Buffett-style conclusion: the cash flow is real, the valuation is wrong. A company generating positive free cash flow and sitting on ample net cash belongs on the list; but the current price is one that even management has not figured out. Wait for the price, not the story.
