## Notes

This scenario-based Q&A applies Duan Yongping's investment framework. The views are inferences drawn from that framework, not his own statements. Figures are from Kuaishou's 2026Q2 results announcement (2026/08/18): revenue RMB 35.535 billion, gross margin 51.6%, adjusted net profit RMB 3.913 billion, R&D RMB 4.6 billion, livestreaming -13.5%, DAU 412 million. Contribution per user per quarter of RMB 9.5 = 39.13 ÷ 4.12. The HKD 18–20 range = (55–70)×7 + RMB 24.27 billion, converted to HKD at 1.092 and divided by 4.33 billion shares.

## Question 1: Is this a good business?

Whether this is a good business comes down to two things: how easy it is to make money, and how long you can keep making it. Kuaishou does not make money easily — gross margin 51.6%, adjusted net margin 11.0%; a platform with 400 million DAU earns RMB 3.9 billion, or RMB 9.5 per user per quarter.

Nor does the money last. Three years ago advertising was growing above 20%; now it grows 4.4%. Livestreaming has gone from growth engine to drag (-13.5%). A business that changes its growth logic every two years is not a good business — it is good luck.

Duan Yongping would say the mark of a good business is that "you can make money without trying very hard." Kuaishou is now trying very hard (R&D +34.7%) and earning less (net profit -30.3%).

## Question 2: Will it still be around in ten years?

It is still here. A platform with 400 million DAU does not vanish overnight, and no national-level app has died since WeChat. But "still here" and "still making money" are two different things. Kuaishou will most likely still exist in ten years, but it may by then be a company with no revenue growth, an 8% margin and EPS sustained by buybacks — that is a utility, not a growth stock.

That judgment matters because it dictates the valuation method: if in ten years it is a utility, it should trade today at a utility multiple (8–10x), not a growth-stock multiple. The market's current 12.9x forward P/E sits right between the two.

## Question three: Can you understand it?

I understand half of it. Advertising, livestreaming and e-commerce are clear mechanics that can be modelled. What I cannot figure out is Kling: is its moat model capability or distribution capability? If ByteDance pushes Seedance's pricing down to the cost line tomorrow, does Kling follow? Follow and it loses money; don't follow and it loses customers.

Duan Yongping's standard is "don't invest in what you don't understand," and he stressed: "Understanding" is not knowing what it does, it is knowing why it can still make money ten years from now. By that standard, only half of Kuaishou is inside the circle today.

## Q3: What is its differentiation?

Duan Yongping's favourite question is "differentiation." Kuaishou's differentiation was once clear: laotie culture, lower-tier markets and the trust chain of livestream e-commerce. All three survive today, but all have thinned—laotie culture diluted by Douyin's rural content, lower-tier markets diverted by Pinduoduo and Channels, trust e-commerce flattened by "refund-only" and the standardisation of shelf e-commerce.

Differentiation is not "I am different from others"; it is "users cannot do without me." Kuaishou now leans closer to the former.

## Question 4: At what price would you buy?

His answer is usually not a price but "let me understand it first." If a number is required, though: value Kling at zero, treat the core business as a declining asset, apply 7x to 2027 net profit of RMB 5.5–7.0 billion, add net cash of RMB 24.27 billion, and you get HKD 18–20. That implies just over 7x 2027E net profit—no further downside.

At HKD 29.26, the stock trades 50% above that number. So the answer now is not "a bit expensive" but "not there yet."

## Question 5: What worries you most?

The biggest fear is not Douyin, but "users still there, advertisers gone." DAU of 412 million shows people are still scrolling, yet advertisers are willing to allocate only 4.4% of incremental budget—the platform remains, but its commercial value is bleeding away. That kind of chronic blood loss is the hardest to fix: there is no inflection point to wait for, only year-after-year decline.

The second fear is that Kling wins on technology but loses on commercialization. The endgame of video generation is a compute business—whoever has the cheaper chips wins—and Kuaishou's cash flow is an order of magnitude thinner than ByteDance's.

## Question 6: If you were CEO, what would you do?

Step one: make the advertising product's ROI competitive—not through subsidies, but through creative generation and conversion attribution. Kling can already help with both, yet today the two operate in silos. Step two: stop propping up the share price with buybacks and hold the cash back for the price war in the core business. Step three: spin off Kling entirely and return the parent company to the simple narrative of "an advertising company."

Duan Yongping said that doing the right things matters more than doing things right. The rightest thing Kuaishou can do now is admit that its core business is already a mature business, and then manage it as one—rather than wrapping it in an AI shell. If there is capacity to spare, convert part of the RMB 24.27 billion in net cash into a stable dividend—for a company that no longer grows, dividends retain shareholders better than buybacks.
