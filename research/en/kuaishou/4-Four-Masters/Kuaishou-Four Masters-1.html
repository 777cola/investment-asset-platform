Core thesis

Four people, four vocabularies, one conclusion: Duan Yongping says "I understand it but don't want to own it," Buffett says "advertising platforms have no pricing power," Munger says "the number two's moat is granted by the number one," and Li Lu says "the dividends of a media revolution do not belong to the previous generation of platforms." They differ only in wording, not in conclusion.

Three-scenario valuation: bear case HKD 16 (-45%), base case HKD 26.6 (-9%), bull case HKD 46 (+57%). Weighted by subjective probabilities of 35/45/20, expected return is -8.4%. Upside requires several things to fall into place at once; downside requires only one — the odds are negative.

Duan Yongping: I understand this business, but I don't want to own it

Duan Yongping never starts with the financial statements; he asks "what does the user actually get?" Kuaishou's answer: cheap laughs, strangers who feel like acquaintances, and a stall where you can sell goods. These three things were scarce in 2016–2021; they are not today—supply is flooding in from four directions: Douyin, WeChat Channels, Hongguo, and AI apps.

His second question is more damning: "What will it make money from in ten years?" Three years ago the answer was advertising plus livestream e-commerce; today there is also AI video. The core business has not improved—it has merely gained a new story. What Duan Yongping dislikes most is using a new story to explain an old problem, because that means the old problem has not been solved.

He would not buy. His reason is "I cannot understand the competitive landscape five years out," not "the valuation is expensive." This distinction matters: if it were merely expensive, you could buy after it falls; if you cannot understand it, you cannot buy no matter how far it falls. The short case for Kuaishou belongs to the latter.

Buffett: The newspaper lesson — what ad platforms fear most is giving advertisers a choice

Buffett explained why newspapers were a good business: with no second local paper, advertisers had nowhere else to go, so pricing power sat with the paper, gross margins ran 40%–50%, and cash flow was extremely stable. Then the internet arrived, classified ads moved to Craigslist overnight, and the moat was not breached—it was bypassed.

Kuaishou's position resembles a newspaper more than Apple. Apple's moat is the user's cost of switching devices; Kuaishou's moat is that advertisers currently have no better choice. The reality in 2026 is that advertisers do have choices—Douyin takes brand budgets, Pinduoduo takes e-commerce budgets, and ByteDance's Seedance takes AI creative budgets.

Compare this with the platform asset he actually bought—Apple. Apple's moat sits with users: switching costs, photos in iCloud, app purchase history. Kuaishou's moat sits in advertisers' budget spreadsheets: a 5% gap in ROI is enough for budgets to move next quarter. Both are called platforms, but the former's moat rests on itself, while the latter's rests on others' patience.

He also calculates margin of safety, and more ruthlessly than most: 10.5x TTM PE looks cheap, but profit is down 30% year on year, and if it falls another 30% next year, the PE becomes 15x. He does not buy low PE on earnings in a downtrend—the first lesson he learned at Berkshire in the 1970s.

Munger: Invert—what would make it fall by another half?

Munger's approach is to "invert": don't ask how to make money, ask how to lose it. Three paths, none of which requires a black swan.

First, advertising turns negative. Q2 was +4.4%; if next quarter turns to -2%, the market will reclassify the company from "growth stock" to "declining platform," the valuation multiple gets cut from 12x to 7x, and the share price goes straight to HKD 18. Second, Kling falls behind. Video-generation API prices have fallen sharply within a year, the standard signal on the eve of a price war; of the USD 18 billion private-market valuation, a cut to USD 8 billion in the next funding round alone would wipe HKD 60 billion off the parent's stake. Third, AI spending keeps ramping while revenue fails to follow. R&D expense ratio moves from 12.9% to 16%, and full-year adjusted net profit drops below RMB 6 billion.

He would also watch incentives, because "never underestimate the power of incentives." Kuaishou has two distorted incentives: the algorithm rewards time spent, so content inevitably drifts toward lower quality and higher stimulation until regulators step in; management's KPI shifts from DAU to "AI revenue share," so resources inevitably move from the advertising product team to Kling. Both incentives are reasonable; together they mean no one is minding the core business while the new business burns cash fast.

He would also flag a psychological trap: the market anchors on the 52-week high of HKD 82.95 and concludes that HKD 29.26 is "already down 65%, so it's safe." Anchoring is a cost basis, not value. History holds more cases of stocks down 65% falling another 50% than cases of them rebounding.

Li Lu: The media revolution is real, but the winner is not it

Li Lu's framework is civilizational evolution: writing, printing, broadcasting, television, the internet, recommendation feeds — each shift in medium lowers the barrier to content production by an order of magnitude, and concentrates profit in a handful of platforms. Short video is this generation's answer, and he would certainly concede the point: 600 million lower-tier market users have an equal channel of expression for the first time, and that is real social value.

But he would separate "industry value" from "shareholder value." Historically, seven-tenths of the profit from a media revolution goes to one company: search to Google, e-commerce to Alibaba and Amazon, short video to Douyin. Kuaishou is the other three-tenths. A three-tenths business can still be very profitable — provided the leader does not intend to take that three-tenths too. In 2026, the leader intends to.

The historical comparables are clear: Sina and Sohu in the portal era were the first beneficiaries of the media revolution, and the profit ultimately went to search; Nokia in the feature-phone era sold handsets to the whole world, and the profit ultimately went to smartphones. The common thread — they all did their own generation's job right; it was the next generation's barrier that changed material.

A longer-horizon layer: AI generation may devalue "distribution" itself. When content can be generated on demand, a platform's core asset shifts from "content library + recommendation algorithm" to "model + compute" — and that puts ByteDance and Kuaishou's Kling on the same starting line, with ByteDance's cash flow an order of magnitude thicker. Li Lu buys the highest-certainty name; in short video that is not Kuaishou, and in AI video it is not either, for now.

Valuation: why the base case sits below the current price

Using sum-of-the-parts, we do not pay a premium for the "AI option." The core business is valued at 2027E adjusted net profit times 7–10x (China internet advertising platforms trade at 8–12x; we take the low end given negative growth), plus net cash of RMB 24.27 billion; Kling is assigned 0 in the bear and base cases, and included only in the bull case at a 50% discount to its primary-market valuation.

The base case yields HKD 26.6, 9% below the current price. This is not conservatism — it is that even under the assumption that "Kling is worthless," the market should not pay a higher price. Conversely, the current price embeds roughly HKD 11.7 billion of Kling option value — equal to 9% of the USD 18 billion post-money valuation. The market is not paying an AI premium; it is applying an AI discount.

Odds: 57% upside, 45% downside, but the probabilities are asymmetric

Set the probabilities of the three scenarios at 35% (bear) / 45% (base) / 20% (bull). The bull case requires three things to hold at once: advertising back to 8%, Kling ARR doubling to USD 1 billion, and no downward revision in private-market valuation — the joint probability of three independent events is very unlikely to exceed 20%. The bear case requires only one thing: advertising turning negative, and the probability of that is no less than one in three.

Weighted expected return = 0.35×(-45%) + 0.45×(-9%) + 0.20×(+57%) = -8.4%. It looks like limited downside and substantial upside, yet the math comes out negative — this is the difference between odds and probabilities. The market can offer you 57% of upside because it simultaneously gives you 45% of downside and a 35% probability of that downside.

Conclusion

Four masters' consensus: avoid. Duan Yongping does not buy a business he "cannot understand five years out"; Buffett does not buy a platform where "advertisers already have a choice"; Munger counted three downside paths that need no black swan; Li Lu believes the profits of a media revolution do not go to the number two player. What all four share: not one of them treats "10.5x PE" as a reason to buy.

Only one of two things would change the call: ad growth returning above 8% (the business repairs), or Kling disclosing profit and loss on a comparable basis with annualized revenue above RMB 6 billion (the story becomes a business). Neither is visible this quarter.
