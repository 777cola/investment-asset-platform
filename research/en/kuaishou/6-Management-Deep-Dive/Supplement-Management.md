## Three major capital allocation moves

The first was the February 2021 IPO. Priced at HKD 115, it raised about HKD 41.3 billion and the market cap briefly touched HKD 1.7 trillion after listing. That capital funded three years of content subsidies, e-commerce infrastructure and algorithm investment. By the outcome, the money went to the right places—DAU grew from 300 million to 400 million—but was raised at the wrong price, with the IPO price still 4x the current share price.

The second was the post-2024 contraction and buybacks. Marketing expense ratio fell from above 50% in 2021 to just over 30%, and adjusted net profit turned positive in 2024. Classic good capital allocation: stop buying growth, start buying profit.

The third was the July 2026 spin-off of Kling. It raised USD 3 billion at a post-money valuation of USD 18 billion, diluting the parent by 16.67%. A smart move—pricing the AI asset separately at its most expensive point and securing cash without losing control. But it also moves the question of "how much is Kling worth" off the financial statements: the parent will report only equity-method income going forward, with no visibility into standalone revenue and losses.

## Buybacks, though a minor item, reveal a judgment on intrinsic value

HKD 880 million, 19.60 million shares, average price HKD 44.9. That trade says more than any earnings-call statement: management believed Kuaishou at HKD 45 was undervalued. Six months later the market had it at 29.26 — they were 35% wrong.

The timing is the bigger problem. The Q2 buyback came before results, and Q2 delivered revenue +1.4% and net profit -30.3%. Buying back stock at close to 20x forward P/E one quarter before earnings decline shows insiders have no forward visibility into their own business either.

This is not a moral issue, it is a competence issue. And competence in capital allocation cannot be fixed — you cannot install a valuation model in management.

## Governance: dual-class shares + shrinking disclosure

Kuaishou's Hong Kong WVR structure, under which each Class A share carries 10 votes, keeps control in founders' hands. The upside is that strategy can be executed over the long term — a three-year money-losing investment like Kling would long ago have been voted down by ordinary shareholders. The downside is the absence of checks and balances for minority holders, who can only read the results in the annual report.

More worrying is the narrowing scope of disclosure: from 2026 the company will no longer disclose quarterly e-commerce GMV, only merchant count; post Kling spin-off it will no longer break out revenue and losses separately; the live-streaming revenue-share ratio has never been disclosed. Three of the most critical operating variables, each vaguer than the last.

Falling transparency usually reflects defence rather than conspiracy — the company does not want to be scored quarter by quarter where it is deteriorating. For investors the result is the same: less verifiable information warrants a valuation discount, which directly affects the multiple we assign.

## Track record: no landmines, but repeated flip-flops

Five years after listing, there has been no financial fraud, no diversion of controlling-shareholder funds and no abnormal audit opinion—none of the hard failures. On this count it is cleaner than many of its China ADR peers. The soft issues are two. First, the growth story packaged at its 2021 IPO ("China's No. 1 short-video stock", a market cap of RMB 170 billion): when the bubble burst, investors took heavy losses. Second, the credibility of management guidance—on the August 2026 earnings call, management conceded that e-commerce marketing and commission revenue would come under pressure in the second half, information that was not adequately flagged in Q1.

## Incentives: change the KPI, and behavior changes

Before 2024, Kuaishou's core KPIs were DAU and revenue; after 2025, the metrics that came to dominate internally were "AI revenue share" and "Kling ARR." When KPIs change, resource allocation follows: the same algorithm team that once optimised ad CTR now optimises video-generation availability; the same budget that once bought traffic now buys compute.

That is not wrong in itself, but it explains a counterintuitive outcome: in the year the company invested most aggressively in AI, core business revenue growth slowed to 1.4%. Incentives pulled the best people away from the main battlefield, and the main battlefield is losing.

## Succession: not a near-term issue

Cheng Yixiao was born in 1985 and turns 41 in 2026, so near-term succession pressure is limited. The real issue is organisational: after the Kling spin-off, the most battle-hardened technical talent went with the subsidiary, leaving the parent with a defensive team focused on advertising and livestreaming. This is less a succession question than one of who is still willing to stay in the core business.

Should Kling list independently, the parent would become a holding platform—at which point its valuation logic shifts entirely from "growth stock" to "an advertising company that holds a stake in Kling," and its P/E steps down further.
