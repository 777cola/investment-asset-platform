Core thesis

Profit distribution across the short-video value chain is highly uneven: three layers capture the bulk of the economics — upstream compute (NVIDIA gross margin 70%+), midstream platforms (Kuaishou gross margin 51.6%) and upstream content (short-drama production gross margin 20%–30%) — while the ad agencies and e-commerce fulfillment businesses sandwiched between them earn only around 10%. Kuaishou sits at the midstream platform layer, one of the most profitable positions in the chain, but its bargaining power is being eroded from both ends.

It holds bargaining power over upstream creators (it sets the revenue-share ratio), yet gave up 4.1pct of gross margin this year; over downstream advertisers it has almost none, since budgets are portable; and vis-à-vis compute suppliers it is a price taker. The only lever that can change its position is Kling — the shift from "selling traffic" to "selling productivity".

Who makes money in the chain: three layers split the pie

The first layer is compute. Video generation and recommendation algorithms both consume GPUs. Nvidia's gross margin exceeds 70%; cloud providers run 30%–40%. This layer's good times stem from scarcity, and scarcity has not eased in 2026—tight supply of memory and HBM has propped up prices across the entire AI infrastructure stack.

The second layer is platforms. Kuaishou's gross margin is 51.6%; Douyin's is higher. Platforms make money by "allocating attention"—they neither produce content nor fulfil orders, theoretically the best position to occupy. But this layer is paying ever more for content—revenue sharing has climbed from 2025 levels to today, directly consuming 4.1pct of gross margin.

The third layer is content and fulfilment. Short-drama producers run 20%–30% gross margins, surviving on platform revenue sharing; express delivery and warehousing run 5%–10% gross margins, surviving on scale. Neither layer holds pricing power, though the short-drama layer briefly gained bargaining power in 2026 on scarce supply.

Kuaishou's position in the value chain: mid-stream, but both ends are shifting

In one sentence: Kuaishou is China's third-largest attention wholesaler, buying from creators and selling to advertisers. Its position beats JD.com's (no inventory) and iQiyi's (no content rights to buy), but trails Douyin's (lower-spending audience, weaker ad products).

Upstream shifts work against it: creators demand ever-higher revenue shares as Douyin and Hongguo poach them, and AI training is pushing up compute costs. Downstream shifts are no friendlier: advertiser budgets are concentrating on platforms with higher ROI, and Kuaishou's ROI data trails Douyin's.

The one change in its favour is content-format iteration: short dramas, livestream e-commerce and AI-generated content each reshuffle creator supply once more.Kuaishou has kept pace well in short dramas (supply up 5x in six months), but Hongguo has poached its talent with higher revenue shares.

Another shift in the chain: e-commerce is moving from livestreaming to shelf-based retail

Value distribution in livestream e-commerce is shifting toward the "shelf" side. Livestreaming requires merchants to pay hosts slot fees and commissions — a high-cost model — whereas shelf e-commerce incurs only advertising and commission costs, a low-cost model. Douyin and Kuaishou are both redirecting traffic from livestream rooms to their malls, an industry-wide move.

For Kuaishou, the shift cuts both ways: it lowers merchants' fulfillment costs and aids retention, but livestream slot fees and tipping commissions are its high-margin revenue sources, and the migration to shelf-based commerce tilts the revenue mix toward lower-margin commissions. This is one of the structural reasons behind "other services +18.5%, gross margin -4.1pct."

The only path upward: from distribution to tools

There is only one way out of being squeezed from both sides: move upstream and become a production tool. Kling is exactly that move—it turns Kuaishou from "helping you be seen" into "helping you produce."

The two businesses have entirely different economic structures. Distribution carries a marginal cost that approaches zero; tools carry a marginal cost of inference compute. Distribution's customers are advertisers, whose budgets can migrate; tools' customers are enterprises, which are hard to switch once embedded in workflows. If Kling succeeds, Kuaishou's gross margin structure shifts from 51.6% to above 60%, since the cost of subscription revenue is mainly compute and dilutes with scale.

If it fails, Kuaishou is an advertising platform facing continued gross margin decline, and its valuation drifts toward that of a utility. This is the single most important fork in the road for the company right now.

Cycle position: three cycles, two trending down

Advertising cycle: bottoming out. Macro consumption will be weak in 2026 and advertisers are cutting budgets, but this is characteristic of a cyclical trough—as consumption recovers in 2027, ad growth has room to rebound.

Content cycle: the short-drama dividend is being carved up.In the first half of 2026, short dramas were Kuaishou's most effective lever for time spent; in the second half, Hongguo pulled supply away with a free model plus higher revenue sharing. The peak of this cycle has passed.

Technology cycle: early stage, and uncertain.Demand for video generation is real, but the business model is not yet settled, and a price war has already begun (API unit prices fell sharply within a year).

With the three cycles overlapping, Kuaishou's position is: the core business waits at the cyclical bottom for recovery, new businesses wait in the early cycle for a settled model. Both kinds of waiting take time, and the share price gives no time.

Conclusion

Industry-chain takeaway: Kuaishou's position is not bad, but its moat comes from scale, not position. The platform layer should be the most profitable layer, yet it is squeezed from both sides—upstream revenue sharing and downstream budgets—and gross margin fell 4.1pct in a year. Kling is the only move that can change its position, but it now accounts for only 2.4% of revenue.

Actionable implication: treat this company as a "cyclical recovery + option" combination, not as a "platform monopoly." The former requires a low price, the latter requires high certainty—right now it has neither.
