Core view

Robin Li made the earliest and most resolute AI bet among Chinese internet founders: he set up the Institute of Deep Learning in 2013, launched Ernie in 2019, and by 2026 AI accounts for 53% of core business.Strategically he was right; on capital allocation he fails.Two large acquisitions (91 Wireless at USD 1.9 billion, YY Live at USD 3.6 billion) created no value, and iQIYI burned cash for a decade before turning a profit; with RMB 276.6 billion of cash on the balance sheet, he bought back USD 373 million of stock during the year, less than 2% of market cap, has never paid a dividend, and the first payout remains only "expected to be announced before year-end."This is not a question of ability but of willingness.

Strategy: He was right, and five years ahead of most

Baidu founded its Institute of Deep Learning in 2013, an outlier in China's internet sector at the time—while peers were still fighting over traffic gateways, it was hiring PhDs. It released ERNIE in 2019, pivoted fully to large models in 2023, and in 2026 its AI business reached RMB 13.2 billion, 53% of general business, above half for three consecutive quarters. This timeline is not luck; it is judgment.

Execution has not lagged either: AI cloud infrastructure hit RMB 8.2 billion in a single quarter, +94% year on year, with GPU cloud +191% and +43% quarter on quarter; Apollo Go covers 30 cities and 390 million cumulative kilometers. A 58-year-old founder who can push the company from search advertising to chips + cloud + models + autonomous driving is rare even by global internet standards. On strategy, a 9 out of 10 is not excessive.

Capital allocation: two large acquisitions, two failures to create value

The hardest metric for management is capital allocation, not strategic slogans. Baidu's record here does not look good. In 2013 it paid USD 1.9 billion for 91 Wireless to buy a mobile gateway; integration failed and large impairments followed. In 2020–2021 it paid USD 3.6 billion for YY Live's domestic business, buying a declining livestreaming asset whose synergies have yet to appear. The two deals total USD 5.5 billion, close to a quarter of its current market cap.

It has also got things right: iQIYI burned cash for a decade but turned an annual profit in 2022, preserving a card in long-form video; Kunlunxin has received sustained investment and became a strategic asset when compute supply tightened; Apollo Go has persisted for seven years and generated the world's largest operating dataset. These are evidence of "long-termism."

But the moves of the past three years have gone off course: investing when it should be returning cash, saving when it should be investing. R&D expense this quarter was RMB 4.8 billion, down 7% year on year, just when AI cloud needs heavy R&D spending; meanwhile RMB 276.6 billion of cash sits on the balance sheet and buybacks totaled only USD 373 million. That combination is hard to explain.

Governance: dual-class super-voting rights + never pays dividends

The shareholding structure is the key to understanding Baidu. Robin Li holds less than 20% of the equity but controls more than half the voting power through Class B ordinary shares (10 votes per share). The upside is that strategy has not wavered in twenty years; the downside is that minority shareholders have no say at all. If they want dividends or larger buybacks, they can only wait for the board to come around on its own — no mechanism can force it.

The integrity record carries two scars. First, the 2016 Wei Zexi incident, when medical paid-search advertising triggered a nationwide public-opinion crisis and the company was ordered to rectify its medical promotion practices — to this day the original sin of "search commercialization." Second, a long-standing bias toward sales over experience: ad density is high and user experience is repeatedly sacrificed. Neither is fraud; both put commercial interests ahead of user interests — under an ESG framework, this is one source of the governance discount.

The good side: no anomalies in the VIE structure, related-party transactions or audit opinions; disclosure quality improved after the 2021 secondary listing in Hong Kong; and there has been no large-scale management departure or infighting.

The compensation structure also merits a mention. Under the 20-F basis, the company discloses only aggregate compensation for "directors and executive officers," not individual figures; actual incentives are mainly equity, with cash compensation a small share. This structure motivates when the share price is high and demotivates after a 60% decline — options are out of the money and retention rests on sentiment. R&D expense falling 7% year on year in 2026 is, in part, a result of the diminished appeal of equity incentives.

Succession: no public plan; a long-term risk

Robin Li was born in 1968 and is 58 in 2026; he has never discussed succession arrangements in public. The company has no COO and no clear "number two." Wang Haifeng runs technology, Shen Dou runs cloud, He Junjie runs the mobile ecosystem and Wang Yunpeng runs autonomous driving; each of the four lines reports to the CEO, and none has been elevated to heir apparent.

This structure is highly efficient while the founder is strong—fast decisions, unified direction; but once the founder exits, the four lines would immediately fall into a power vacuum. Against Tencent (the dual core of Liu Chiping and Ren Yuxin) and Alibaba (the partnership system), Baidu's succession risk is clearly higher. For long-term holders this is a factor that must be priced in as a discount, and one more reason the assets look cheap but few dare to take a heavy position.

Conclusion

Management score 6/10: Strategy 9, Execution 7, Capital allocation 3, Governance 5, Succession 4. Robin Li has steered the company in the right AI direction, but he does not treat shareholders' money as his own—two mistaken acquisitions of USD 5.5 billion each, five years without dividends, and buybacks of less than 2% of market cap are all evidence. The implied meaning of the "Watch" rating: wait for management to prove through actions that it cares about the share price (dividends, stepped-up buybacks), not just say at results briefings that "the investment payback period is shortening."
