## Why not build a heavy position: it is an option, not a bond

A heavy position demands "limited downside plus certain upside." Baidu offers only the first half: RMB 276.6 billion in cash and investments and 0.52x PB mean the downside is genuinely floored. The upside is not—advertising is shrinking, cloud is burning cash, and the dividend has yet to materialise. For a name with downside protection but no upside certainty, the allocation logic is that of an "option," and the position must be small.

The math: at a 10% position, a neutral scenario of +34% or a bear case of -29% contributes +3.4% or -2.9% to the portfolio. The same 10% in Tencent buys certainty in social cash flow. On that opportunity cost, 6% is already the ceiling.

## Correlation: it buys China ADR beta, not AI alpha

Correlation is the most overlooked item in position management. Baidu trades in lockstep with the Hang Seng Tech Index: in 2026 the index fell more than 25% while Baidu fell 60.4% — same direction, larger magnitude (beta greater than 1). Its correlation coefficient with Tencent and Alibaba is roughly 0.7–0.8, and only about 0.3 with the Nvidia supply chain.

Two implications follow. First, holding Baidu is not "diversification" — it, Tencent and Alibaba are three expressions of the same China internet position, and together they should not exceed 15% of the portfolio. Second, Baidu is no longer part of the AI trade: on 11/23, Nvidia rose 31% while Baidu fell 0.78% — that is the evidence. For AI exposure, Baidu is not the tool; for China internet assets at a discount, it is.

## Rebalancing discipline: three quarters of no action is the answer

This rule matters more than the entry price: if neither trigger appears for three consecutive quarters (Q1–Q3 2027), zero out the observation position and stop tracking it. The rationale is opportunity cost, not bearishness—a stock that gives no signal for two years is structurally cheap, not mispriced.

Conversely, if the dividend materialises and is meaningful (say, an annual payout ratio above 2%), the position can go to 3% even with advertising still falling. A change in capital allocation is itself worth pricing, because it turns an "asset" into "cash flow"—the definition of a value stock.

## If the portfolio already holds China ADRs, start with the aggregate exposure before discussing individual names

Most portfolios that allocate to Baidu already hold Tencent or Alibaba. In that case the first step is not deciding how much Baidu to buy, but calculating total China ADR exposure. Suppose the portfolio is 100%, with Tencent at 8%, Alibaba at 5% and Baidu at 2%, for a combined China exposure of 15% — already at the cap. Any further addition to Baidu can only come from trimming Tencent or Alibaba.

The order of trimming matters: cut the least elastic position first, not the biggest loser. Tencent's social cash flow is the most certain, making it the ballast; Alibaba's cloud re-rating carries the most elasticity, making it the offensive position; Baidu's asset discount is the defensive position. The three serve different functions, so the trimming order should be "cut Baidu, keep Alibaba, hold Tencent" — not the reverse.

One more angle, cash: if the portfolio's cash ratio is below 10%, skip the 2% in Baidu altogether — it is not a must-hold asset but an option you can choose not to buy. Holding cash and waiting for a trigger signal beats taking the position early.
