Duan Yongping-style Q&A

Run Baidu through Duan Yongping's framework and six questions, and none of the answers comes easily. The business is not a good one (advertising is disappearing, cloud is selling electricity), the picture ten years out is unclear, it is understandable but uncomfortable, the price is cheap enough to "run the numbers on," and the biggest fear is that "cheap can get cheaper." He would most likely say: "At this price I'd take a look, but I wouldn't buy." It is not that he doesn't recognize cheap; it is that he doesn't recognize this business's future.

Q: Is this a good business?

"Not great. It used to be a wonderful business — search advertising, high gross margin, no capital outlay, customers pay upfront. That is the kind of business I like at first glance. Of the two things it is doing now, one is shrinking and the other does not make money.

Advertising came in at RMB 11.9 billion, down 22% — this is not a one- or two-year issue, user habits have changed. Cloud came in at RMB 8.2 billion, up 94%, which looks impressive, but selling compute is much like selling electricity: anyone with the cards can sell it, gross margin is 20-30%, and you have to buy the cards first. Capex this quarter was RMB 7.3 billion, more than it earns, and free cash flow was negative RMB 4.5 billion.

The business model has changed, from collecting rent to hauling bricks. Hauling bricks can make money too, but it takes hard work and thrift, and anyone else can haul them as well. I generally do not much like this kind."

Q: Will it still be around in ten years?

"I can't say for sure. The company will certainly still be around—it has over RMB 200 billion in cash on the books, so it won't collapse. But 'the company still being around' and 'the business still being around' are two different things.

Ten years ago we called search a gateway; now young people with questions ask AI directly—they don't search anymore. I see this change, but I don't know how fast it's moving. Maybe five years, maybe ten. Baidu's app still has 600 million monthly active users—the people are still there, but what they come for, I don't know.

Cloud should still be around in ten years—compute demand is long-term. But it will become a utility, like electricity—prices regulated, margins low. I understand this kind of business, but I don't like it—companies that earn hard money don't get high valuations."

Q: Can you understand it?

"I understand the business, I understand the accounts, but I don't understand the future. My principle is 'don't invest in what you don't understand,' but 'understand' doesn't mean understanding the product — it means understanding how it makes money ten years from now.

I can explain all three of Baidu's businesses clearly: how ads are sold, how cloud is billed, how Apollo Go runs. But three things I can't explain: what AI search will ultimately make money from, how far the cloud price war will go, and when Apollo Go will no longer need safety drivers.

If I can't answer these three questions, I don't count as understanding it. If I don't understand it, I don't buy it — that has nothing to do with how cheap it is. I've seen plenty of cheap things; cheap things I don't understand always turn out expensive in the end."

Q: At what price would you buy?

Good question — but I won't answer it with price. Cheap is not a reason to buy; 'cheap plus things getting better' is.

The stock trades at HKD 63.5, a market cap of HKD 168 billion, against cash and investments on the books of RMB 276.6 billion — 0.52x book. The math is simple, and it does screen cheap: the market is effectively giving away AI cloud, search and Apollo Go for free.

But I won't buy on cheapness alone. I'll wait for one of two things: first, advertising stops declining — down no more than 10% YoY, say; second, cloud starts making money — over RMB 10 billion in a single quarter with positive operating profit. If either shows up, I'm willing to buy a little at this price. If both show up, I can buy more.

If neither shows up, I won't buy even at HKD 45. What I'm buying isn't cheapness — it's a stabilised business.

Q: What do you fear most?

"What I fear most isn't the fall — it's 'cheap with no end in sight.'

I've seen companies like this: plenty of cash on the books, the business slowly shrinking, management says 'we're transforming' every year, and the stock goes lower year after year. Every time you think it has bottomed, it drops another 20%. That isn't value investing — it's a race against time, and time is on the other side.

The second fear is that the money gets spent. RMB 276.6 billion looks like a lot, but at annual capex of RMB 29.2 billion it's gone in ten years. And what gets spent goes into buying chips and building data centers — assets that depreciate in three to five years. If the return on that investment is only 2% or 3%, that money is slowly disappearing for shareholders.

The third fear is 'sudden improvement' — it actually makes AI work, the stock doubles in a year, and I'm not on board. That fear I accept: missing out doesn't lose money."

Q: What if I already hold it?

"If you hold the stock, ask yourself: why did you buy it in the first place?

If the answer was 'it's cheap and Chinese ADRs will rebound,' think it through — that is not a reason, it is a wish. If it was 'asset discount plus AI optionality,' you can hold, but keep the position small — no more than 5% or 6%.

Then set two lines for yourself: if advertising falls back below 25%, or capex climbs back above RMB 10 billion, get out, don't hesitate. This has nothing to do with the share price. In all my years investing, the most important thing is not what to buy, but what not to do. No leverage, no shorting, no businesses you don't understand. Baidu right now falls into 'worth watching, no need to act.'

Stay level-headed. The market offers opportunities every day; there is no need to force a rationale in a place you don't understand."

Conclusion

Duan Yongping's verdict: "At this price I'd take a look, but I wouldn't buy." He does not dispute the cheapness — 0.52x PB, RMB 276.6 billion in cash, the math is easy. What he disputes is the future of the business: advertising is disappearing, cloud is being sold like electricity, and it is unclear what will make money ten years from now. Baidu belongs in his "understandable but unappealing" column, and he would wait for one of two signals before looking again. "Don't do what you don't understand" matters more than "grabbing a bargain."
