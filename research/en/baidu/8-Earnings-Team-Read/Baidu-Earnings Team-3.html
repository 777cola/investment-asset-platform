Core view

Munger's first move on any financial report is to hunt for "adjustments." At Baidu, operating profit of RMB 630 million becomes adjusted EBITDA of RMB 4.5 billion — a 7x gap built entirely from adjustments. What fills that gap: share-based compensation, depreciation and amortisation, impairments, investment gains. Adjustments are not the objection; adjusting only the good and not the bad is.

Adjusting by 7x: EBITDA is useful, and also a fig leaf

Operating profit of RMB 630 million against adjusted EBITDA of RMB 4.5 billion—a 7.1x gap. Two items explain it: share-based compensation of about RMB 1.07 billion (taking 630 million to 1.7 billion) and depreciation and amortisation of about RMB 2.8 billion (taking 1.7 billion to 4.5 billion).

Munger was always harsh on EBITDA: "EBITDA is for people who think depreciation isn't a cost." For a company like Baidu, mid-transition to an asset-heavy model, that applies with particular force—depreciation is the money spent on GPUs amortised over time, a real cash outflow, merely shifted in timing. Viewing Baidu through EBITDA means pretending the cloud business needs no capex.

Share-based compensation is no different. It drains no cash, but it drains equity and dilutes existing shareholders. For a company whose R&D expense fell 7% YoY and which still relies on large share-based awards to retain staff, that signals weak cash compensation competitiveness—not a "non-cash cost" but a "real cost."

RMB 16.19bn impairment: last year's hole, this year's step-up

In Q3 2025 a RMB 16.19 billion impairment of long-term assets directly produced a RMB 11.23 billion loss. With no impairment in the same period this year, net profit attributable to shareholders of RMB 1.40 billion looks like a "turnaround." This is an accounting phenomenon, not an operating improvement.

Munger would press: which assets were impaired last year? If they were search-related intangibles, servers and content investment, management at the time was admitting "these assets are not worth that much." That admission is a good thing—the books are more real. But it also flags a risk: if returns on AI cloud assets also fall short, a second round of impairment follows. The RMB 16.19 billion figure is management's one-time acknowledgment that "transition assets were overstated," not the last one.

The test is simple: watch the annual asset impairment loss. If a large impairment appears again in the 2026 annual report, the cycle of "impairment—transition—re-impairment" is repeating.

The accounting trap: what lies behind three "good-looking" numbers

"Net profit attributable to shareholders turned positive" — on last year's low base; strip out the base effect and non-GAAP is -36%. "AI business accounts for 53%" — the share rose partly because the denominator (total revenue) is shrinking, not only because the numerator is growing. "Operating cash flow is positive" — RMB 2.8 billion is positive, but net of RMB 7.3 billion of capex it is -RMB 4.5 billion.

Munger's advice: when reading financial statements, first translate every "good news" into "why it holds up"; whatever cannot be translated is spin. All three numbers can be translated, but they look far less pretty once translated. This is not fraud; it is selective narrative — every company does it, and investors must break it down themselves.

Conclusion

Munger-style conclusion: the books are credible, the narrative is not. The GAAP numbers show no sign of fabrication—impairments, depreciation and share-based compensation are all real line items. But the company's three headline claims—"AI now more than half of revenue," "returned to profit" and "EBITDA of RMB 450 million"—are selective narratives. Two figures, operating profit of RMB 630 million and free cash flow of -RMB 4.5 billion, tell you more about this company than any non-GAAP metric.
