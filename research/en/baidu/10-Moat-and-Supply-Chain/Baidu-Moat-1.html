Barrier assessment

Five moats scored item by item: brand 6, cost 3, network 5, switching cost 4, licenses 7, totalling 25/50 (simple sum of the five). Licenses are the strongest (Class A mapping survey qualification, autonomous driving test permits, cloud and payment licenses); cost is the weakest (cloud has no scale advantage). Baidu's moat was not breached but bypassed: rivals did not seize the search box; they changed how users ask questions. That erosion is slower than head-on competition but harder to reverse.

Industry-chain position: full-stack is both an advantage and a burden

Baidu is the only player in China's AI landscape that owns chips (Kunlunxin), cloud (AI Cloud at RMB 8.2 billion/quarter), models (Ernie) and scenarios (search, Apollo Go) under one roof. Technically, the pieces reinforce each other: chips can be tuned to its own models, the cloud can run inference for those models, and the scenarios feed data back into them.

Commercially, though, full-stack means fighting on four fronts at once, each against a stronger rival: chips against Nvidia and Ascend, cloud against Alibaba Cloud and Volcano Engine, models against Doubao and DeepSeek, Robotaxi against Pony.ai and WeRide. Resources are spread thin, and on every front the best it can manage is "not falling behind" — never "leading."

Supply chain: GPUs, power and bandwidth — all three depend on others

The AI cloud supply chain has three segments: compute chips, data centre power and network bandwidth. Baidu's response is "half in-house, half purchased": Kunlun chips secure baseline supply while high-end cards still have to be bought externally; IDC capacity is partly self-built and partly leased; bandwidth is purchased from carriers.

The weak link is high-end cards. In 2026 global AI chips are in short supply, with both advanced process nodes and HBM tight—whoever secures inventory can expand capacity. Baidu reduces external dependence through Kunlun chips, but Kunlun's absolute performance trails Nvidia, requiring more cards and more power to build training clusters—the result is "saving on procurement, spending on electricity," and cost per unit of compute may not be lower.

The other segment is traffic acquisition. The 9% increase in cost of sales includes "traffic acquisition," indicating Baidu must buy traffic externally to fill the gap at its own entry points. A company that once monopolised Chinese-language search traffic has begun paying for traffic—the most alarming change in the supply chain.

Five scores: the licence is the only thing not depreciating

Brand 6 points. "Baidu it" remains muscle memory for a generation, and Chinese search's brand recognition is irreplaceable. But for users under 25, the first app they open is Doubao or Quark. The brand is aging, not collapsing. The medical-advertising cleanup after the 2016 Wei Zexi incident also left a long-term drag on the brand—which is why it scores 6 points, not 8 points.

Cost 3 points. The weakest item. Cloud is a scale business: Alibaba Cloud's scale is several times Baidu's, giving it lower unit costs, while Volcano Engine has ByteDance's cash flow behind it. Baidu spends RMB 7.3 billion of quarterly capex for RMB 8.2 billion of cloud revenue—a poor input-output ratio.

Network effects 5 points. The search-era network effect was "more users → more click data → more accurate ranking," and that flywheel has failed in the LLM era—model capability depends on pre-training and compute, not user clicks. The flywheel has stalled, and this is the most fundamental damage.

Switching costs 4 points. Advertisers can move budgets in a day; cloud customers can run multi-cloud in parallel by training batch; consumer users migrate at zero cost. All three are low.

Licenses 7 points. Class-A mapping survey qualifications, autonomous-driving road-test and commercial-operation permits, cloud and payment licenses—these are administrative gatekeepers that new entrants cannot obtain and incumbents will not lose. This is Baidu's only moat that does not depreciate as technology shifts, and the precondition for Apollo Go to operate in 30 cities.

The most dangerous rival: ByteDance's Doubao

Not Alibaba Cloud, not Tencent — ByteDance's Doubao. Because what it is taking is not Baidu's customers but Baidu's entry point. Users with a question no longer open a search box; they open a chat box. Once that behaviour becomes habit, Baidu's 604 million MAU slowly turns into an "aging curve of the existing user base."

Doubao's advantage is threefold: ByteDance's cash flow (no need to monetise through cloud), Douyin's traffic pool (a natural distribution channel), and sustained investment in model capability. It does not need to beat Baidu at search; it only needs to make "searching" unnecessary.

The second layer of threat is the scale advantage Alibaba Cloud and Volcano Engine hold in AI cloud. Once a price war starts, Baidu's RMB 8.2 billion/quarter cloud revenue will struggle to hold its 94% growth rate — which is why this report assigns AI cloud only RMB 50 billion of revenue in 2027 (roughly 40% annualised growth). The real risk is not being defeated, but being squeezed simultaneously at the entry point and on the cost side.

Conclusion

Moat conclusion: 25/50 (weighted 29/50), below average and depreciating. Brand, network effects and switching costs are all eroding, the cost advantage was thin to begin with, and only the licence remains stable. On this structure Baidu cannot sell a "moat" story and can only be priced on "assets + optionality". Investment implication: no growth premium; wait for two hard signals — advertising stabilising or cloud profit turning positive.
