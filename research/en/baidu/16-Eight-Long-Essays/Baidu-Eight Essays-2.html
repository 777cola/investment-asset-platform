Core view

Baidu now runs three businesses at once, each with a completely different return-on-capital structure: selling intent (advertising), selling compute (cloud), selling miles (Robotaxi). The first is disappearing, the second survives but does not make money, and the third is unproven. All three share one management team, one balance sheet and one brand—this is Baidu's biggest strategic problem: resources are spread thin across three tracks with different rhythms.

Sell thesis: the best business is disappearing

Search advertising is at heart an "auction of intent." The moment a user enters a keyword, intent is at its clearest and most valuable, and advertisers bid for that intent. The beauty of this business: inventory is infinite (keyword combinations are infinite), marginal cost is zero, customers pay upfront, and there is no need to build factories or buy equipment.

Its decline is not because advertisers ran out of money, but because the act of "entering a keyword" is shrinking. AI search delivers answers directly; users read and leave without clicking links. No links means no ad slots. This is not "ad performance getting worse," it is "ad inventory disappearing."

The numbers are blunt: online marketing RMB 11.9 billion, -22%, 39% of total revenue. Three years ago that share was above 70%. The company is trying to catch the fall with "AI-native marketing," but that pool is only RMB 2.4 billion and still -15%. The old is gone, the new has not arrived.

Selling compute: survivable, but not profitable

The essence of AI cloud is "slicing capex into hours and selling them." Its economics mirror those of electricity exactly: heavy asset investment first, then dilute fixed costs through utilization, and ultimately compete on scale and power prices.

Run the numbers: quarterly capex of RMB 7.3 billion against cloud revenue of RMB 8.2 billion. Assuming GPUs and data centers depreciate over 4 years, RMB 7.3 billion of capex generates roughly RMB 1.8 billion of depreciation per year (rough calculation, quarterly basis); add power, bandwidth and labor, and fixed costs dominate the cloud business's cost structure. Gross margin of 20%–30% (rough calculation) means RMB 8.2 billion of revenue leaves only RMB 1.6–2.5 billion of gross profit — not enough to cover RMB 7.3 billion of capex.

So the business is currently cash-flow negative. It survives because it holds RMB 276.6 billion on the books; it doesn't make money because scale is insufficient. There are only two paths to profitability: scale up (can't beat Alibaba or Volcano) or raise prices (impossible in a price war).

Selling miles: an option, not a business

The essence of Robotaxi is "replacing driver costs with autonomous driving." In the ride-hailing cost structure, the driver takes the largest share; if the driver can be removed, per-kilometer cost can fall by more than half—that is the entire logic of this business.

But the reality today is: vehicles still need a safety operator, and regulators require a human-to-vehicle ratio of no less than 1:3. One person watching three cars means only two-thirds of driver cost is removed, while new costs are added for remote takeover centers, sensor maintenance and insurance. Factor in vehicle depreciation (cars with lidar cost far more than ordinary ride-hailing vehicles), and per-kilometer cost at this stage is most likely higher than ride-hailing.

Baidu's lead is real: operations in 30 cities, 34 million kilometers in a single quarter, 390 million cumulative kilometers, the widest operating footprint globally. But this lead is a lead in "technology + data," not in "business." Its current role is an option: if by 2028 it can remove safety operators in 3–5 cities and make per-vehicle economics work, it is a valuable card; if not, these kilometers are R&D expense.

Three businesses sharing one team: the real strategic question

Put the three businesses on one balance sheet and the problem surfaces. Advertising needs "defend the gateway, lift ad efficiency"; cloud needs "spend hard to expand capacity, chase scale"; Robotaxi needs "long-term investment, wait for regulation." Three tempos, three playbooks, one CEO.

The traces of resource allocation show up in the financials: R&D expense RMB 4.8 billion, down 7% year on year (cutting R&D in the middle of an AI race), sales and administrative expense RMB 5.5 billion, down 17% year on year (shrinking the organization), capex RMB 7.3 billion (down from RMB 11.4 billion). Together the three numbers mean one thing: there isn't enough money to fight three wars at once, so every front gets fought on a tight budget.

This is the commercial price of "full stack." Full stack in technology is an advantage; full stack in business is dispersed resources. Buffett said the most important thing for a company is "knowing what not to do." Baidu is doing all three, and it is not the strongest in any of them. If it cut two and concentrated on one, the valuation logic would be completely different — but it won't cut, because each one is a strategic bet by Robin Li.

Conclusion

The business in one sentence: Baidu is a company using cash from "selling intent" to fund "selling compute" and "selling miles," and the former is disappearing. This structure holds as long as cash flow has slack; once advertising's cash contribution falls short of covering cloud capex, a choice becomes unavoidable. That point arrives when advertising revenue drops below RMB 9 billion per quarter—at the current rate of decline, 4–6 quarters away.
