Moat dissection: a river that got bypassed — Baidu

Core view

A moat is not "what I have" but "what rivals cannot get around." Baidu's defence rests on four pillars: traffic (604 million MAU), data (two decades of Chinese-language corpus), chips (Kunlun) and licences (mapping and road-test permits). Tested one by one, the first two are depreciating, the third is a cost tool rather than a barrier, and only the fourth still holds. Yet its rivals (Doubao, Quark, Yuanbao) never set out to cross the river—they changed its course.

Entry point: from "intent monopoly" to "intent drain"

Search's moat was never technology; it was habit. When users want to look something up, their first move is to open Baidu—muscle memory built over twenty years, an "intent monopoly." Advertisers pay for this gateway because it concentrates the most explicit intent.

The moat did not collapse overnight; it eroded as the "first move" became the "second move." The path now is: ask AI first, search only when AI cannot answer. Baidu has degraded from "gateway" to "fallback tool." A fallback tool still has value, but of far lower value density—users arrive without explicit intent, so ads cannot command a price.

604 million MAU remains, and this is why many are bullish on Baidu. But MAU measures "people," not "intent." A gateway with 600 million MAU but half the intent density may be worth only a third of its former value. The financials show it: online marketing RMB 11.9 billion, -22%, while MAU did not fall 22%. The gap is the erosion of "intent density."

Data: In the pretraining era, the corpus moat is depreciating

Data is Baidu's second-largest asset: two decades of search logs, click behaviour and web indexes. In the traditional search era this was the hardest moat—more data meant more accurate ranking, which meant more satisfied users, which meant more data, and the flywheel spun.

In the large-model era, the flywheel has slowed. Model capability gains come mainly from pre-training corpus scale, compute and algorithms—not "user click data." Anyone can crawl public internet corpora; high-quality Chinese corpora are scarce but not exclusive to Baidu (publishers, academic databases, Zhihu and WeChat official accounts all sell licences).

More critically, Baidu's search data has a "short query + click" structure, whereas large models need "long text + reasoning chains." The two data types carry different value. Baidu holds massive short queries, which in the AI era are an asset of "user intent distribution," not of "model capability"—useful for training ad recommendations, not foundation models. The data moat has not disappeared, but it has been downgraded from a "core asset" to a "supporting asset."

Chips: Supply security is not a competitive advantage

Kunlunxin is Baidu's most overvalued asset. Its value lies in "not being strangled": with global AI chip supply falling short of demand in 2026, in-house chips secure a slice of compute supply without queuing for Nvidia. That matters strategically.

But supply security is not competitive advantage. Competitive advantage means lower cost or better performance than rivals. Kunlunxin trails Nvidia on performance and is weaker on ecosystem (CUDA compatibility, framework support); building large-scale training clusters requires more cards and burns more power — the result is savings on procurement offset by extra spend on electricity, with unit compute cost not necessarily lower.

Kunlunxin is also mainly for internal use and is not sold externally at scale, so it generates no revenue and only lowers costs. An asset that only saves cost and generates no revenue is a cost-optimisation item in valuation, not a moat. Valuing it as a "Chinese Nvidia" is the most common error in this type of report.

License: the only moat that does not depreciate

Of the four moats, only licenses are hard. Class-A mapping qualifications, autonomous driving road-test and commercial operation permits, cloud service and payment licenses—all are administrative gatekeepers. New entrants cannot obtain them, and incumbents will not have them revoked.

Licenses matter most in Robotaxi. Apollo Go can operate in 30 cities not because its technology is the best (Waymo's may be stronger), but because it holds operating permits in 30 cities. Autonomous driving commercialisation is a "license business + technology business"—you need to clear both to run. That is also why Apollo Go's lead is more "real" than its lead in AI cloud: cloud can be chased with money, licenses cannot.

But licenses have boundaries: they protect you from being squeezed out by new entrants, not from being bypassed by substitute solutions. If regulators allow L4 to open up in more cities, the scarcity of licenses will decline; if the Robotaxi business model itself doesn't work, licenses are meaningless. So it is a "defensive moat," not an "offensive" one.

Why "full-stack" is not a moat

Many investors treat Baidu's full stack of "chip + cloud + model + application" as its moat. That confuses capability with a barrier. A moat requires that rivals cannot go around it; a full stack merely shows that "I have everything."

What does a genuine full-stack moat look like? Apple: chips (A series) + OS (iOS) + app store + hardware, four layers reinforcing each other, so that switching to an Android device imposes huge migration costs on users. Tesla: battery + electronic control + autonomous driving + charging network, with vertical integration delivering a cost advantage. What they share: each layer creates an advantage for the next that "others cannot match."

Baidu's full stack, by contrast, is "do every layer, but every layer has a stronger rival." Its chips trail Nvidia, its cloud trails Alibaba, its models trail Doubao, and its entry point is being bypassed. This kind of full stack generates no lock-in, only cost. Its only synergy is "Kunlun chips cut cloud costs," but that synergy is not large enough to offset its scale disadvantage.

Conclusion

Dissection conclusion: Four lines of defence — three are eroding, one holds. The entry point has degraded from "intent monopoly" to "fallback tool"; data has been demoted from a core asset to a supporting one; chips are a cost-optimisation lever, not a moat; only the licence remains solid, and it matters only for Robotaxi. Total moat score: 25/50 — not a company with a moat, but one "not yet fully displaced."
