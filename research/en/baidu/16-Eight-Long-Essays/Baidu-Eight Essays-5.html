Core view

Chinese AI has three battlegrounds: compute, models and applications. Baidu fights on all of them and leads on none. In compute it is a third-tier cloud; in models it is a second-tier ERNIE; in applications it guards an entry point that is being bypassed. Its only lead is on a fourth battleground—Robotaxi—but that battleground has yet to be decided.

The compute battlefield: a war of attrition over scale

AI cloud is the most "industrial" of the four battlegrounds: buy chips, build data centers, sell hours. Its competitiveness formula is simple — scale determines unit cost, unit cost determines price, price determines share. There is no technical secret, only price competition.

Ranked by this formula: Alibaba Cloud has the largest scale and lowest unit cost; Volcano Engine has ByteDance's cash flow and in-house model demand, so it can fight without making money; the three telecom carriers' clouds have power and government-enterprise relationships, giving them a different cost structure; Baidu AI Cloud at RMB 8.2 billion per quarter is the smallest, saving some cost via Kunlun chips and differentiating somewhat through its full stack.

The price-war timeline can be inferred: in 2026 compute is in short supply (HBM and advanced process nodes are tight), so whoever has chips makes money; in 2027 new capacity comes online, supply loosens and prices fall. Baidu's +94% was achieved inside a supply-shortage window; once the window closes, growth will drop to 30%–40%. This is not bearishness, it is arithmetic.

Model battleground: the consumer side has a clear winner, the enterprise side is still a free-for-all

Competition at the model layer splits into two parts: on the consumer side it comes down to DAU and retention; on the enterprise side, API pricing and private deployment. On the consumer side, Baidu has already lost. Doubao rode ByteDance's traffic pool to lead on MAU, Quark used the Alibaba ecosystem and its "AI search" positioning to capture the student demographic, and Yuanbao leans on the WeChat entry point. ERNIE Bot was the first to launch, but "first" is not "best to use."

The enterprise side is still a free-for-all, but the playbook has changed: model API prices have been pushed below cost, and vendors sell bundled "model + cloud + private deployment" packages. This actually works in Baidu's favor — it has cloud, chips and private-deployment delivery capability, so it can treat models as a customer-acquisition tool and make money on the cloud. That is also why the figure of "AI applications RMB 2.6 billion, flat" is not bad: it is running a "trade models for cloud" business.

But the end of that road is this: models do not make money, and neither does the cloud (20%–30% gross margin), and the two together still add up to no profit. For Baidu, the value of the model battlefield is "defense," not "offense."

Application battlefield: the war for the entry point is over

In the app battlefield, what matters is who users open first. Baidu lost this battle, and not to a search rival — it lost to the habit of not searching at all. WeChat is the social gateway, Douyin the content gateway, Doubao the Q&A gateway — Baidu has retreated from first gateway to fallback gateway.

The data shows it: 604 million MAU remain, but online marketing came in at RMB 11.9 billion, -22%. The people stayed, the money left — because intent left. Advertisers do not pay for passers-by; they pay for people who want to buy. Once MAU no longer equals intent, ad inventory shrinks.

That is why AI-native marketing of RMB 2.4 billion, -15%, is more alarming than the -22% in advertising: it shows Baidu has not made even the path of inserting ads into AI answers work. Had that path worked, the ad business would have a second spring; as things stand, it does not.

Robotaxi: the only battleground where it leads, and the most uncertain

Few players compete in this arena: Waymo and Baidu globally; Pony.ai, WeRide and Didi domestically. Baidu's edge is "operating footprint + data volume": 30 cities, 34 million km in a single quarter, 390 million km cumulative — an operating scale no rival can match.

Waymo's edge is "per-vehicle technology + high ticket size in the US market", but its city count is far below Baidu's. Pony.ai and WeRide's edge is "focus + cost control", but their scale is small. The deciding factor in this arena is not technology but "the speed of removing safety drivers" and "the speed of reaching single-city break-even".

Regulation is the biggest variable. A 1:3 human-to-vehicle ratio is the current floor; if relaxed to 1:1 or even fully remote within three years, the cost structure improves instantly; if regulation tightens, expansion stalls. This business should be valued as an "option" at HKD 0–10 billion, not a DCF. The current share price assigns it zero.

Competitive conclusion: a supporting-role position, a supporting-role valuation

Four battlegrounds: three already decided (apps lost, consumer-facing models lost, cloud behind on scale), one still too early to call (Robotaxi). That is the definition of a "supporting player": not defeated, but unable to dominate any single battleground.

Supporting players have their own way of surviving: asset discounts, niche advantages, option value. But a supporting player's valuation cannot command a leading player's multiple. The neutral scenario of HKD 85 implies a market cap of about HKD 225 billion, a fraction of Alibaba's or Tencent's — that pricing is reasonable, because it reflects exactly the "supporting player position".

Conclusion

Industry conclusion: Across China AI's four battlegrounds, Baidu trails in three and leads in one that remains unproven. This position means it cannot command a growth-stock valuation, only an "assets plus optionality" valuation. Investment implication: do not buy it for the "great AI era"; look at it because "net cash is close to market cap plus Robotaxi for free." The value of a supporting player lies in being cheap, not in being great.
