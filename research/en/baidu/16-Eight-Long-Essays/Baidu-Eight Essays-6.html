Management: one strategist, half a capitalist — Baidu

Core view

Over twenty-six years Robin Li has turned Baidu from a search company into an AI company, and his strategic calls have rarely missed the big direction: the Institute of Deep Learning in 2013, ERNIE in 2019, all in on large models in 2023. Capital allocation is another matter: two bad acquisitions totalling USD 5.5 billion, no dividend for five years, buybacks under 2% of market cap. That split between strategist and capitalist is the man-made reason for the 0.52x PB.

2000–2010: The search empire, the best decade

Robin Li returned from Silicon Valley in January 2000 to found Baidu and build a Chinese-language search engine. The company listed on Nasdaq in 2005, and its first-day gain set a record at the time. Over the following decade Baidu became the gateway to the Chinese internet: "Baidu it" became a verb, ad revenue doubled year after year, and gross margin held above 70%.

That decade of success rested on two premises—the technical barrier to Chinese search (word segmentation, indexing, ranking) and the traffic dividend of China's internet. Both began to shift after 2010, as open source and compute eroded the technical barrier and the walled-garden apps of the mobile internet fragmented the traffic dividend. Baidu's response was to "buy gateways"—the starting point for its two later large acquisitions.

2013–2019: The AI bet, earliest and loneliest

Baidu set up the Institute of Deep Learning (IDL) in 2013 and brought in a group of top scholars. In 2017 Lu Qi joined as group president and COO, driving "All in AI" and repositioning Baidu from a "search company" to an "AI company." Those two years marked Baidu's clearest strategic period, with the Apollo open platform, DuerOS and the intelligent cloud all pushed forward at once.

Lu Qi's departure in May 2018 was a turning point in Baidu's governance history. It proved the company could not retain a "number two": the organisational reform and strategic focus he brought were largely rolled back within 16 months. No manager capable of sharing power with the CEO has emerged at Baidu since; the four business lines each report to Li Yanhong.

This history matters because it explains the "succession risk": the problem is not a lack of talent but a structure that does not permit a number two to exist. A company where the founder holds less than 20% of shares but more than half the voting power naturally tends toward "one-man decision-making."

2020–2026: large models, cloud and Robotaxi — three fronts opening at once

Baidu launched ERNIE Bot in 2023, becoming China's first ChatGPT rival and lifting its share price to HKD 160.5. From 2024 the strategic focus shifted to "AI cloud + applications + Robotaxi." By 2026Q3 the results: AI business RMB 13.2 billion, 53% of core business; AI cloud RMB 8.2 billion, +94%; Apollo Go in 30 cities.

Strategically this is a success: three years ago no one believed Baidu could build an AI cloud business worth RMB 8.2 billion a quarter. Commercially, though, it comes at a cost — R&D expense -7% YoY, selling and administrative expense -17%, capex cut from RMB 11.4 billion to RMB 7.3 billion. Fighting on three fronts at once means each front can only be fought on a tight budget.

This is also the divide between "strategist" and "capitalist": the strategist asks "can I make it work," the capitalist asks "how many dollars do I get back for every dollar I put in." Robin Li scores high on the former and low on the latter.

Capital allocation: USD 5.5 billion bought two lessons

In 2013 it paid USD 1.9 billion for 91 Wireless to buy a mobile gateway; in 2020–2021 it paid USD 3.6 billion for YY Live's domestic business to buy content and traffic. Together the two deals cost USD 5.5 billion, close to a quarter of the current market cap. Neither delivered: 91 Wireless failed to integrate and was followed by large impairments, while YY was a declining asset with limited synergies.

Both deals shared the same error: paying a high price for an asset that is disappearing. The mobile gateway was already being displaced by super apps in 2013, and showroom livestreaming was already under attack from short video in 2020. The misjudgment was not about price; it was about trend.

By contrast, the investment in Kunlunxin and Apollo Go is buying the future — right direction, slow returns. The capital allocation record is therefore right at both ends, wrong in the middle: early search investment right, current AI investment right, the decade in between spent buying the wrong gateway. And that wrong decade was exactly the most profitable decade of mobile internet.

Governance and succession: a one-man company

Li Yanhong holds less than 20% of the shares but controls more than half the voting power through Class B super-voting rights. This structure ensures strategic continuity, and also ensures that minority shareholders have no say. Five years without dividends, buybacks amounting to less than 2% of market cap, and a dividend deferred to "expected to be announced before year-end" — none of these required consulting minority shareholders.

Succession is the longer-term issue. Li Yanhong was born in 1968 and is 58 in 2026; there is no public succession plan, no COO, and no clear number two. Wang Haifeng (CTO), Shen Dou (Cloud), He Junjie (MEG) and Wang Yunpeng (IDG) each report separately. This structure is most efficient while the founder is in place and most risky when the founder exits.

Implication for investors: the "people" discount on this company should not be ignored. At the same 0.52x PB, if this were a company with a clear succession mechanism and dividend discipline, the discount would have closed long ago. Part of the discount is the market pricing a "one-man company."

Conclusion

Management verdict: Strategy 9, execution 7, capital allocation 3, governance 5, succession 4, weighted 6/10. Robin Li has positioned the company well but does not treat shareholders' capital as his own. The "Watch" rating is simple: wait for management to prove through action — dividends, buybacks — that it cares about the share price, rather than merely asserting at results briefings that "the payback cycle is shortening."
