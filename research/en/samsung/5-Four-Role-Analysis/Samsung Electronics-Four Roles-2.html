Financial analysis (Buffett's perspective) — Samsung Electronics

Can the three statements corroborate one another: is the profit real, where is the cash, is the balance sheet leveraged

One line: the profit is real, cash is being consumed by capex, and the balance sheet is clean

The three statements corroborate each other: profits are real, cash is being intercepted by capex, and the balance sheet is clean. On the profit side: revenue KRW 171.5 trillion, operating profit KRW 89.49 trillion, net profit KRW 71.62 trillion, net margin 41.8%, effective tax rate 20.0%, with no one-off gains propping up the numbers (FX gains KRW 3.1 trillion, 3.5% of operating profit).

On the cash side: net profit KRW 118.7 trillion (first half), but memory is asset-heavy, with depreciation, amortization and equipment investment swallowing a large chunk, leaving free cash flow far below net profit. On the asset side: net cash, no net interest-bearing debt, net assets of about KRW 996 trillion, PB 1.8x. This is a balance sheet that can weather a downturn, and one that will not surprise shareholders.

Income statement: profit is real, but the source is narrow

Start with the most basic check: confirm the profit is not an accounting construct. Revenue of KRW 171.5 trillion and gross profit of KRW 119.4 trillion divide to 69.6%, matching the gross margin the company discloses; operating profit of KRW 89.49 trillion less net profit of KRW 71.62 trillion leaves a gap of KRW 17.87 trillion, corresponding to a 20% effective tax rate—the three figures tie together, with no "other income" line propping up profit.

The only item to flag is a KRW 3.1 trillion FX gain, 3.5% of operating profit. The won was weak in the first half of 2026 (around KRW 1,500/USD), so export-priced products translate into more won.This is a denominator effect, not an operating improvement—if the won strengthens, the same USD revenue converts into fewer won and operating profit shrinks with it.

By segment, the DS division's KRW 89.2 trillion of operating profit accounts for 99.7%, while the remaining segments are negative in aggregate. Buffett would draw a line here: this is a single-profit-source company; diversification shows up in revenue, not in profit.

Cash flow: book profit on the income statement, real cash out the door

Storage industry cash flow has a quirk: net profit looks good, free cash flow looks bad. The reason is depreciation and capex. A DRAM line costs trillions of won, depreciated over five years, with the annual depreciation charge deducted straight from profit, but the equipment money is spent upfront. So when net profit is high, operating cash flow is usually higher still (depreciation added back), but free cash flow is largely eaten by capex.

First-half 2026 net profit was KRW 118.7 trillion. The money goes roughly three ways: equipment and line investment, R&D (Q2 alone KRW 16 trillion, +78% YoY), and dividends (at the policy floor of KRW 9.8 trillion/year). Dividends are about 4% of net profit, and everything else stays in the company to fund further capacity expansion.

Is that good or bad for shareholders? It depends on return on capital. If new capacity earns more than shareholders could earn investing the money themselves, retaining it is right; if the company is just buying equipment at high prices at the top of the cycle and prices have already fallen by the time capacity comes online, that destroys value. Samsung's track record is mostly the latter — every cycle top it has been the most aggressive spender on capex.

Balance sheet: net cash is what gives it the confidence to expand capacity counter-cyclically

Samsung has long been a net cash company with no net interest-bearing debt. Backing out from 1.8x PB and a market cap of KRW 1,793 trillion, net assets are about KRW 996 trillion, or roughly KRW 153,000 book value per share. This structure gives it two capabilities others lack: endurance and buying the dip.

Endurance: when the entire industry lost money in 2023, Samsung did not cut output and instead kept investing in R&D, because it had no debt to repay. Buying the dip: the 2016 acquisition of Harman (USD 8 billion) and the Taylor fab investment from 2024 onward were both moves made while others were retrenching.

Buffett would like this balance sheet, but he would press one question: why isn't the net cash returned to shareholders? A market cap of KRW 1,793 trillion against KRW 9.8 trillion of dividends implies a 0.55% yield. He would rather the company use the money for buybacks — buying back at a P/E of 7.5x is like paying 0.75 to buy 1 of earnings. This is a governance issue, not a financial one; we will expand on it in the management section.

Conclusion

The verdict from the three statements: profit is real but undiversified, cash is abundant but intercepted by capex, and the balance sheet is clean enough to bet against the cycle. This is a company that "won't die, but won't return profits to shareholders either."
Buffett-perspective score: financial quality 8/10, shareholder returns 3/10.
