Business analysis (Duan Yongping's perspective) — Samsung Electronics

Where the money in this business comes from, why it can keep collecting it, and whether it will still be here in ten years

One line: they sell standardized products and profit from shortages.

Samsung's business model in one sentence: produce the most standardized components at the largest capacity, and sell them at contract prices when supply is short. Of the KRW 171.5 trillion in 2026Q2 revenue, memory accounted for KRW 120.8 trillion, mobile KRW 33.2 trillion, display KRW 7.5 trillion and appliances KRW 14.5 trillion. Only memory made money — KRW 89.2 trillion in operating profit, while the other three divisions together lost KRW 0.1 trillion.

The upside of this business is that the larger the scale, the lower the cost, and customers cannot walk away; the downside is that it does not set its own prices — a money printer when supply is short, a money shredder when there is a glut. It does not generate steady cash flow; it generates cyclical cash flow.

Where the money comes from: one sector supports four sectors

Breaking down the 2026Q2 segment numbers, the structure is stark: memory revenue KRW 120.8 trillion, up 471% year on year, and DS division operating profit KRW 89.2 trillion, or 99.7% of the company total.The other three divisions (mobile, display, appliances) together posted revenue of KRW 55.2 trillion and operating profit near zero.

This is not new. It is Samsung's normal state: in good memory years it is a memory company that sells phones on the side; in bad memory years, mobile and appliances cushion the fall and keep the consolidated company from a loss. That is how 2023 played out—semiconductors lost money for four quarters, and MX and SDC held up the consolidated statements.

Would Duan Yongping like this structure? Half of it. He would like the resilience of "when the east is dark, the west is bright"; he would dislike that "the most profitable piece is the least stable." A truly good business should have every piece earning steadily, not one piece's windfall covering the mediocrity of the other three.

Why the fees keep coming: customers can't switch, capacity can't be bought

Memory customers are server makers, phone makers and PC makers. Their cost of switching suppliers is not in the purchase price but in the qualification cycle: a DRAM chip takes 6–9 months from sample delivery to mass production, and HBM takes more than 12 months, because it must undergo system-level validation together with the GPU. This time cost is Samsung's moat.

The capacity side is harder. An advanced DRAM line requires investment in the trillions of Korean won, a build cycle of 2–3 years, and equipment deliveries still have to queue. The industry-wide shortage in 2026 is essentially because no one dared expand capacity in 2023–2024 — the three major manufacturers were all losing money in those two years. The lag in supply is the fundamental reason prices could rise 44% quarter on quarter this cycle.

But this also explains why it cannot keep collecting forever: the capex committed this year will become capacity in 2027–2028. If the 2026 expansion plans of Samsung, SK hynix and Micron all materialize, supply will be in surplus again in 2028. This is the physical law of this business, not a failure of management.

Unit economics: HBM is a different business under the same roof

The same wafer, made into HBM versus ordinary DDR5, generates four to five times the revenue. The reason: HBM requires stacking multiple DRAM dies, connecting them with through-silicon vias (TSVs), then handing them to TSMC for advanced packaging—more process steps, lower yield, greater capacity consumption. HBM consumes more than three times the wafer area per GB that DDR5 does.

This leads to a counterintuitive conclusion: as HBM's share rises, Samsung experiences "margin expansion + shipment decline" simultaneously. In 2026Q2 memory revenue rose 471%, but bit shipments grew only modestly—that is this mechanism at work. The market is willing to assign HBM a high valuation because it is essentially paying a premium for the ability to "earn more money per wafer."

Samsung once lagged on this path: HBM3E certification came a year late, and its 2025 HBM share was only just over 20%. In 2026 it became the first to mass-produce HBM4, and its share returned to 33%. This history shows strong engineering capability but a slow commercialization cadence—the technology is ready, but customer certification requires queuing.

Will it still be here in ten years? Yes, but how much it earns is unknown

Will DRAM still be needed in ten years? Yes. The larger AI models grow, the higher the demand for memory bandwidth and capacity; on-device inference will put memory into phones and cars; and DRAM capacity per server generation in data centers keeps doubling. The demand trajectory is certain.

Will Samsung still be in the top three in ten years? Most likely. Memory is a capital-intensive industry; only three companies have survived three cycles, and new entrants face a triple barrier of patents, equipment and customer qualification. ChangXin Memory Technologies is the only variable, holding a 9.5% share now and mainly challenging consumer-grade DDR5.

How much money can be made in ten years? There is no answer to that question. Over the past two decades, Samsung's quarterly operating profit has swung between -KRW 4 trillion and +KRW 89 trillion, with a midpoint of roughly KRW 10 trillion. You can say "upward over the long term," but no single year's figure can be predicted. Duan Yongping's conclusion would be: this is a good business you can understand but cannot value, so buy only when it is extremely cheap.

Conclusion

Business essence: a cost-based moat built on scale plus certification barriers, paired with cyclical pricing. This is not a business that earns money while sitting still; it is one that must keep running, and run faster than rivals. Samsung runs at no slow pace, but not fast enough to escape the cycle.
Scored through Duan Yongping's lens: business quality 8/10, predictability 3/10 — together, "a good company that is hard to value."
