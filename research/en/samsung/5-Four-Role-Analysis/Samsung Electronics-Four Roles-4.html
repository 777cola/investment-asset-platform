Risk Assessment (Li Lu's perspective) — Samsung Electronics

What is the maximum downside, what would trigger it, and which risks are overstated?

One line: it can lose 40% without being wiped out, but it will cost you two years

We model downside risk in three tiers: mild downside (price falls 15%) → share price 200,000–220,000; moderate downside (falls 30%) → 150,000; extreme downside (falls 50% plus share loss) → 110,000. The three scenarios imply P/B of 1.3x, 0.98x and 0.72x, all within the historical range, so "insolvency" is not a possibility.

The real risk is not the size of the loss but its duration: downturns in cyclical stocks typically run 4–6 quarters, meaning all of 2027 could be spent in "falling profits plus share price grinding along the bottom". Every quarterly report in that stretch looks ugly, and the bottom is where it is easiest to sell. The point of risk assessment is not to scare people but to write this process down in advance.

Risk 1: Pricing inflects sooner than expected

Storage pricing follows a "slow rises, fast falls" pattern. Rises come from quarterly contract negotiations, 10%–20% at a time; falls begin with a spot-market collapse—spot prices can drop 10% in a week—and contract prices are then cut the following quarter.The 44% QoQ increase in 2026Q2 has never been sustained for more than two quarters in history.

Samsung's profit is extremely sensitive to price: every 10% fall in price cuts operating profit by roughly 25%, since costs are rigid and depreciation does not fall with it. If contract prices fall 30% in the first half of 2027, annualised operating profit drops from KRW 293.8 trillion to around KRW 150 trillion,which at 4x gives a share price of KRW 150,000—down 45% from the current price.

This is not a forecast but a stress test, and it makes one point:buying Samsung is not a bet on whether it will fall, but on whether you dare add when it does.

Risk 2: HBM share stalls at 33%

HBM is Samsung's only structural improvement this cycle. Share rose from 21% to 33%, driven by being first to mass-produce HBM4. But 12-layer validation for HBM4E has yet to pass, SK hynix is closing the gap on HBM4 yield, and Micron's HBM4 is already sampling.If Samsung's share stalls at 33% in 2027, it is merely a "second supplier" with no pricing power.

Second-supplier status means pricing follows the leader, capacity follows orders, and gross margin runs 5–8 percentage points below the leader. That is fine in a shortage, but fatal in a glut—in a glut, customers cut the second supplier's orders first.

The watch indicators are clear: Nvidia's next-generation platform HBM4E supplier list, HBM as a share of Samsung's memory revenue, and the price spread between HBM contract prices and conventional DRAM. Deterioration in any one of the three warrants a de-rating.

Risk 3: China's capacity and geopolitics

CXMT holds 9.5% of DRAM, concentrated in consumer DDR5. Its technology trails by two generations, but it has an advantage Samsung lacks: it does not need to make money; it needs to exist. So long as it keeps adding capacity, it will cap the price floor for commodity DRAM.

Geopolitical risk cuts both ways. US export controls on equipment to China will cap CXMT's leading-edge capacity — near-term support for Samsung — but they also accelerate substitution toward domestic Chinese equipment and materials, eroding Samsung's cost advantage over time. The more direct risk is memory being drawn into tariffs: more than half of Samsung's memory is produced at its Xi'an and Suzhou plants in China.

This one I cannot quantify, only characterise: it is a tail risk, not the base case. If it materialises, the hit is halved profit and a valuation de-rating at the same time — harder to recover from than a plain price cycle.

Overstated Risks: Foundry Drag and FX

Two risks the market frequently cites look overstated to us. The first is foundry losses: Samsung's foundry business is indeed burning cash, but losses narrow through 2026 as 2nm customers ramp, and the unit is small in the consolidated picture—within the DS division's KRW 127.5 trillion revenue, foundry accounts for only about KRW 6.7 trillion.A KRW 1 trillion foundry loss affects 1% of the division's KRW 89 trillion profit.

The second is FX: a weak KRW is a tailwind for Samsung given its export exposure, not a headwind. The real FX risk is sharp KRW appreciation, which would compress translated revenue—but typically by only 3%–5%, not enough to change the conclusion.

Strike these two from the "major risks" list and what remains are the real ones: price, share, geopolitics. None of the three is fully within management's control, which is why the stock merits only a 5% position, not 15%.

Conclusion

Risk conclusion: A 40% downside is quantifiable rather than catastrophic; the real cost is time — two years of bottoming out. Of the three real risks (price, share, geopolitics), only price can be observed in advance; the other two can be confirmed only after the fact.
Score from Li Lu's perspective: downside controllability 7/10, time cost 3/10. Recommendation: cap the position at 5%, scale in tranches, and hold cash to handle the KRW 150,000 scenario.
