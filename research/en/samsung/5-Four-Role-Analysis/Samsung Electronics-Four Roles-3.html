One line: The oligopoly is real; supply discipline is not

Memory is a classic three-player oligopoly: DRAM Samsung 39.4%, SK hynix 24.9%, Micron 23.3% — 87.6% combined, and with CXMT's 9.5%, industry concentration approaches 97%.High concentration should in theory produce pricing discipline; but historically the three have never held to it.

2026Q2 DRAM industry revenue rose 59.5% QoQ (TrendForce), the sixth up-quarter of this cycle. I use four indicators to gauge cycle position: contract price QoQ, channel inventory, capex growth, and HBM as a share of wafer capacity.The first two are already decelerating, the latter two still accelerating — this is a classic "top zone", not "the top".

Competitive structure: three oligopolists, but none can set the rules

DRAM is more concentrated than aero-engines: Samsung 39.4%, SK hynix 24.9%, Micron 23.3%. By the textbook, this structure should produce stable pricing discipline—the three take turns as price leader and no one starts a price war.

In reality, every cycle turns into a price war. The reason is product homogeneity: DDR5 is DDR5, and customers care only about price and lead time. What lets an oligopoly hold high prices is not tacit collusion but "no one dares expand first." Once one player judges demand will persist and moves first on capacity, the other two must follow or lose share. That is exactly what is happening in 2026—all three are raising capex in lockstep.

The sole exception is HBM. HBM must be validated alongside the GPU, so customer switching costs are high and share is relatively stable: SK hynix 50%, Samsung 33%, Micron 18%. HBM is the only part of this business with a "customer relationship" attribute, and the only part that can sustain a premium.

Demand side: AI is real, but the demand curve is not a straight line

An AI server carries 8–10x the DRAM capacity of a general-purpose server, and 6–8 HBM stacks per GPU. This is the physical driver of the current demand surge, not a narrative. In 2026, the four major North American cloud providers are raising capex in tandem, translating directly into orders for HBM and high-speed DRAM.

But the demand curve carries two risks. First, cloud capex depends on whether AI revenue materializes; if AI application revenue growth falls short of expectations in 2027, capex will be cut first—memory is the easiest item to cut in a capex budget, because orders can be deferred. Second, model efficiency is improving, and the HBM required for the same compute may decline (inference optimization, quantization, sparsification), eroding memory demand per unit of compute.

The Munger-style question is: if cloud capex grows only 10% in 2027 (rather than 40%), what happens to memory prices? The answer is that contract prices most likely flatten or turn down, because 2026 capacity is already sufficient to cover 10% demand growth. That is the source of the bear case.

Supply side: 2026 capex is the 2028 oversupply

Semiconductors follow one iron law: today's capex is capacity two years out. In 2023–2024 none of the three dared to invest, so 2026 will be short of supply; in 2026 all three are investing, so 2028 will be oversupplied. This time lag is the engine of the memory cycle, and it has never changed.

Samsung is expanding most aggressively: Pyeongtaek P4/P5, the Taylor fab's 2nm line, and dedicated HBM lines. Its logic is to "grab share with a cost advantage during a downturn," and it has executed this twice before (2016 and 2020), both times successfully. But the price of success is that current profit is suppressed by depreciation and shareholder returns are sacrificed.

China is the long-term variable. ChangXin Memory holds a 9.5% DRAM share, focused on consumer-grade DDR5; its process node trails by two generations, but its pricing is cheap. It poses no threat to Samsung's high-end products, but it does threaten the "price floor for commodity DRAM"—once consumer-grade prices break, Samsung's most profitable mainstream DRAM shipments will be the first to feel it.

Cycle position: top zone, not the top

Of the four indicators, the sequential contract price increase is narrowing (Q1 +60% or more → Q2 +44%), a deceleration signal; capex is accelerating, a future supply signal; HBM's share of wafer capacity is still rising, a structural support. Three point to "near the top," one points to "room left."

My judgment: H2 2026 to H1 2027 is a high-level plateau, and H2 2027 is the high-risk window for an inflection point. The reason is the timing of capacity release—equipment installed in 2026 begins ramping at the end of 2027. That is also why the valuation applies only 5x to 2027, not to 2028 earnings.

Munger would say: you don't need to know exactly where the top is, you only need to know this isn't the bottom. KRW 275,000 and a -24% drawdown mean "one leg down," not "the decline is over." That sentence determines position sizing, not whether to buy.

Conclusion

Industry conclusion: The oligopoly is real, pricing discipline is illusory, and the cycle is in the top zone. HBM is the only segment with customer stickiness, and the only source of Samsung's share improvement.
Munger-lens score: industry structure 7/10, cycle predictability 4/10. The combined recommendation is "holdable, not a heavy position."
