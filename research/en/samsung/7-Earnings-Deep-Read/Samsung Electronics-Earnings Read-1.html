Earnings Read — Samsung Electronics

Core call: growth is almost entirely price-driven, and the terminal business is being undercut by its own price hikes

2026Q2 revenue KRW 171.5 trillion, operating profit KRW 89.49 trillion and net profit KRW 71.62 trillion — all three at record highs, up 22.5%, 55.9% and 52.0% QoQ respectively. On the breakdown, however, bit shipments contributed little to revenue growth; pricing did the work: DRAM ASP +44% QoQ, NAND +53%.

The mix is more telling still: semiconductors generated 99.7% of operating profit, while mobile lost KRW 0.7 trillion — memory price inflation cuts both ways for Samsung itself. It captures the entire industry's memory profits on one side while absorbing memory costs in handsets on the other. That internal hedge nets out at the consolidated level, but it is an important clue to where the cycle stands.

Layer one: revenue mix — who is driving growth

Of KRW 171.5 trillion in revenue, DS accounted for KRW 127.5 trillion (74.3%), MX KRW 33.2 trillion (19.4%), CE KRW 14.5 trillion (8.5%) and SDC KRW 7.5 trillion (4.4%). DS's share is nearly 20 percentage points higher than in the same period of 2025 — a dramatic forward shift in the revenue mix.

The incremental gain was even more concentrated: revenue rose KRW 96.9 trillion YoY, of which memory alone contributed about KRW 99.6 trillion (120.8 versus 21.2 in the year-earlier period), already exceeding the total increase — meaning every business outside memory was a net drag. Handset revenue edged up YoY, but memory costs ate the profit; home appliances were roughly flat.

Samsung's growth is therefore not broad-based but single-division-led. The upside is extreme elasticity; the downside is weakened resilience: once memory turns, no business can absorb the KRW 89 trillion profit gap.

Layer two: gross profit and pricing — how 69.6% came about

Gross margin rose 35.4 percentage points in a year, from 34.2% to 69.6%. Only the 2017–2018 cycle is comparable in semiconductor history, and even that peaked at around 55%. At 69.6%, Samsung sits at an extreme among large global manufacturers.

The breakdown: price contributed about 30 percentage points, product mix (a higher HBM share) about 5 points, and cost reduction close to zero, as depreciation rises with new lines coming online. In other words, 85% of those 35 points came from price.

Margin given by price can be taken away by price. On historical elasticity, every 10% fall in DRAM contract prices cuts Samsung's gross margin by roughly 6–7 percentage points. A 30% price decline in 2027 would return gross margin to the 45%–50% range — the normal level for this business.

Tier 3: Costs and profit — R&D stepping up, tax burden normalising

Period expense ratio of 17.4pp (gross margin 69.6 less operating margin 52.2), down more than 10pp from 27.9pp in 2025Q2. Not because expenses fell, but because revenue grew too fast and diluted them. In absolute terms, Q2 R&D expense was KRW 16.0 trillion, +41% QoQ and +78% YoY.

R&D expense ratio of 9.3%, the highest among the three memory makers (SK hynix about 7%; Micron about 10% but on a small base). Samsung is funding three areas: 12-layer stacking for HBM4E, 1c/1d nm DRAM and 2nm foundry. This underpins its confidence in leading the next round, and explains why not all profit can be returned to shareholders.

Tax burden normal: operating profit of 89.49 less net profit of 71.62 gives 17.87 trillion, an effective tax rate of 20.0%, broadly in line with Korea's statutory rate (about 24.2% including local taxes, with R&D credits). No one-off tax gain; clean earnings quality. The only item to deduct is KRW 3.1 trillion in FX gains, 3.5% of operating profit.

Layer four: how money moves between segments — memory levies an invisible tax on handsets

One thing the consolidated statements do not show: memory price increases amount to an invisible tax the DS division levies on MX and CE. MX posted KRW 33.2 trillion in revenue and a KRW 0.7 trillion loss — driven not by weak phone sales but by rising memory costs, since DRAM and NAND are the most expensive single components in a handset.

A rough calculation: memory accounted for about 12% of bill-of-materials cost in a flagship phone in 2025, rising above 20% in 2026 after a 44% increase in DRAM prices. Samsung ships about 240 million phones a year, so the cost hit to MX from memory price increases is roughly KRW 5 trillion–8 trillion per year (rough estimate) — which precisely explains MX's swing from KRW 2 trillion in profit to a KRW 0.7 trillion loss.

At the consolidated level this money is an internal transfer and generates no additional profit, but it has two external implications. First, Samsung will not raise phone prices without limit — it has to defend share, so it can only absorb the cost itself, and MX's losses will persist until memory prices fall. Second, competitors (Apple, Xiaomi) have no memory capacity of their own, so the cost shock hits them more directly, and Samsung's relative competitiveness is actually rising.

The same applies to CE: panel and memory costs for TVs and home appliances are both rising, and KRW 14.5 trillion in revenue produced a small loss. Samsung's device businesses are playing the role of a buffer in this cycle, not a growth engine. Judging the turning point is simple: when MX returns to profit, it means memory cost pressure has passed through to devices — and that memory prices have peaked.

Layer 5: Cash flow and balance sheet — profit did not convert into cash

Storage industry cash flow follows a fixed pattern: high net profit, higher operating cash flow (depreciation added back), worst free cash flow (capex). Q2 2026 standalone net profit was KRW 71.62 trillion; depreciation and amortization, roughly estimated on the heavy-asset base, exceeds KRW 10 trillion, so operating cash flow most likely tops KRW 80 trillion. But equipment investment is also at a historical high over the same period, so free cash flow will come in well below net profit.

The balance sheet is clean: net cash, no net interest-bearing debt, equity of about KRW 996 trillion (backed out at 1.8x PB). The point of this balance sheet is not "having money" — it is "being able to withstand" — when the whole industry lost money in 2023, Samsung neither cut output nor cut staff, and this balance sheet is what carried it.

Shareholder returns are the weak spot. First-half profit was KRW 118.7 trillion; full-year dividends at the policy floor of KRW 9.8 trillion imply a payout ratio of about 4%. Not buying back stock at 7.5x PE amounts to leaving the cheapest money sitting on the books. This is the only uncomfortable part of the report.

Stack the four layers together and the picture in this report is clear: revenue is highly concentrated, gross margin is set by price, expenses are diluted by revenue, cash is eaten by equipment, and the balance sheet is clean but shareholders get nothing. Every one of these traits points to the same conclusion — the quality of earnings depends on how long prices hold, not on what the company did right.

Conclusion

Earnings takeaway: the profits are real, but the structure is extremely concentrated, capex is intercepting cash flow, and the balance sheet is clean. This is a textbook late-cycle print — margins, revenue growth and single-segment concentration all sit at historical extremes.
Read it not as "what is this worth" but as "how many quarters can this margin hold." The answer: two.
