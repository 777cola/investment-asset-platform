Buffett reads financial reports: buying a money printer at 1.8x P/B, except this machine needs constant parts replacement — Samsung Electronics

Three financial statements, return on capital, and "where the money went"

Balance sheet scores full marks, cash flow statement loses points, income statement fails (unsustainable)

Buffett reads the balance sheet first, then the cash flow statement, then the income statement: he wants to confirm the company will survive, then that the money it earns is real, and only then how much it earned. Samsung runs the sequence in reverse: an impressive income statement, a mediocre cash flow statement, an excellent balance sheet.

Net assets of about KRW 996 trillion, net cash, no net interest-bearing debt, P/B 1.8x, annualised ROE 24%—this is the structure he likes. What he does not like: earnings must be ploughed straight back into equipment or the next round is lost, and the return on that reinvestment is set by the price of the next cycle.

Income statement: headline numbers look good, but three questions come to mind first

Revenue 171.5 trillion, operating profit 89.49 trillion, net profit 71.62 trillion. First question: is the 69.6% gross margin sustainable? The same period in 2025 was 34.2% — a doubling in a year. Real operating improvement cannot move at that speed; only price can. Price is not sustainable, so neither is this gross margin.

Second question: are there one-off items in profit? Yes — 3.1 trillion in FX gains, 3.5% of the total, negligible. No asset disposals, no litigation settlements, no government subsidies — cleaner than many semiconductor companies on this point.

Third question: R&D of 16 trillion, +78% YoY — is it expense or investment? He treats it as investment: R&D targets the next technology node, and without it the company falls behind by 2028. This expense therefore cannot be cut, which means "normal-year margin" is far below current levels.

Cash flow statement: book profit is earned, but real cash is spent

This is the table Buffett cares about most, and the ugliest one for Samsung. Memory follows a rule: when net profit is high, capex is high too; in years of heavy capex, free cash flow is bound to look ugly.

Samsung's 2026 capex sits at a historic high (Pyeongtaek P4/P5, the Taylor fab, HBM-dedicated lines). That money is spread across the next five years through depreciation on the income statement, but in cash flow terms it is a current-period outlay. The result: profits on the financial statements look handsome, but the cash shareholders can get their hands on is scant.

His test is the "one-dollar principle": for every dollar of profit the company retains, can it create more than one dollar of market value? Samsung has retained a large amount of profit over the past decade, and its market-cap growth has come mainly from the cycle and market share, not from compounding of retained earnings. On this count, it fails.

Balance sheet: the one area where he would give full marks

Net cash, no net interest-bearing debt and net assets of about KRW 996 trillion. Those three conditions mean that even if prices collapse in 2027 and the company posts four consecutive quarters of losses, Samsung will not need financing, asset sales or layoffs.

The balance sheet also confers a capability others lack: countercyclical capacity expansion. When the entire industry cut output in 2023, Samsung kept investing in R&D and kept building fabs, precisely because it had no debt to repay. The result: its share in 2026 is higher than in 2023 — a textbook case of "the balance sheet is the strategy."

But Buffett would press further: if money is not the constraint, why not buy back stock? Buying back at 7.5x P/E means paying 0.75 yuan for 1 yuan of earnings — the most certain form of value creation. Management instead put the money into capacity, implying it believes expansion returns exceed buyback returns — a judgment that is usually wrong at the top of the cycle.

Conclusion

Buffett-style conclusion: a business that "won't die, but reinvests all its profits." A 1.8x P/B against a 24% ROE screens cheap, but the ROE denominator (net assets) keeps growing while the numerator (profit) is volatile, so the "cheap" case only holds at the top of the cycle.
His likely move: watchlist it and wait for P/B below 1.2x (around KRW 190,000) before considering a position.
