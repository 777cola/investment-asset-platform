Munger reads financial reports: first find the most abnormal line in the report — Samsung Electronics

Reverse thinking: which single number, once it rolls over, would collapse the entire earnings report?

The whole earnings report hinges on one number: DRAM contract prices.

Munger reads financial statements unusually: he ignores growth and hunts first for "the most abnormal line." In Samsung's report, that line is the 69.6% gross margin—a maker of standardized products posting a gross margin above TSMC's (67.7%), which in this industry can occur at only one point: the peak of a supply shortage.

The second anomaly is the semiconductor division contributing 99.7% of profit. For a conglomerate electronics company with annual revenue of KRW 171 trillion, almost all profit comes from a single division—so the stability of the entire financial report equals the stability of that division.

The first anomaly: a 69.6% gross margin

Three variables set a manufacturer's gross margin: price, cost and product mix. All three moved in the right direction at Samsung this quarter, but their contributions were wildly uneven — price accounted for 30 percentage points, mix 5, and cost close to zero.

Munger would ask: if prices returned to 2025 levels, what would the gross margin be? The answer is 34%, the figure of a year ago. That is arithmetic, not a forecast: strip out the price increases and what remains is the original business.

He would also flag a detail: the gross margin is higher than TSMC's. TSMC is the world's strongest foundry, with pricing power, technical barriers and customers queuing up; Samsung sells standard memory chips. There is only one explanation for a gross margin above TSMC's — this is not the normal state.

The second anomaly: 99.7% of profit comes from one segment

Samsung has three businesses, four divisions and 200,000 employees, yet 99.7% of its profit comes from semiconductors. The handset, display and consumer-electronics divisions combined posted a negative operating profit.

Munger's reading would be: this is not diversification, it is one bet plus three hedges. The hedges exist to keep the company alive when the bet fails (as proven in 2023), not to contribute profit at any time.

The implication for investors is direct: buying Samsung means buying DRAM prices; the other businesses only lower the probability of bankruptcy. If you do not want to bet on DRAM prices, you should not buy it; if you do, you should know what you are betting on. Many people buy Samsung because "it is a big company with diversified businesses" — that reasoning is wrong.

The third anomaly: cash generated is not returned to shareholders

First-half net profit of KRW 118.7 trillion; the full-year dividend sits at the policy floor of KRW 9.8 trillion, a payout ratio of 4%. That ratio is anomalous for a mature manufacturer—20%–30% would be typical.

So where did the money go? Three routes: capacity expansion, R&D and cash on the balance sheet. The first two are investment; the third is inefficiency. Samsung's cash pile keeps growing, and its failure to buy back stock at 7.5x P/E amounts to telling shareholders: "I can deploy this money more efficiently than you can if you take it back."

Munger's view of such claims: "If management's capital-allocation record over the past decade doesn't prove it, don't believe it." Over the past decade Samsung has added capacity at cycle peaks and endured the troughs; the record is one of "survived," not "generated excess returns."

Reverse inference: when will this earnings report turn?

Put the three anomalies together and the path to the reversal is clear: DRAM contract prices flatten sequentially → gross margin drops 6–7 percentage points → DS profit halves → consolidated operating profit falls back below KRW 40 trillion → the market reprices for a "downcycle."

This chain needs no "black swan," only prices to stop rising. And prices stopping rising is inevitable — the capex of the three players in 2026 becomes capacity in 2027–2028. In other words, this earnings reversal is not a risk; it is on the calendar.

Munger's question is always: if this is inevitable, does the price you are paying today discount it? KRW 275,000, a -24% drawdown, 7.5x P/E — it is discounted, but not enough. Truly "enough" is P/B below 1.2x, or P/E within 10x on normalized profit.

Conclusion

Munger-style conclusion: Only one number matters in this earnings report — the DRAM contract price; only one question matters — did you pay the right price for its turn. The answer is no.
Action: do not buy, or wait for the price to factor in the "inevitable reversal" (P/B 1.2x, about KRW 190,000).
