One line: the money comes from the shortage, not from skill

Duan Yongping asks only one question when reading financial statements: can this profit be repeated next year? Samsung's KRW 89.49 trillion operating profit in 2026Q2 breaks down as "price 89% + structure 11% + cost 0%". Prices may not hold next year, so the profit in this report is not repeatable.

But non-repeatable does not mean worthless. Samsung, at the peak of pricing, grabbed share from 21% to 33% and made HBM4 the world's first in mass production — these two are repeatable capabilities, they just do not show up in this quarter's profit. Reading financial statements requires separating these two layers.

First, where the money comes from: one segment contributes 99.7%

Of the KRW 171.5 trillion in revenue, semiconductors accounted for KRW 127.5 trillion; of the KRW 89.49 trillion in operating profit, semiconductors accounted for KRW 89.2 trillion. The other three divisions combined posted KRW 55.2 trillion in revenue and negative operating profit. This structure shows Samsung is no longer a "conglomerate electronics company" but "a memory company plus three unprofitable divisions."

Would Duan Yongping like this kind of "focus"? Quite the opposite. What he likes are companies where "each business can support itself," such as Apple — hardware, services and wearables each generate profit. Samsung's problem is not a lack of focus; it is too much focus on one extremely cyclical link in the chain, with the remaining divisions serving only as a buffer.

The value of that buffer showed in 2023: semiconductors lost money for four quarters that year, while MX and SDC held up the consolidated statements, and the company avoided an annual loss. That is the real role of "diversification" at Samsung — not growth, but survival.

Look again at the quality of the money: is it given by price hikes, or earned by capability?

Memory revenue rose 471% year on year, but bit shipments grew only modestly—almost all of the growth came from price. That distinction matters, because price is what the industry gives you; capability is what you earn. What the industry gives, it takes back; what you earn, you keep.

Samsung genuinely earned two things this quarter: HBM share rose from 21% to 33%, and it was first to ship HBM4 in volume. Both hold up even when prices fall—HBM gross margins run 15–20 percentage points above conventional DRAM, so mix improvement cushions a downcycle.

So Duan Yongping's verdict would be: "This is a good earnings report, but not one you can use to calculate value." A good report shows the company captured the cycle; valuing it requires "normal earnings in the next cycle," and that number cannot be produced right now.

Finally, the price: is KRW 275,000 worth it?

Using his "rough estimate" method: if the cycle returns to normal in 2028, Samsung's quarterly operating profit would be roughly KRW 25 trillion–35 trillion (this cycle's midpoint has been lifted by HBM), full-year KRW 100 trillion–140 trillion, net profit KRW 85 trillion–120 trillion, corresponding to EPS of KRW 13,000–18,400. Applying an 8–10x normal-case valuation gives a fair value of KRW 104,000–184,000.

The current price is KRW 275,000, 50% above the top of that range. This is why he says "I understand the business but can't put a price on it"—on normal earnings it is not cheap; on peak earnings it is very cheap. The two methods differ by a factor of two.

His approach in such situations is consistent: don't buy, or buy only a small tracking position. He won't build a heavy position just because "the PE is only 7.5x," because he knows the denominator is fake.

Conclusion

Duan Yongping-style conclusion: good company, good financials, bad timing. 85% of profit comes from price; the part that comes from capability (share, HBM4) is not yet at a level that can support the valuation.
If a move must be made: keep a tracking position at no more than 1%, and discuss building a position only when the price corresponds to normalized profit (below KRW 180,000).
