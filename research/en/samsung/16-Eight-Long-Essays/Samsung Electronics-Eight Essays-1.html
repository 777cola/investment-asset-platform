One line: it has long been a semiconductor company; handsets are merely its cash-flow buffer

Most people's impression of Samsung stops at phones—Galaxy, TVs, refrigerators. Samsung in its financial reports looks different: of the KRW 89.49 trillion operating profit in 2026Q2, 99.7% came from semiconductors; the phone, TV and appliance divisions combined were loss-making.

This perception gap is not a detail—it is the starting point for valuation. Value it as a "consumer electronics company" and you get 15–20x PE; value it as a "memory cycle company" and you get only 4–6x. The market gives it 7.5x, meaning it is already pricing it as a semiconductor company, with only a small "conglomerate electronics" discount left.

First perception gap: how it makes money

2026Q2 revenue KRW 171.5 trillion. Breakdown: semiconductors KRW 127.5 trillion (74.3%), handsets KRW 33.2 trillion (19.4%), home appliances KRW 14.5 trillion (8.5%), displays KRW 7.5 trillion (4.4%). Semiconductors account for three-quarters of revenue.

The gap in profit is more extreme: semiconductors KRW 89.2 trillion, handsets -KRW 0.7 trillion, home appliances a small loss, displays KRW 0.7 trillion. Semiconductors account for 99.7%. In other words, handsets, home appliances and displays combined contribute negatively to profit.

This is not a one-quarter anomaly. When semiconductors lost money in 2023, handsets and home appliances held up the consolidated statements; when semiconductors earned windfall profits in 2026, handsets and home appliances dragged. Together the two show: Samsung's device businesses were never a profit center, but a stabilizer.

Second perception gap: follower or leader

"Samsung is a fast follower" — that label dates from the 2000s: someone else invents a new technology, Samsung uses scale and cost to make the product the cheapest, then takes the share. That impression is only half right today.

The right half: Samsung is indeed not first on DRAM process nodes (1c nm); SK hynix is sometimes earlier; its foundry 2nm also lags TSMC. On "0 to 1," it is not the leader.

The wrong half: it was the world's first to ship HBM4 in volume — that is not following. The hard part of HBM is not "invention" but "stacking yield + joint customer qualification" — engineering capability, not scientific discovery. Samsung's strength is precisely engineering: taking something from the lab to million-unit mass production.

So the accurate statement is: it is not the inventor but "the last one to get the yield up," and in manufacturing, the last one to get there often takes the largest share.

Third perception gap: the governance discount on Korean companies

Foreign investors have long valued Korean chaebols below Taiwanese and US peers, and the reason is governance: cross-shareholdings, related-party transactions, inheritance tax, strikes. Samsung trades at a PE of just 7.5x versus TSMC's 27.9x — the gap is not just the cycle.

How much of this discount is justified? In the first half of 2026 it earned KRW 118.7 trillion, paid KRW 9.8 trillion in dividends, a payout ratio of 4%. If it raised the payout ratio to 30%, the dividend yield would go from 0.55% to 4%, and the valuation would re-rate by at least 30%. It has not done so, so the discount is justified.

But the discount also obscures something: Samsung's capital allocation is restrained by chaebol standards — a decade without large M&A, without reckless investment, without high-leverage expansion. Its money has gone mainly into capacity and R&D, both of which are effective over the long term, except the returns do not reach shareholders.

So the governance discount is "structural" and will not disappear because of one quarter's profit. Buy it and you accept the discount; do not count on it closing.

After the reset, three questions remain

Once the old impression is cleared, valuing Samsung requires answering only three questions. First, how much longer can DRAM prices rise? Second, can HBM share go from 33% to 40%? Third, does 7.5x PE discount the answers to the first two questions?

The answer to the first question lies on the supply side (the pace of capacity expansion by the three players), the second on the customer side (NVIDIA certification), and the third in the price (the gap between PE and PB). All three questions can be verified with public data and can all be falsified—that is what makes a company worth researching.

If the answer to all three questions is "uncertain," the right move is not "don't buy" but "buy a small position, buy in tranches, and hard-code the falsification conditions." That is what the next seven pieces will lay out.

Conclusion

The conclusion of the cognitive reset: Samsung is a memory company disguised as a consumer electronics company, its margins set by DRAM contract prices and its valuation set by where it sits in the cycle. Phones and home appliances are stabilizers, not profit centers; the governance discount is structural and will not disappear.
What remains is to answer three questions: how long prices can keep rising, how much share it can win, and how steep a discount the price carries.
