One line: Buy, but put "when not to buy" first

The previous seven pieces covered "what this company is." This piece covers only "what to do." Three questions: is the business good (yes), is the price expensive (not expensive but not cheap), how large a position (5% cap).

Combined, the answers point to "buy (in tranches)": open a 1.5% initial position at the current price of KRW 275,000, add a second tranche below 240,000, and fill out to 2% once Q3 guidance is ≥ KRW 95 trillion and HBM4E certification is passed. If 2027 DRAM contract prices fall for two consecutive months, cut the position by half, regardless of profit or loss.

Question 1: Is it a good business?

Good. 2026Q2 gross margin 69.6%, operating margin 52.2%, DRAM share 39.4% ranking first globally, HBM share 33% and rising, first to mass-produce HBM4. All four pieces of evidence point to one conclusion: this is an extremely profitable business at the top of the cycle.

But it is not a business you can "buy and hold." 2023Q2 semiconductors lost 4.36 trillion, 2026Q2 earned 89.2 trillion — same fab. So the definition of "good" needs a qualifier: good business, strong cycle.

That qualifier dictates every subsequent move: no growth-stock position sizing, no DCF, no assumption that margins hold.

Question 2: Is the price expensive?

Two numbers conflict: 7.5x PE (cheap), 1.8x PB (not cheap). Which is right? Both — they just use different denominators.

PE's denominator is peak-cycle profit, which can shrink at any time; PB's denominator is net assets, which are relatively stable. For cyclicals, look at PB at the top and PE only at the bottom. So PB is the one to trust now: 1.8x sits above the historical median (range 0.8–2.6x) — neither cheap nor expensive.

Cross-check with three scenarios: bear case 110,000 (-60%), base case 268,000 (-3%), bull case 399,000 (+45%). The probability-weighted expected value roughly equals the current price — the market is pricing this efficiently. The reason to buy is not "clearly undervalued" but "net-asset downside protection, share-gain upside optionality."

Question 3: How large should the position be?

Cap 5%, initial position 1.5%. The rationale: the worst case (-60%) would damage the portfolio by -3%, which is bearable; and its correlation with other semiconductor holdings is above 0.85, so it is not a diversification tool.

The three-tranche build is not designed to "average down the cost" but to keep the cost of "being wrong" within a bearable range: if it falls right after purchase, the loss is only 1.5% × 60% = 0.9% of the portfolio on the initial position; if it rises, the 1.5% still captures part of the upside.

Conversely, if the initial position were 5%, a wrong call would cost the portfolio -3%, with no dry powder left to add at lower levels. The essence of position sizing is not "how much you make when you are right" but "how much you have left when you are wrong."

Five disciplines: write it down, no further judgement needed

Rule one: No single name above 5%; memory and AI chips combined no more than 15%. Prevent overconcentration in a single bet.

Rule two: If DRAM contract prices fall month-on-month for two consecutive months, cut the position by half. A logic-based stop takes priority over all other rules.

Rule three: If HBM4E certification fails, do not add a third tranche. Do not add and do not sell — the 33% second-supplier stake still makes money.

Rule four: Above 330,000, stop adding and trim by one-third. Do not chase the last leg when the odds have vanished.

Rule five: Each quarter, watch only four numbers: DRAM contract prices, HBM share, DS operating margin, capex guidance. Ignore all other news, including strikes, lawsuits and the chairman's travel schedule.

What the five rules share: none requires judgment, only execution. That is why they work when sentiment is at its worst.

If you can only remember one thing

Remember this: "Buying Samsung means buying the DRAM price cycle plus the HBM share catch-up; the price cycle will be given back, the share will stay."

So the reason to buy should be share (33%→40%), not price (KRW 89 trillion). If one day you cannot say what the share is now, it is time to sell.

This is also the one judgment that has not changed from start to finish in this report: the company is fine, the price is the problem, and position sizing solves the price problem.

Conclusion

Decision framework conclusion: The answers to the three questions are "good business, not cheap, 5% cap." The action is to build the position in tranches plus five disciplines.
Final line: The upside of this investment is set by HBM share, the downside by net assets, and the volatility in between by DRAM prices—none of the three requires forecasting, only tracking.
