Financial reality: of the RMB 8.9 trillion in profits, how much will still be there next year? — Samsung Electronics

One line: 85% price, 15% mix, 0% cost

Decompose the 2026Q2 gross margin expansion (34.2%→69.6%, +35.4pp) into three drivers: price accounts for roughly 30pp, product mix (HBM share) about 5pp, and cost improvement close to 0, as depreciation is rising.

This decomposition sets the denominator for the valuation. Only those 5pp will still be there next year — equivalent to quarterly operating profit of about 15 trillion–20 trillion, not 89 trillion. On 89 trillion the stock trades at a P/E of 7.5x; on "the profit that will still be there next year" it trades at more than 30x.

Layer one: the source of profit determines its lifespan

Price-driven profit lasts only as long as the price-up cycle. DRAM average prices rose 44% QoQ in 2026Q2, a pace never sustained for two consecutive quarters in history. Across the past three cycles, profit has peaked 2–3 quarters after the price gain peaked.

Mix-driven profit lasts as long as the product generation. The HBM4 premium holds until HBM4E enters mass production (roughly 12–18 months), after which the new generation resets pricing. Mix improvement, however, is cumulative — HBM share rises with each generation, lifting the floor under this profit.

Cost-driven profit has the longest life but is the hardest to achieve. Samsung's unit cost falls 5%–8% a year on process advances, but depreciation rises more than 10% a year as new lines come online; the two offset, leaving a net contribution near 0. The "cost improvement" lever has thus contributed essentially no profit over the past five years.

Layer two: cash flow is more honest than profit

Depreciation policy can flatter the income statement; cash flow cannot. Samsung's operating cash flow (net profit plus depreciation and amortisation) will most likely exceed KRW 80 trillion in 2026Q2, above net profit of KRW 71.62 trillion, simply because depreciation is added back.

Capex, however, is also at a historic high, with Pyeongtaek P4/P5, the Taylor fab's 2nm line and the dedicated HBM lines all under construction simultaneously. Free cash flow = operating cash flow − capex, and in 2026 it will most likely be positive but thin.

The implication is clear: Samsung's profit is not converting into shareholders' money; it is converting into fabs and equipment. Those assets return to the income statement through depreciation over the next five years, but by then prices may already have fallen. This is the most fundamental value trap in the memory industry: the money earned must be spent immediately, and the money spent does not necessarily earn itself back.

Layer three: the three ROE measures — don't use the wrong one

The working paper cites "ROE near 40% at the cycle peak"; I calculate 24%. The gap is the denominator: 40% uses average equity for 2023–2025, depressed by losses, while 24% uses equity at the end of 2026Q2 (about 996 trillion).

Which is right? To judge "how much this business can earn when times are good," use 40%; to judge "how much return the price I pay today corresponds to," use 24%. The purchase price corresponds to current equity, so valuation uses 24%.

More important: a 24% ROE does not look impressive at a cycle peak (consumer staples leaders run above 30% year-round), but at a cycle trough it turns negative. Samsung's ROE in 2023 was around 4%. Valuing a cyclical stock on ROE is like averaging the highest and lowest scores — meaningless.

Fourth layer: three accounting details worth noting

First, FX gains of KRW 3.1 trillion, equal to 3.5% of operating profit. The won was weak in the first half of 2026 (KRW 1,500/USD), so export-denominated revenue translated into more won. This is not an operating improvement and will disappear once the won strengthens.

Second, R&D expense of KRW 16 trillion, up 78% year on year, fully expensed (no capitalization). This is conservative accounting and shows profit is not inflated; it also shows that if it wanted to dress up the statements, capitalizing part of R&D would add trillions of won of profit. It did not do so, and that deserves credit.

Third, inventory. In a memory price upcycle, inventory is the most valuable asset — wafers bought cheap and chips produced cheap, sold at current prices, so part of gross profit is inventory appreciation. That portion of profit reverses into inventory write-downs when prices turn down. The quarterly report does not disclose inventory turnover days, which is an information gap.

Conclusion

Financial reality: of the RMB 89.49 trillion, roughly RMB 55–60 trillion came from price, RMB 15–20 trillion from structure and nothing from cost. Only that RMB 15–20 trillion will still be there next year, which implies a P/E of over 30x.
The right way to read this earnings report is not to work out how much it is worth, but to judge when the price-driven component disappears. The answer is within two quarters.
