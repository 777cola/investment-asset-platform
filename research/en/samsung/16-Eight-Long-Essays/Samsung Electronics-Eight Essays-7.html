One line: the first two risks can genuinely impair capital; the last three are chronic ailments

Rank risks by "probability × impact"; only two warrant a position change: DRAM prices turning down (high probability, high impact) and HBM4E certification failure (medium probability, medium impact). The other three—China capacity, geopolitics and governance—are chronic conditions that drive the valuation discount rather than the probability of bankruptcy.

The point of this list is not to read it and panic, but to write the trigger conditions in advance. When risk actually hurts, it is not because it went unforeseen, but because it was foreseen and no rule was written.

Risk 1: Price downturn — highest probability, greatest downside

The memory price cycle is not a question of "whether" but "when." With all three players expanding capacity in sync in 2026 and equipment ramping from late 2027, oversupply in 2028 is a high-probability event, and H2 2027 is the high-risk window for a price reversal.

How much damage? On elasticity estimates, a 30% price decline cuts operating profit by more than 50%, since costs are rigid and depreciation does not fall. The annualized KRW 293.8 trillion operating profit of 2026Q2 would drop to around KRW 130 trillion.

The good news is that it is observable: TrendForce publishes contract prices monthly, and two consecutive months of month-on-month declines confirm the signal. The response to this risk is not "anticipation" but "following" — exit when the signal appears; do not guess the top.

Risk 2: HBM4E qualification, a one-time switch

HBM4E's 12-layer stack must clear Nvidia certification by December. Pass, and share can reach 40% in 2027; fail, and share stalls at 33%, leaving the company as a second supplier only. As a second supplier, pricing follows the leader and you are first in line for order cuts when supply exceeds demand.

Impact: the 7pp share gap equates to roughly KRW 10 trillion in annual profit (rough calculation) and KRW 130,000 in valuation (base case 268,000 vs bull case 399,000). A 30% share-price variable, all riding on a single certification outcome.

Observability is high — the certification result is an announced event, no guesswork required. The response is equally simple: on a fail, do not add a third tranche; hold what has already been bought, since a 33% second-supplier position is still profitable.

Risk 3: CXMT, the slow squeeze

CXMT now holds 9.5% share. It trails by two technology generations but prices 20%–30% cheaper. Its expansion is not judged on returns, only on whether capacity exists — the biggest difference between CXMT and Samsung, SK hynix and Micron.

Transmission path: consumer-grade DDR5 prices collapse first → Samsung's mainstream DRAM gross margin falls from 60% to 30% → high-end (HBM, server DRAM) is unaffected, but total volume shrinks. This risk will not push Samsung into losses, but it will lower its bottom-line profit.

Watch consumer-grade DDR5 spot prices and CXMT's monthly capacity. If CXMT's capacity doubles by 2027, the risk shifts from chronic to acute.

Geopolitical and governance risk: a discount, not a bankruptcy

The geopolitical risk here takes two specific forms: tariffs and export controls. More than half of Samsung's memory output comes from its plants in Xi'an and Suzhou. If memory is caught up in tariffs, the impact is "halved profit plus a valuation discount" arriving at the same time. The probability is low, but if it happens, the recovery period is long.

Governance risk is a slow variable: a 4% payout ratio, circular shareholding, the succession issue. It won't push the company into losses, but it will keep its valuation 30%–50% below TSMC's over the long term. That risk is already in the price, so no additional discount is warranted, but nor can one count on it being repaired.

Taken together, the two mean: Samsung's valuation ceiling is locked by governance, and its floor is opened up by geopolitics. This is the biggest difference between it and TSMC—TSMC's risk lies in demand, Samsung's in institutions and geography.

Overestimated Risks: Competition and Technological Lag

The two risks the market cites most often are, in my view, overstated.First, "SK hynix leads on technology and Samsung will never catch up": the 2026 fact is that Samsung was first to mass-produce HBM4 and gained 12 points of share in a single quarter — evidence it can catch up.

Second, "Chinese vendors will upend memory": CXMT trails by two generations and near term can compete only on price at the low end, not on technology at the high end. Genuine disruption requires breakthroughs across equipment, materials and EDA — a ten-year-plus undertaking.

Overstated risks drive wrong actions: selling at lows for fear of competition, never touching semiconductors for fear of China.The value of a risk list lies in ranking, not completeness — treating secondary risks as primary ones is tantamount to no risk management at all.

Conclusion

Risk conclusion: Two risks warrant position changes (price reversal, HBM4E qualification); three need only be accepted (China, geopolitics, governance). Competition and technological lag are overstated.
Execution rules: halve the position if contract prices fall for two consecutive months; do not add on qualification failure; do not pre-position for geopolitical risk — cap it with a 5% position limit.
