Industry and competition: three oligopolists, one China variable, one declining curve — Samsung Electronics

One line: price is set by supply and demand, not by the oligopoly; the share grabber is China, not SK.

DRAM is an oligopoly: Samsung 39.4%, SK hynix 24.9%, Micron 23.3% and CXMT 9.5%, a combined concentration of nearly 97%. But oligopoly does not equal discipline—every historical cycle has devolved into a price war, because the product is commoditised.

Two variables truly matter: HBM, the only segment with customer stickiness (Samsung 33%, SK 50%, Micron 18%), and Chinese capacity, namely CXMT's DDR5, which is attacking the price floor. The former determines how much premium Samsung can earn; the latter determines whether Samsung's commodity DRAM turns into a loss-making business.

DRAM: highest concentration, weakest discipline

Together the three hold 87.6% — a concentration exceeding that of the global aero-engine market (GE, RR, PW). Industrial-economics textbooks say such a structure should produce "tacit pricing." But memory's history is one of profit in every upcycle and losses in every downcycle; it has never been stable.

The reason is product homogeneity. DDR5 is DDR5; customers care only about price, lead time and yield. The oligopolists sustain high prices not through tacit agreement but because "no one dares to expand capacity first." Once one player judges demand to be durable and moves first on capacity, the other two must follow or cede share — and that is exactly what is happening in 2026.

The DRAM price cycle therefore reflects "oligopolistic gaming" rather than "oligopolistic manipulation." The three players' capex decisions are independent and each second-guesses the others, and together they form the cycle. That also means no one can eliminate the cycle through "coordination," Samsung included.

HBM: the only battleground with customer stickiness

HBM competes on entirely different logic from DRAM. Customers (NVIDIA, AMD, Broadcom) must lock in HBM specifications, stack layer counts and packaging methods at the GPU design stage, and suppliers are engaged from the design phase and undergo more than 12 months of validation. Switching suppliers amounts to redesigning an entire chip, at extremely high cost.

This produces two outcomes. First, stable share: SK hynix 50%, Samsung 33%, Micron 18% — a three-player structure unchanged for two years. Second, a stable premium: HBM gross margins run 15–20 percentage points above ordinary DRAM and erode more slowly when prices fall.

Samsung's 12-percentage-point catch-up in 2026 rests on being first to mass-produce HBM4 plus parallel validation with customers. But after reaching 33% it will hit a ceiling: SK hynix's ties to NVIDIA run too deep, and its position as primary supplier will not change near term. 40% is Samsung's cap this cycle; 50% would require SK hynix to stumble.

China variable: fighting the price floor, not the technology ceiling

CXMT holds 9.5% of DRAM share, two generations behind (equivalent to Samsung's 1y/1z nodes), but prices 20%–30% cheaper. Its target customers sit in the consumer market—phones, PCs, white-box servers, not AI training.

The threat to Samsung is at the low end, not the high end. Standard DDR5, LPDDR and consumer products account for the bulk of Samsung's DRAM shipments. Once CXMT's DDR5 ramps in volume, consumer DRAM prices break first—and of the three, Samsung carries the largest shipment volume in this segment.

Timeline: CXMT's 2026 capacity is about 300,000 wafers per month (industry estimate) and could double in 2027. A doubling would lift global DRAM supply 5%–8%, enough to drive consumer prices down 20%. This is one of the core assumptions behind the 2027 bear case, and the risk the market is most reluctant to price.

Cycle position: three are decelerating, one still holds up

Three decelerating factors: DRAM contract prices slowed from above +60% QoQ to +44%; NAND from above +100% to +53%; and capex at Samsung, SK and Micron was raised in tandem, implying more supply in 2027–2028.

One supporting factor: HBM's share of DRAM wafer capacity has risen from under 5% to above 20% and is still climbing. That squeezes supply of conventional DRAM and prolongs the shortage longer than expected. This is the biggest difference between this cycle and 2018.

Conclusion: H2 2026 to H1 2027 is a high-level plateau; H2 2027 is the high-risk window for an inflection point. The plateau is characterised by "earnings still rising, share prices flat", as the market prices in the inflection point early — which is where we are now.

The endgame of the competitive landscape: three models, each surviving on its own

Samsung is the all-round player, SK hynix the HBM specialist and Micron pure memory plus long-term contracts. The three models behave completely differently through upcycles and downcycles: SK hynix has the most upside in upcycles, Samsung holds up best in downcycles, and Micron sits between the two.

The question is not "who is better" but "whom to buy at which point in the cycle." Late-cycle territory, where we are now, favours the defensive name, so choose Samsung; at a cycle bottom, buy SK hynix for maximum upside. The stock-picking conclusion depends on the cycle call, and that must be stated honestly.

Long term, all three survive—memory is a strategic industry, and no country will let it consolidate to a single player. Share oscillates between 35%–42% (Samsung), 22%–28% (SK hynix) and 18%–24% (Micron), plus 10%–15% for Chinese manufacturers. This is a stable oligopoly, not an industry that will be disrupted.

Conclusion

Industry conclusion: DRAM is an oligopoly but lacks discipline; HBM is sticky but share is frozen; the China variable sets the price floor. The cycle sits in the top zone, with three indicators decelerating and one holding up.
The endgame is three players coexisting long term with only minor share swings, so returns come from where in the cycle you buy, not which is the better company to buy.
