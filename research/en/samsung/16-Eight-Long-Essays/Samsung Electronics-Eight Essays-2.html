The nature of the business: three charging models, three fates for one business — Samsung Electronics

One line: in the same fab, making HBM and making DDR5 are two different industries

Samsung's semiconductor business has three revenue lines: memory sells dies at quarterly contract prices, HBM sells stacks under long-term agreements, and foundry sells capacity by wafer. The pricing mechanisms differ completely: contract prices track supply and demand, long-term agreements lock in volume and price, and foundry depends on process node and customer trust.

In the same fab, making HBM rather than DDR5 changes the economics four to five times over — HBM consumes more than three times the wafer area per GB that DDR5 does, at four to five times the unit price. This is why "HBM mix" explains Samsung's gross margin swings better than "shipments."

Line one: contract prices, the business itself

Commodity DRAM and NAND contract prices are negotiated quarterly. Large customers — server and handset makers — sign quarterly or semi-annual framework agreements, with prices tracking the market. The essence of this line is "no pricing power, only supply and demand": the three oligopolists can influence supply but cannot set prices unilaterally, because customers can switch suppliers at any time.

2026Q2 readings for this line: DRAM average price +44% QoQ, NAND +53%, with bit shipments up slightly. In other words, volumes barely grew, prices rose 40% and profit rose 19x.

Cash flow on this line is excellent: prepayment or short payment terms, fast inventory turnover, and capex for capacity expansion can be funded from operating cash flow. The drawback is volatility — a 30% price decline would take this line's gross margin from 60% to below 30%.

Second track: HBM, the only product with customer relationships

HBM is not a commodity. It requires custom stacking to match each customer's GPU architecture, design engagement from the outset and a qualification cycle of more than 12 months. Switching suppliers is therefore extremely costly — a matter not of money but of time.

Pricing works entirely differently: HBM is mostly sold under annual long-term contracts with price floors and take-or-pay clauses (Micron has disclosed 16 such contracts, 14 of which correspond to roughly USD 100 billion). The purpose of these contracts is not to lock in high prices but to lock in volume — securing supply when chips are scarce and sell-through when they are in surplus.

On economics, each GB of HBM consumes more than three times the wafer area of DDR5, commands a unit price four to five times higher and earns gross margins 15–20 percentage points higher. Every 5 percentage point increase in the HBM mix therefore lifts blended gross margin for the memory business by roughly 1 percentage point. This is the second reason Samsung's gross margin can reach 69.6% in 2026 (the first being price increases).

Third front: foundry, a costly option

Foundry sells capacity by the wafer to design companies (Nvidia, Qualcomm, AMD, Tesla). Process node and yield are everything here: if 2nm yield does not improve, customers will not come; if customers do not come, yield will not improve. A classic chicken-and-egg problem.

Samsung Foundry holds about 10% share, TSMC about 67% and SMIC about 6%. Foundry accounts for only about KRW 6.7 trillion of the DS division's KRW 127.5 trillion in revenue, or 5%. The business is now near breakeven, propped up by group subsidies and premium advanced-node projects.

Why stay in it? Because it is the only non-memory option. If the memory cycle turns down, foundry provides a second leg; if 2nm works in 2027, it can win transferred AI chip orders when TSMC capacity falls short. This is an option that should be priced at zero now — neither a plus nor a minus.

Depreciation is the largest single cost item and amplifies the cycle

Depreciation and equipment amortisation are the largest single line in the memory cost structure (typically 30-40%), followed by materials (wafers, photoresist, specialty gases); labour is a very small share given the high level of automation. This structure means capacity utilisation amplifies gross margin volatility more than price does.

At full capacity, depreciation per chip is at its lowest; at 70% utilisation, unit cost immediately rises by more than 40%. Memory makers in a glut therefore cut prices rather than cut output, because shutting down lines raises unit cost even more. That is why price wars are always so brutal.

Samsung's advantage is the largest single-fab scale, giving it the strongest depreciation dilution. For the same 30% price decline, its gross margin falls 5-8 percentage points less than peers'. This is the quantitative expression of its cost moat, and the reason its 2023 losses were smaller than competitors'.

Cash conversion: why this business looks profitable but is short of cash

Storage has a distinctive cash cycle: revenue comes in cash or on short payment terms, while spending goes out as equipment prepayments. Equipment vendors require 30%–50% on signing and full payment on delivery, yet a fab takes 2–3 years to reach full capacity. Free cash flow in expansion years is therefore bound to be negative or razor-thin.

Samsung's net profit in the first half of 2026 was KRW 118.7 trillion, with capex at a historic high over the same period. Operating cash flow (net profit plus depreciation) covers capex without issue, but little is left over. That is why it earns KRW 89 trillion while distributing only KRW 9.8 trillion — the money stays in the business to buy equipment.

The implication for investors: do not value Samsung on discounted free cash flow, because its free cash flow swings violently through the cycle and is consumed by capex over the long run. P/B, normalized earnings and replacement cost are all more reliable than DCF.

Conclusion

Business model conclusion: three revenue lines — one driven by supply and demand (commodity memory), one by customer relationships (HBM), one by process node (foundry). In 2026, profit will come almost entirely from pricing on the first line; the second offers structural improvement; the third remains an option.
Valuation must use a cyclical-stock approach, not a DCF — the free cash flow from this business has never belonged to shareholders.
