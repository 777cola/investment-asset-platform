One line: first-class execution, zero checks and balances

Samsung's management has two tiers: Chairman Lee Jae-yong sets strategy and capital expenditure, while professional managers of the three major divisions handle execution. This structure is highly efficient when the "direction is right" (the 2026 HBM catch-up, progress on the Taylor plant), but corrects course very slowly when the "direction is wrong" (HBM3E fell a year behind in 2024–2025).

Capital allocation cuts both ways: on one side, the KRW 10 trillion buyback in November 2024 and annual dividends of no less than KRW 9.8 trillion; on the other, earnings of KRW 118.7 trillion in the first half of 2026 with a payout ratio of just 4%. This is not stinginess but a strategic choice to "reinvest all profits" — whether it is right depends on returns in the next cycle.

Lee Jae-yong: three decisions that defined the decade

The first decision was the 2016 acquisition of Harman (USD 8 billion). The logic was that "auto electronics is the next smartphone," but a decade later Harman remains a standalone business and has not become the group's second growth curve. The acquisition was not a failure (Harman is profitable in its own right), but it did not deliver on the strategic intent.

The second decision was the 2021 US trip that finalized the Taylor fab. At the time no one favored building fabs in the US (high costs, talent shortage); Samsung's bet was that "geopolitics would pull advanced process back to the US." In 2026, localization demand for AI chips validated that judgment.

The third decision was replacing the head of the DS division in 2024 and elevating HBM to a company-wide project. This was a correction and an admission of the misjudgment of the prior two years. 18 months after the change, share rose from just over 20% to 33%.

Of the three decisions, two were right and one neutral. That record is upper-middle among large tech companies, but the cost is that "decisions depend on one person"—when the chairman judges right he is unbeatable; when he judges wrong, no one can stop him.

Jeon Young-hyun: The man who shortened the process

He took over as head of the DS division in May 2024, previously head of the Future Technology Research Institute and a storage business veteran. The three things he did were all about "process," not "technology": HBM elevated to a company-wide project (resource priority), validation process run in parallel with customers (compressed from 12 months to 8 months), capacity expanded before discussing price (trading capacity for trust).

These three moves don't sound sexy, but they are the direct cause of HBM share rising from 21% to 33%. Samsung's technology was always there; what was slow was the chain "from lab to customer."

The cost is front-loaded capex. HBM-dedicated lines see concentrated investment in 2025–2026, with depreciation pressing on 2027–2028. If prices turn down then, this round of capacity expansion becomes a burden—the one risk in his decisions that is not hedged.

Capital allocation: why RMB 118 trillion in profit distributes only RMB 9.8 trillion

This is the question investors ask most often. There are three layers to the answer.

The first layer is policy: the shareholder return policy for 2024-2026 is "50% of free cash flow returned, annual dividend no less than 9.8 trillion." The policy says "free cash flow," not "net profit." Capex in 2026 sits at a historic high, so free cash flow is far below net profit, and the dividend does not need to rise with it.

The second layer is strategy: memory competition is capacity competition. Without expansion in 2026, there is no share in 2028. Management believes "returns on capacity investment exceed buybacks" — that judgment holds in an upcycle and is usually wrong at the top.

The third layer is governance: chaebol cash flow stays inside the group, available for M&A, support for affiliates and inheritance tax. Minority shareholders have no mechanism to force change and can only wait for the new policy in 2027.

Integrity and governance: three incidents, three boundaries

2017 bribery case: Lee Jae-yong sentenced to five years, reduced to a suspended sentence on appeal, paroled in 2021, pardoned in 2022. Boundary one: the government-business relationship is a historical burden; it does not affect products or financials.

2015 merger case: prosecutors alleged the merger price harmed minority shareholders; the Seoul High Court acquitted in February 2025 on appeal. Boundary two: the institutional problems of chaebol succession remain unresolved; control is maintained through circular shareholding.

2024 union: the first large-scale strike since the company's founding; an agreement was reached in 2025. Boundary three: labor costs will rise rigidly, but labor relations now have an institutional outlet.

What the three events share: they do not affect competitiveness, they affect valuation. Foreign investors have long assigned Samsung a lower PE than TSMC, and governance is one reason. This discount will not disappear because of a single quarter's profit.

Succession: not a problem, but no answer either

Lee Jae-yong was born in 1968 and is 58 in 2026. No succession plan has been made public, and his children have not entered management. Near term this is not a problem — Korean chaebol chairmen routinely serve past 70.

Over the medium term it is a structural risk: once the handover comes, the mergers, restructurings and tax disputes of 2015 will recur. For secondary-market investors, this means the governance discount reappears in another form; it does not disappear.

The saving grace is a complete professional-manager system: all three division heads are veterans developed internally for more than a decade. This system ensures that "the chairman changes and the company keeps running," and also that "no one challenges the chairman." Stability and the ceiling are the same thing.

Conclusion

Management verdict: Execution 8, course-correction 7, shareholder friendliness 3, governance transparency 5. This is a team that can fight and can correct course, but will not return cash.
Investment implication: do not count on a governance-improvement re-rating; returns come from the cycle and market share, not from dividends or buybacks.
