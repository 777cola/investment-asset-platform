## Lee Jae-yong: the man who sets direction, not the man who runs the day-to-day

Lee Jae-yong formally took office as chairman in October 2022, after effectively running the company for a decade as vice chairman. His role is not in day-to-day operations—those are handled by professional managers across the three divisions—but in two things: signing off on major capital expenditure, and external relations (the US government, customers, labor unions).

The 2021 US trip that secured the Taylor fab, the post-2024 ramp-up of dedicated HBM lines, and the 2026 push into 2nm were all decisions made at the chairman level. Such decisions share three traits—long cycles, large sums, and no way to measure them by quarterly results—so one person must own them. This is the rationale for the chairman system: memory-industry capex demands the courage to bet against the cycle, while professional managers are typically appraised on a one-year horizon.

The risk lies in the same place. The other side of the chairman system is that no one can challenge the chairman. In the year HBM3E fell behind, outsiders widely read it as a technical misjudgment, while insiders called it a long certification process—and a long process is often a euphemism for no one daring to make the call.

## Jeon Young-hyun and the 2026 correction

In May 2024, Jun Young-hyun took over as head of the DS division, replacing Kye Hyun Kyung. The backdrop to the leadership change was that Samsung was comprehensively behind SK hynix on HBM3E qualification, with Nvidia orders going almost entirely to its rival. In the 18 months after the change, Samsung lifted its HBM share from just over 20% to 33% and became the first to mass-produce HBM4.

He got three things right. First, he elevated HBM yield improvement from a "divisional project" to a "company-wide project", giving R&D resources priority to HBM. Second, he changed the qualification process: instead of the old "complete internal validation, then send to the customer", it became "parallel validation with the customer", compressing the cycle from 12 months to 8. Third, he traded capacity for trust — first expanding HBM-dedicated lines as the customer requested, then negotiating price.

But note: the cost of this playbook is front-loaded capex. Investment in HBM-dedicated lines is booked intensively in 2025–2026, and depreciation will weigh on profit in 2027–2028. If prices turn down by then, this round of capacity expansion will become a burden.

## Capital allocation: three tracks of buybacks, dividends and capacity expansion

Samsung's capital allocation breaks into three lines. The first is capacity expansion: Pyeongtaek P4/P5, the Taylor fab's 2nm line, and dedicated HBM lines. Equipment investment in 2026 is the highest in its history, aimed at "grabbing share with the cost advantage of a downcycle." This line determines competitiveness over the next five years, and also determines current free cash flow.

The second is shareholder returns: in November 2024 it announced a KRW 10 trillion buyback plan (including share cancellation), alongside a commitment to annual dividends of no less than KRW 9.8 trillion for 2024-2026. The problem with this line is that it is a "fixed amount that does not float with profit" — with 2024 net profit of KRW 34 trillion, the payout ratio was 29%; with net profit of KRW 118.7 trillion in the first half of 2026, the full-year payout ratio will fall to 4%.

The third is M&A: the USD 8 billion acquisition of Harman in 2016 was the last large deal, and in the ten years since, no outlay has exceeded USD 1 billion. Samsung's M&A discipline is restrained by chaebol standards; the cost is that it has built no position in new directions such as automotive electronics and AI software.

Taken together: this is a company that plows all its profit back into capacity. Shareholders are "backers of capacity," not "beneficiaries of dividends." In an upcycle that is fine; in a downcycle it draws criticism.

## Integrity and governance: three incidents that defined its boundaries

The first was the 2017 bribery case. Lee Jae-yong was sentenced to five years for providing bribes to the Park Geun-hye government, had his sentence suspended on appeal, was paroled in 2021, and received a Liberation Day special pardon in 2022. The substance of this case was government-business relations, not operational fraud—the company's products, financial reports and customers were unaffected.

The second was the 2015 merger of Samsung C&T and Cheil Industries. Prosecutors alleged the merger price favored Lee Jae-yong's succession and harmed minority shareholders; in February 2025 the Seoul High Court acquitted him on appeal. The substance of this case is the systemic problem of chaebol inheritance, which remains unresolved—control is maintained through circular shareholding, and minority shareholders have no voice.

The third is the union. In 2024 Samsung faced its first large-scale strike since founding, and in 2025 a wage agreement was reached. For a company long known as "union-free," this is a substantive change in governance structure: labor costs will rise rigidly, but labor relations now have an institutional outlet.

What the three events share: they do not affect product competitiveness; they affect the valuation discount. Foreign investors have long assigned Samsung a lower PE than TSMC, and governance is one reason.

## Succession: not a problem, but no answer either

Lee Jae-yong was born in 1968 and turns 58 in 2026. He has no public succession plan, and neither of his two children has entered company management. Near term this is not a problem — by Korean chaebol convention, chairmen routinely serve past 70.

Over the medium term, however, it is a structural risk: if control passes, the mergers, restructurings and tax disputes of 2015 will recur. For secondary-market investors, this means the "governance discount" will not disappear — it will only resurface in another form.

The other thread is the professional-manager system. Samsung's three division heads are all internally groomed veterans with more than ten years in the role. This system ensures that "the chairman changes and the company keeps running," but it also ensures that "no one can challenge the chairman's judgment." That is both its stability and its ceiling.
