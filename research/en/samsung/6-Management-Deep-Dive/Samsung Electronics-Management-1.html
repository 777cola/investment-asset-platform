Core thesis: the chairman sets direction; the managers fight the battles

Samsung's governance is the archetypal Korean chaebol structure: Lee Jae-yong sets strategic direction, professional managers across the three divisions handle execution, and major capex and M&A are decided at group level. The upside is fast decision-making and the capacity to place heavy countercyclical bets — all three divisions expanded capacity in sync in 2026, with Samsung the most aggressive. The downside is that course correction rests on the chairman's own judgment, and the year-long lag on HBM3E stemmed precisely from too long a decision chain.

The capital allocation record cuts both ways. On one side, a KRW 10 trillion buyback in November 2024 and a commitment to pay at least KRW 9.8 trillion in dividends annually; on the other, earnings of KRW 118.7 trillion in the first half of 2026 against a payout ratio of just 4%. The money stayed inside the company to fund further capacity expansion, leaving shareholder returns fully decoupled from profit.

Lee Jae-yong: the man who sets direction, not the man who runs the day-to-day

Lee Jae-yong formally became chairman in October 2022, after effectively running the company for a decade as vice chairman. He does not run day-to-day operations—professional managers across the three divisions handle those—but owns two things: sign-off on major capital expenditure, and external relations (the US government, customers, labour unions).

The 2021 US trip that secured the Taylor fab, the post-2024 ramp-up of dedicated HBM lines and the 2026 push into 2nm were all decided at chairman level. Such decisions share three traits—long cycles, large sums and no way to measure them by quarterly results—so one person must own them. That is the rationale for the chairman system: memory-industry capex demands the courage to bet against the cycle, whereas professional managers are typically appraised on a one-year horizon.

The risk sits in the same place. The flipside of the chairman system is that no one can challenge the chairman. In the year HBM3E fell behind, outsiders widely read it as a technical misjudgment, while insiders called it a long certification process—and a long process is often a euphemism for no one daring to make the call.

Jeon Young-hyun and the 2026 correction

Jun Young-hyun took over as head of the DS division in May 2024, replacing Kye Hyun Kyung. The leadership change came against a backdrop of Samsung trailing SK hynix comprehensively on HBM3E qualification, with Nvidia orders going almost entirely to its rival. In the 18 months after the change, Samsung lifted its HBM share from just over 20% to 33% and became the first to mass-produce HBM4.

He got three things right. First, he elevated HBM yield improvement from a divisional project to a company-wide one, giving HBM priority on R&D resources. Second, he changed the qualification process from "complete internal validation, then send to the customer" to "parallel validation with the customer", compressing the cycle from 12 months to 8. Third, he traded capacity for trust — expanding HBM-dedicated lines as the customer requested first, then negotiating price.

But note: the cost of this playbook is front-loaded capex. Investment in HBM-dedicated lines is booked intensively in 2025–2026, and depreciation will weigh on profit in 2027–2028. If prices turn down by then, this round of capacity expansion will become a burden.

Capital allocation: buybacks, dividends and capacity expansion

Samsung's capital allocation breaks into three lines. The first is capacity expansion: Pyeongtaek P4/P5, the Taylor fab's 2nm line and dedicated HBM lines. Equipment investment in 2026 is the highest in its history, aimed at "grabbing share with the cost advantage of a downcycle." This line determines competitiveness over the next five years, and it also determines current free cash flow.

The second is shareholder returns: in November 2024 the company announced a KRW 10 trillion buyback (including share cancellation) and committed to annual dividends of no less than KRW 9.8 trillion for 2024-2026. The problem is that this is a "fixed amount that does not float with profit": on 2024 net profit of KRW 34 trillion the payout ratio was 29%, while on net profit of KRW 118.7 trillion in the first half of 2026 the full-year payout ratio falls to 4%.

The third is M&A: the USD 8 billion acquisition of Harman in 2016 was the last large deal, and in the ten years since no outlay has exceeded USD 1 billion. By chaebol standards Samsung's M&A discipline is restrained; the cost is that it has built no position in new directions such as automotive electronics and AI software.

Taken together, this is a company that plows all its profit back into capacity. Shareholders are "backers of capacity," not "beneficiaries of dividends." In an upcycle that is fine; in a downcycle it draws criticism.

Integrity and governance: three incidents that set the boundaries

The first was the 2017 bribery case. Lee Jae-yong was sentenced to five years for bribing the Park Geun-hye government, had the sentence suspended on appeal, was paroled in 2021 and received a Liberation Day special pardon in 2022. The substance of this case was government-business relations, not operational fraud: the company's products, financial reports and customers were unaffected.

The second was the 2015 merger of Samsung C&T and Cheil Industries. Prosecutors alleged the merger price favoured Lee Jae-yong's succession and harmed minority shareholders; the Seoul High Court acquitted him on appeal in February 2025. The substance of this case is the systemic problem of chaebol inheritance, which remains unresolved: control is maintained through circular shareholding, and minority shareholders have no voice.

The third is the union. In 2024 Samsung faced its first large-scale strike since founding, and a wage agreement was reached in 2025. For a company long known as "union-free," this is a substantive change in governance structure: labour costs will rise rigidly, but labour relations now have an institutional outlet.

What the three events share is that they do not affect product competitiveness; they affect the valuation discount. Foreign investors have long assigned Samsung a lower P/E than TSMC, and governance is one reason.

Succession: not a problem, but no answer either

Lee Jae-yong was born in 1968 and turns 58 in 2026. He has no public succession plan, and neither of his two children has entered company management. Near term this is not a problem — by Korean chaebol convention, chairmen routinely serve past 70.

Over the medium term, however, it is a structural risk: any transfer of control would revive the mergers, restructurings and tax disputes of 2015. For secondary-market investors, that means the "governance discount" will not disappear — it will only resurface in another form.

The other thread is the professional-manager system. Samsung's three division heads are all internally groomed veterans with more than ten years in the role. This system ensures that "the chairman changes and the company keeps running," but it also ensures that "no one can challenge the chairman's judgment." That is both its stability and its ceiling.

Conclusion

Management conclusion: Execution 8, course-correction 7, shareholder friendliness 3, governance transparency 5. This team fights and corrects course, but will not proactively return cash to shareholders.
Investment implication: do not expect a re-rating on governance improvement. Value the stock as a "retain profits for reinvestment" company, with returns driven mainly by the cycle rather than dividends or buybacks.
