# Samsung Electronics（005930.KS）— Quality Screen Supplement

- Research date：2026/09/21
- Price：275000.0 KRW
- Market cap：About KRW 1,793trn (USD 1.20trn, on 6.52bn shares at KRW 1,500/USD)

## Notes

Metric definitions: ROE = annualised H1 net profit of KRW 237.4 trillion ÷ net assets of approximately KRW 996 trillion (back-solved from 1.8x PB), a rough calculation; gross and net margin from the 2026Q2 consolidated statements; net cash reflects the company's long-standing absence of net interest-bearing debt and H1 2026 net profit of KRW 118.7 trillion; operating cash flow ratio is a rough calculation (net profit + depreciation and amortisation), not separately disclosed in the quarterly flash report; the 2023Q2 semiconductor loss of KRW 4.36 trillion reflects segment disclosure at the time. Threshold values are set by this framework, not by industry standards.

## What do the six approvals show

All three profitability metrics clear the bar by a wide margin: gross margin 69.6% (threshold 40%), net margin 41.8% (threshold 10%) and ROE of about 24% (threshold 15%). This is no bare pass — it is more than triple the required cushion. Price is the driver: DRAM average selling price rose 44% QoQ and NAND 53% while costs barely moved, so the price gains flowed straight through to gross profit.

The three balance-sheet metrics are cleaner still. Samsung has long been a net cash company — net profit of KRW 118.7 trillion in the first half of 2026 — and that money has not gone to repay debt, because there is no debt to repay. Operating cash flow covers net profit comfortably: memory is asset-heavy, with depreciation and amortisation running above KRW 30 trillion a year, so adding D&A back to net profit leaves cash flow above earnings.

The negative screen therefore concludes clearly: this is not a company that needs to be excluded. The seven hard metrics were designed to filter out names that look cheap but are value traps — high leverage, low gross margin, revenue propped up by receivables. Samsung matches none of these traits.

## The only one that fails: profit stability

Samsung's semiconductor division posted an operating loss of KRW 4.36 trillion in Q2 2023 — one of the ugliest single-quarter figures in the company's history. Three years later, in Q2 2026, the same division earned KRW 89.2 trillion. The peak-to-trough ratio exceeds 20x, failing any standard of "stability."

Why this is not fatal: the loss year reflected industry-wide prices falling below cost, not an operating failure. Samsung's DRAM share did not slip, its technology node did not fall behind, its customers did not leave; the price cycle simply hit bottom. Rejecting the stock on "profit stability" is equivalent to rejecting the entire memory industry on the grounds that "no cycles are allowed."

But this must be flagged because it changes the valuation method. Stable companies can be valued on P/E or DCF; cyclical companies only on P/B, normalised earnings or replacement cost. Every target price below uses cyclical multiples, not growth multiples, precisely because this test fails.

## If only four had passed, the conclusion would be different.

Run the reverse test: assume gross margin of only 45%, net margin of 12% and net debt of RMB 3 trillion. Even at a P/E of just 7.5x, you should not buy it, because it would be the first to die in a downturn. Samsung is not that case; its net cash and 39.4% share are proof that it "won't die."

What truly warrants caution is a different combination: all seven criteria passed, but valuation at 20x P/E. At that point it is a "good company at an expensive price," carrying more risk than now. Now it is a "good company at a cyclical peak, at a discounted price." The sources of risk differ, and so does the handling: the former means waiting for a pullback; the latter means buying in tranches and controlling position size.

## Actions after weeding out the weak

Six of seven criteria passed, so move to the next layer: is it worth buying, and at what price. Negative screening answers neither question; valuation and cycle position do. The only conclusion negative screening delivers is this: it is worth spending time on.

Operationally, that means: allow it to take 3%–5% of the portfolio and allow adding on declines (it is not a value trap), but do not take a heavy position just because "the P/E is only 7.5x" (the denominator is unsustainable). That is the difference between "flagging" and "eliminating".
