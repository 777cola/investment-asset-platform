Industry Chain — Samsung Electronics

Core call: Samsung holds two of the three most profitable segments in this chain

Value along the memory supply chain is dumbbell-shaped: upstream equipment (ASML, 52% gross margin) and downstream AI chips (Nvidia, over 70% gross margin) capture the most, while midstream manufacturing only makes money when supply is tight. 2026 is a shortage year, so midstream gross margins have overtaken upstream — Samsung at 69.6%, SK hynix at an operating margin of 71.5%.

Samsung's position is unusual: it sits in three segments at once — midstream manufacturing (39.4% of DRAM, top three in NAND), packaging (HBM stacking plus its own CoWoS alternative) and downstream end devices (phones, TVs). It is the only company in the chain doing all three. The cost is that it leads none of them: second in memory, second in HBM, third in foundry, first in phones but not a profit centre.

Upstream: equipment is the only stable toll collector

The only player in this chain that does not bear price risk is the equipment maker. ASML's EUV machines cost more than EUR 200 million each and it is the only company in the world that makes them; Applied Materials' deposition equipment and Lam's etch equipment each hold more than 70% share in their respective segments. Their gross margins hold at 45%–52% year-round, fluctuating not with memory prices but only with capital expenditure.

With the three memory makers expanding capacity in sync in 2026, equipment makers' order books are already full through 2027. For investors, this is the "selling shovels" position—it gets paid whether or not the memory makers make money. The trade-off is slower growth than the memory makers': memory makers' profit rose 18-fold in a year, while equipment makers rose 50% at most.

The materials segment is weaker. Silicon wafers (Shin-Etsu, SUMCO), photoresist and electronic specialty gases carry gross margins of 30%–40%, and pricing power depends on supply and demand. Silicon wafers were in short supply in 2026 and rose a little in tandem, but far less than DRAM.

Midstream: manufacturing is the cycle, and the most profitable segment in 2026

Memory manufacturing is the only link in this chain where "pricing is set by the players themselves (the three)". In 2026Q2, Samsung's gross margin was 69.6%, SK hynix's operating margin 71.5% and Micron's gross margin 84.9% — all three at their highest-ever profitability at the same time, a textbook year of industry-chain profit concentrating in the midstream.

But this is also the most dangerous position. Upstream equipment makers can raise prices and downstream chipmakers can pass costs through; only midstream manufacturing must bear the "capacity expansion—glut—price cuts" cycle alone. The 69.6% gross margin of 2026 was bought with the RMB 436 billion loss of 2023.

HBM is the only subsegment here with a "customer relationship" attribute. It must be validated and iterated alongside the GPU, and customers will not switch suppliers just because a rival is 5% cheaper. So HBM gross margins run 15–20 percentage points above ordinary DRAM, and they fall more slowly when prices decline.

AI chips attracted the most capital and carry the highest expectations

NVIDIA's gross margin exceeds 70%, and HBM accounts for 30%–40% of the cost of a single GPU.In other words, the HBM Samsung sells to NVIDIA ultimately feeds into NVIDIA's gross margin.Who holds the most power in this chain? In 2026, it is NVIDIA—it controls both demand (orders) and allocation (who gets the HBM orders).

The end-market segment fares worst. Makers of phones, PCs and TVs have no pricing power; when memory prices rise, they either absorb the cost or cut specifications. Samsung's MX division losing KRW 0.7 trillion and its CE division posting a small loss are direct manifestations of this mechanism.The sole exception is Apple—its brand premium lets it pass costs on to consumers.

So profit distribution across the chain is:equipment makers collect rent → memory makers ride the cycle → AI chip makers capture the premium → end-market players absorb the cost.2026 is the best year for memory makers and AI chip makers, and the worst year for end-market players.

Samsung's position: present in all three segments, never first in any

Samsung is the only company in the chain that does memory, packaging and end devices at once: memory share 39.4% (No. 1), HBM share 33% (No. 2), foundry share about 10% (No. 3, behind TSMC and SMIC), and handsets No. 1 globally (though not a profit centre).

The advantage of this position is resilience: when memory loses money, devices cushion it; when devices lose money, memory cushions it. 2023 proved the point. The downside is that every segment must fight the strongest rival: memory against SK hynix, HBM against SK hynix, foundry against TSMC, handsets against Apple.

Its supply-chain position in one sentence: it is the "all-rounder" of this chain, not a "specialist champion." An all-rounder earns the most at the top of the cycle, given the largest capacity, and survives the longest at the bottom, given the most diversified businesses, but it never commands a specialist champion's valuation premium.

Cycle position: midstream margin peaks typically lead the inflection point by one or two quarters

In past cycles, the years when industry-chain profit concentrated in the midstream always came late in the cycle. 2017Q4, 2018Q2 and 2021Q4 all followed the same pattern: memory makers' gross margins hit record highs, then prices turned down one to two quarters later. The reason is that profit concentration spurs capacity expansion, and capacity expansion turns into oversupply two years later.

Current readings: DRAM contract prices are up 44% QoQ (Q1 was up more than 60%), so the gain is narrowing; all three makers are raising capex in tandem; HBM takes more than 20% of wafer capacity and is still squeezing ordinary DRAM supply. Two of the three are already turning, one is still supportive.

Conclusion: industry-chain profit distribution is already at its most favourable point for the midstream, but the marginal change has already turned unfavourable. For Samsung this means earnings will still look good over the next two quarters (prices lag), but the valuation will not follow — the market will price in the 2028 oversupply ahead of time.

Conclusion

Industry-chain conclusion: value is distributed in a dumbbell shape; the midstream captured the fattest segment in 2026, but historically that position has never lasted long. Samsung spans manufacturing, packaging and end devices, which gives it the greatest resilience but less upside than pure-play memory names.
Investment implication: buying Samsung means buying "the last two quarters before midstream margins peak + HBM share catch-up," not buying "industry-chain upgrading."
