## Upstream: equipment is the only stable toll collector

The only player in this chain that does not bear price risk is the equipment maker. ASML's EUV machines cost more than EUR 200 million each, and it is the only company in the world that makes them; Applied Materials' deposition equipment and Lam's etch equipment each hold more than 70% share in their respective segments. Their gross margins hold at 45%–52% year-round, fluctuating not with memory prices but only with capital expenditure.

With the three memory makers expanding capacity in sync in 2026, equipment makers' order books are already full through 2027. For investors, this is the "selling shovels" position—it gets paid whether or not the memory makers make money. The trade-off is that its growth is slower than the memory makers': memory makers' profit rose 18-fold in a year, while equipment makers rose 50% at most.

The materials segment is weaker. Silicon wafers (Shin-Etsu, SUMCO), photoresist and electronic specialty gases carry gross margins of 30%–40%, and pricing power depends on supply and demand. Silicon wafers were in short supply in 2026, and they rose a little in tandem, but far less than DRAM.

## Midstream: manufacturing is the cycle itself, and the most profitable segment in 2026

Memory manufacturing is the only link in this chain where "pricing is set by the players themselves (the three)". In 2026Q2, Samsung's gross margin was 69.6%, SK hynix's operating margin 71.5%, and Micron's gross margin 84.9% — all three sit at their highest-ever profitability at the same time, a textbook year of industry-chain profit concentrating in the midstream.

But this position is also the most dangerous. Upstream equipment makers can raise prices, downstream chipmakers can pass costs through, and only midstream manufacturing must bear the "capacity expansion—glut—price cuts" cycle alone. The 69.6% gross margin of 2026 was bought with the RMB 436 billion loss of 2023.

HBM is the only subsegment here with a "customer relationship" attribute. It must be validated and iterated alongside the GPU, and customers will not switch suppliers just because a rival is 5% cheaper. So HBM gross margins run 15–20 percentage points above ordinary DRAM, and they fall more slowly when prices decline.

## Downstream: AI chips captured the most capital and carry the highest expectations

NVIDIA's gross margin exceeds 70%, and HBM accounts for 30%–40% of the cost of a single GPU.In other words, the HBM Samsung sells to NVIDIA ultimately becomes part of NVIDIA's gross margin.Who holds the most power in this chain? In 2026, it is NVIDIA—it controls both demand (orders) and allocation (who gets the HBM orders).

The end-market segment fares worst. Makers of phones, PCs and TVs have no pricing power; when memory prices rise, they either absorb the cost or cut specifications. Samsung's MX division losing KRW 0.7 trillion and its CE division posting a small loss are direct manifestations of this mechanism.The sole exception is Apple—its brand premium lets it pass costs on to consumers.

So profit distribution across the chain is:equipment makers collect rent → memory makers ride the cycle → AI chip makers capture the premium → end-market players absorb the cost.2026 is the best year for memory makers and AI chip makers, and the worst year for end-market players.

## Samsung's position: present in all three segments, never first in any

Samsung is the only company in this chain that does memory, packaging and end devices at the same time. Memory share 39.4% (No. 1), HBM share 33% (No. 2), foundry share about 10% (No. 3, behind TSMC and SMIC), handsets No. 1 globally (but not a profit center).

The advantage of this position is resilience: when memory loses money, devices cushion it; when devices lose money, memory cushions it. This was proven once in 2023. The downside is that every segment must fight the strongest rival: memory against SK hynix, HBM against SK hynix, foundry against TSMC, handsets against Apple.

Its supply-chain position in one sentence: it is the "all-rounder" of this chain, not a "specialist champion." An all-rounder earns the most at the top of the cycle (because it has the largest capacity) and survives the longest at the bottom (because its businesses are the most diversified), but it never commands the valuation premium of a specialist champion.

## Cycle position: when midstream margins peak, it is usually one or two quarters before the inflection point

In past cycles, the years when industry-chain profit concentrated in the midstream always came in the later stage of the cycle. 2017Q4, 2018Q2 and 2021Q4 were all like this: memory makers' gross margins hit record highs, then prices turned down one to two quarters later. The reason is that profit concentration spurs capacity expansion, and capacity expansion turns into oversupply two years later.

Current readings: DRAM contract prices are up 44% quarter on quarter (Q1 was up more than 60%), so the gain is narrowing; all three makers are raising capex in tandem; HBM takes more than 20% of wafer capacity and is still squeezing ordinary DRAM supply. Two of the three are already turning, one is still supportive.

Conclusion: industry-chain profit distribution is already at its most favorable point for the midstream, but the marginal change has already turned unfavorable. For Samsung this means: earnings will still look good over the next two quarters (prices lag), but the valuation will not follow — the market will price in the 2028 oversupply ahead of time.
