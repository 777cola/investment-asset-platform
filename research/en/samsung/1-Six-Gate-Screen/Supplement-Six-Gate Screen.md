## Gate one: circle of competence — all three lines are understandable; the hard part is the cycle position

Samsung makes money in three ways: selling DRAM and NAND at contract prices to server, handset and PC makers; selling leading-edge capacity by the wafer to design companies; and selling phones and TVs by the unit to consumers. Three sentences and you're done — no technical background required. This gate scores 9; the 1 point deducted reflects a failure to understand the cycle, not the business.

Judging where memory stocks sit requires three data sets: QoQ contract price changes, channel inventory in weeks, and the capex cadence of the big three. All three are available, but all three lag — contract prices are published monthly, and by the time you see them turn down, the stock has already fallen 20%. "Understanding the company" and "understanding the stock price" are therefore two different things, and the edge of the circle of competence lies in the latter.

Foundry is messier: 2nm yield, customer mix, the Taylor fab ramp — from the outside you can only infer these from order changes at Nvidia, Qualcomm and Tesla. I rate myself "half competent" here, so none of the valuation that follows prices foundry separately; I treat it as an option.

## Stage two: a good business — the 69.6% gross margin reflects oligopoly pricing

2026Q2 gross margin 69.6% versus 34.2% a year earlier. A 35pp gross margin gain in a year is not management improvement — it is price. DRAM ASP rose 44% QoQ and NAND 53%; with all three suppliers running at full utilisation and supply still short, pricing power sits entirely with the seller.

A good business meets three tests: customers cannot do without it, competitors cannot get in, and the seller sets the price. Memory satisfied the first two in 2017–2018, when Samsung's peak quarterly operating profit was KRW 17.6 trillion; this cycle it reached KRW 89.49 trillion, five times that level. The difference is HBM: the same wafer sold as HBM to Nvidia generates four to five times the revenue of ordinary DDR5.

The 2-point deduction reflects unstable cash flow. A good business should still earn money in a downturn; memory cannot: the semiconductor division lost KRW 4.36 trillion in 2023Q2 and earned KRW 89.2 trillion in 2026Q2. A twentyfold swing in three years makes this a commodity, not a consumer product.

## Stage three: the moat lies in cost and capacity, not technological leadership

DRAM revenue share of 39.4%, 14.5 percentage points ahead of second-placed SK hynix at 24.9%; HBM share of 33%, up 12 percentage points from 21% last quarter; HBM4 the first in the world to ship in mass production. Taken together, these three numbers show it has at least not fallen behind.

But the nature of the moat needs to be clear. Samsung's advantage is that it "can afford to lose": vertical integration (in-house procurement of materials other than equipment, in-house packaging, its own end devices to absorb capacity), a net-cash balance sheet, and the largest single-fab scale in the world. When prices fall below cash cost, it can hold out until rivals cut output first, then trade share for the next cycle. This moat guarantees it survives, not that it is immune to price cuts.

Its technology lead is "six months", not "three years". It trailed SK hynix by about a year on HBM3E, has moved half a step ahead on HBM4, and is still awaiting results on 12-layer verification for HBM4E. So the third gate scores 8, with the 2-point deduction for technology uncertainty.

## Gate four: management — quick to correct mistakes, but the half-step lag is structural

From 2024 to the first half of 2025, Samsung trailed SK hynix by a full year on HBM3E, with Nvidia awarding almost all orders to its rival. The reasons are not complicated: yields were resolved late, internal qualification took long, and the DS division changed leadership (Jun Young-hyun took over in May 2024). A decision that could have been made in one quarter took three.

2026 reverses this: first to mass-produce HBM4, share recovering from 21% to 33%, and the Taylor fab's 2nm progressing on schedule. The capacity for course correction remains, but the method is "change people + spend more," not "make processes faster." The same half-step-behind will recur at the next technology transition.

Capital allocation is another layer. First-half 2026 net profit reached KRW 118.7 trillion, yet dividends remain at the KRW 9.8 trillion floor, a payout ratio of about 4%. The money stayed on the company's books rather than returning to shareholders—at the top of the cycle this is conservative, and possibly wasteful. Read this item alongside the sixth gate, so it scores only 6.

## Gate Five: Margin of safety — the P/E is cheap, not the business

At 7.5x P/E and 1.8x P/B, Samsung's market cap is KRW 1,793 trillion (about USD 1.20 trillion). On the numbers alone it is one of the cheapest large-cap tech companies in the world: on the same 2026Q2 profit, Nvidia trades at more than six times its valuation and TSMC at four times.

The problem is the denominator. Of the KRW 89.49 trillion in single-quarter operating profit, the vast majority is price contribution — memory bit shipments grew only modestly, yet revenue rose +471% YoY. Prices can turn; capacity does not disappear. If DRAM contract prices fall 30% in 2027, profit on the same capacity drops below KRW 40 trillion, and 7.5x instantly becomes more than 15x.

So the margin of safety does not score "cheap"; it scores "downside is cushioned". Backing out from 1.8x P/B gives net assets of about KRW 996 trillion, or book value per share of about KRW 153,000. The bear-case target price of KRW 110,000 implies 0.72x P/B — Samsung has never fallen below 0.8x P/B in its history. This is not cheap valuation; it is a floor provided by the balance sheet.

## Gate 6: Discipline — can the buy thesis be falsified?

The question this gate asks: if three months from now prove me wrong, will I know where I went wrong? For Samsung the answer is yes: HBM share (reported quarterly), DRAM contract prices (reported monthly) and DS division margin (reported quarterly) can all falsify my thesis.

The reason to buy is not "AI will keep growing" but two specific things: HBM share continuing to climb from 33%, and HBM4E entering Nvidia's mass-production list within the year. The first is already in the data; the second is yet to be verified. If both fail, the investment should be exited, regardless of whether the stock goes up or down.

The 3-point deduction reflects how hard it is to stay disciplined with cyclicals: when the stock rises, it is easy to forget it is a cyclical; when it falls, it is easy to mistake the cycle for growth. So the entry rules must be written down upfront — three tranches, at least one month apart, with a hard cap on total position size.

## Veto items and items to be verified

Veto items: none. None of the six gates is a hard veto, and that is what separates this from a theme stock: not "a good story" but "real profit, real share, real cash."

Three items remain to be verified. ① Q3 preliminary guidance in early October: consensus expects operating profit above KRW 95 trillion; below KRW 90 trillion would signal that price-hike pass-through has run its course. ② Whether HBM4E 12-layer clears Nvidia certification within the year — the precondition for share to reach 40% in 2027. ③ Whether foundry actually turns profitable after excluding one-off performance bonuses: disclosure is opaque, so the only read is next quarter's internal DS structure.

The biggest risk is not the company, it is the price. The stock trades below 5x expected 2027 profit, and that is only cheap if "no shortage in 2027" holds. Once contract prices turn down in the first half, 5x becomes 15x. Hence the rating is "Buy (staged)" rather than "Buy."
