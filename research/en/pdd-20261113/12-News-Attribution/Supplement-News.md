## Three-month picture: down 9%, mostly in two stretches

USD 86.94 on August 17 against USD 78.97 on November 13 — a 9.2% decline over three months. The path was not one-way: the stock spiked to USD 90.2 on August 19 (the three-month high), plunged to USD 75.38 on October 2 (the low, down 16% from the high), then rebounded to around USD 79 in November. September saw the steepest drop; mid-October to early November delivered the steadiest gains.

## Attribution 1: The results were not the cause of the selloff — merely the pretext for it

Revenue came in at RMB 112.36 billion on August 24, 3% below consensus, with Non-GAAP net profit at −13% — a clear miss. Yet the stock fell just 1.5% that day and rose 0.8% the next. The market had already priced in ugly numbers: 2026Q1 net profit was already −15%, so another weak quarter surprised no one.

The real de-rating came after earnings. Soochow cut its 2026 net profit forecast to RMB 99.5 billion on August 29, and the stock fell −2.0% on August 31. Sell-side downgrades hurt the stock more than the earnings themselves, because what they reset is the anchor for the next four quarters.

## Attribution 2: the 7.3% move in September was unrelated to the company

The stock fell from USD 84 on August 31 to USD 77.84 on September 10, down 7.3% over seven trading sessions, with no company-specific news in between. Hang Seng Tech and China ADRs broadly fell over the same period as the market's focus shifted from the AI narrative to cash flow. This leg is beta, not alpha.

The test is simple: if comparable assets fell by a similar amount over the same period, the move is market-driven; only a decline in isolation belongs to the company. September's move was the former.

## Attribution 3: Two rounds of tariff headlines, with a diminishing market reaction each time

CBP's proposed rule landed on October 8, bringing everything below USD 2,500 under strict scrutiny — a substantive negative — yet the stock only edged down that day and actually rose 3.95% on October 9. A textbook "bad news priced in": a policy trailed for six months was finally issued, the uncertainty cleared and shorts covered.

The EU's proposed EUR 6 billion levy on November 7 sent the stock down 3.05% on November 9, while its October 25 announcement of a EUR 2 per-item handling fee drew only a 1.2% decline on October 26. Bad news pointing the same way, with the market's marginal reaction fading — evidence these costs are being written into the price.

## Which parts are noise, and which are worth remembering

Noise: The 7.3% drawdown in September, the 1.9% drop on November 12 (no Singles' Day sales report — there was never going to be one) and the down day on October 21. None of these change any assumption.

What matters: ① Sell-side is cutting earnings estimates (the late-August round) — a trend, not a one-off; ② the market reaction to tariff news is fading, suggesting pricing is becoming fuller; ③ the stock has support in the USD 75–80 range — both probes into that range, on October 2 and November 12, were bought back.

Next catalyst: Q3 results (late November). This is the most important event of the next three months. The market expects revenue growth of around 6% and a double-digit YoY decline in Non-GAAP net income. I am watching one thing only: whether transaction services revenue growth holds at 10%.

## Why bad news no longer crushes the market: three technical reasons

1. Valuation already leaves little room to short. At 8.2x TTM and 3.4x ex-cash, shorts can only make real money by betting on a profit collapse, and RMB 456.4 billion in cash makes that outcome unlikely.

2. Institutional ownership is already light. Institutions hold about 28.66%, insiders about 32%, and the rest is retail and passive funds. No crowded long means no stampede.

3. The marginal impact of bad news is fading. Tariffs went from "rumor" to "proposed rule" to "formal fees," and the market priced in part of it each time; by the time the actual document lands, it turns into good news.
