Core view

Buffett reads financial statements in a fixed order: first, whether profit is real cash; second, how clean the assets are; last, how much return shareholder earnings can deliver.

This report's answer: the cash is real (operating cash flow of RMB 25.7 billion, +19%), the assets are exceptionally clean (zero interest-bearing debt), and shareholder earnings run at about RMB 100 billion a year.

Pinduoduo carries minimal fixed assets and maintenance capex is close to zero, so shareholder earnings approximate Non-GAAP net profit. Capex is not disclosed separately and is estimated roughly from the company's asset-light structure.
Question 1: Is the profit real money?

Operating cash flow of RMB 25.7 billion, +19% YoY, covered 0.95x the RMB 27.18 billion net profit attributable to shareholders. That is below 1x for the quarter, but Pinduoduo's working capital is negative (merchants pay first, the platform settles later), so the full-year ratio typically exceeds 1.

No receivables risk, no inventory write-downs, no capitalised R&D. In accounting terms this is the "cleanest" kind of income statement — it leaves management almost no room for adjustment.

Question two: How clean are the assets?

RMB 456.4 billion in cash and short-term investments, zero bank borrowings, zero bonds. Cash dominates total assets; fixed assets are minimal. This is a balance sheet that is almost nothing but money.

Buffett's first question would be whether that money is truly free cash. Part of it is merchant funds pending settlement and security deposits — float that legally sits in liabilities. Any "cash-adjusted valuation" therefore has to be discounted, the single most easily overlooked detail in this report.

Q3: what return does shareholder surplus deliver?

At a market cap of RMB 798.0 billion, RMB 100.0 billion of shareholder earnings yields 12.5%; net of cash, the RMB 341.6 billion market cap implies 29%. On any market, that yield is cheap enough that no precise calculation is needed.

But he would add: a high yield does not mean you get it. For shareholder earnings to become shareholders' money, they must be paid out via dividends or buybacks, and this company has done neither. So the 29% is a "paper yield".

Conclusion

Buffett-style conclusion: this is as clean a set of financials as he rarely sees, paired with a zero-return policy he rarely sees.

At 3.4x ex-cash P/E and a 29% ex-cash shareholder earnings yield, the price is not the problem. The problem is that the money is not in his pocket.

His likely move: do not buy, but keep it in the notebook and wait for a buyback announcement.
