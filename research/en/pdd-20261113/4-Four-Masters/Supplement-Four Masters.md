## Duan Yongping: business 8 out of 10, people 4 out of 10 — combined, not a position for a heavy bet

Duan Yongping would grasp this business quickly: the platform holds no inventory and builds no warehouses; merchants undercut each other on price while the platform takes commission and sells advertising. This is rent collection, not trading. A 57% gross margin and 24% net margin are an anomaly in retail — JD.com sold RMB 346.4 billion of goods and earned just RMB 7.1 billion in a quarter.

What he really cares about, though, is the people. With RMB 456.4 billion on the books and five years without dividends, buybacks or any explanation of how the cash will be used, to him that is not a question of ability but of willingness. He bought Apple and Moutai because management treats shareholders as partners; Pinduoduo's management treats shareholders as capital providers — the money came in, and how it gets spent is my call.

His conclusion, in his own idiom, would run roughly: "I understand this business, and the price is cheap, but I don't like how they do things. Buy a little, not a lot." Business 8 out of 10, people 4 out of 10 — weighted together, this is not something he would take a heavy position in.

## Buffett: strip out the cash and you get the business for free — provided that cash can be put to work

Buffett's first move is to deduct cash. Market cap of RMB 798.0 billion, cash of RMB 456.4 billion — the core business sells for RMB 341.6 billion against RMB 99.5 billion of profit, or 3.4x. He has bought companies at 5x earnings, but he has never hesitated at 3.4x.

He would add two conditions, and neither is a formality. First, the cash must be accessible: if the RMB 456.4 billion has to be held back for fines and localization spending, it cannot be added back at face value. Second, the business must not be disappearing — advertising growth of 3.5% barely counts as "not dead," but it is far from "growth."

The third he would not say aloud but would certainly hold in mind: he never bets big where he cannot understand the regulatory environment. China ADRs have always fallen for him into the category of "I understand the business, not the rules." So his answer would be "absurdly cheap, but outside my circle of competence."

## Munger: a stock is often cheap because it deserves to be

Munger would split Pinduoduo into three parts: the domestic main site (mature, growth peaking), Temu (fast growth, uncapped regulatory exposure) and the cash on the balance sheet (which does not belong to shareholders). Only the first is certain, and the first is no longer growing.

He would repeat what he has said many times: I have seen far too many traps in cheap goods. The governance discount on Chinese ADRs is not the market going mad — VIE structures, super-voting rights and disclosure timing decided by the company are real risks, not bias.

But he would also concede the structure is asymmetric: RMB 456.4 billion cushions the downside, while the upside is a valuation re-rating. His approach would be: you can buy, but never use leverage, never take a heavy position, and never pretend you understand it just because it is cheap.

## Li Lu: a classic asymmetric opportunity, with position sizing as the risk control

In Li Lu's framework the best opportunity is not the best company but the one with the most asymmetric payoff. Pinduoduo now offers 17% downside against 62% upside — a ratio of 3.6 to 1. The same structure drew him to BYD: the business was at an inflection point and the price had not yet reflected it.

He is more willing to buy than on the previous three occasions because he habitually hunts for mispricing inside "institutional risk." Chinese assets are not a black box to him but an object of study — one that simply deserves a discount.

He would, however, insist on position discipline. The tail risks of Chinese ADRs — regulation, delisting, geopolitics — cannot be researched away, only controlled through position sizing. His approach: a 3% cap, built in tranches over three to four quarters, with "no re-rating for a long time" treated as the base case rather than a surprise.

## Three-scenario valuation: bear case USD 66, bull case USD 128

Valuation is sum-of-the-parts: apply a P/E to the core business, then add back RMB 456.4 billion of cash at face value. No method suits PDD better. Cash is such a large share of the whole that a consolidated P/E badly distorts the picture: 8.2x consolidated looks unremarkable, but 3.4x ex-cash is the real story.

The bear case assumes 2027 net profit falls to RMB 70 billion and the market assigns only 3x (below today's level), yielding USD 66, 17% downside. The base case assumes RMB 90 billion at 6x, yielding USD 99, 25% upside. The bull case assumes RMB 105 billion at 8x, yielding USD 128, 62% upside.

The RMB 105 billion in the bull case is no fantasy — 2025 Non-GAAP net profit was already RMB 107.3 billion; as long as Temu stops burning cash and main-site advertising returns to 6%, getting back to that level is a recovery, not a breakthrough.

## Odds of 3.6 to 1, but those odds come with a caveat

Upside 62%, downside 17% — a 3.6-to-1 payoff, and one of the few buy-side structures on the list. But be clear: the downside protection rests on cash carried at face value.

If Temu's compliance costs and fines consume a third of that cash, the bear-case floor drops from USD 66 to USD 45, the payoff falls to 1.4-to-1, and the investment no longer holds. The real bet, then, is not whether Pinduoduo can grow again, but whether management burns this money overseas.

## Where all four could be wrong at the same time

All four paragraphs above rest on the same premise: "cash is worth at least face value, and the business will not suddenly disappear." Both parts can be wrong, and they can fail together: Temu turns from a growth engine into a cash black hole.

The US now subjects all packages under USD 2,500 to strict scrutiny, the EU imposes a fixed cost of up to EUR 5 per category, and product-safety investigations carry penalties of up to 6% of global revenue. Stack these three together and Temu has only one path: local warehouses, local procurement, local compliance. That path costs money, and it converts balance-sheet cash into expenses.

If in 2027 quarterly transaction services revenue growth falls below 5% while cash declines quarter on quarter, the four masters' common premise has broken. That is not a valuation issue; the business has changed.
