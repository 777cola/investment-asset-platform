# Pinduoduo（PDD）— Industry Chain Supplement

- Research date：2026/11/13
- Price：78.97 USD
- Market cap：About USD 112.4 billion

## Notes

China's online retail sales reached about RMB 15 trillion in 2025, with e-commerce penetration at about 45% (approximate public industry figures). GMV-based estimates put Alibaba at about 45%, JD.com at about 20%, Pinduoduo at about 15% and Douyin at about 10%; these are not official statistics. Public industry gross margin ranges are 5%–15% for white-label factories, 40%–60% for brands and 10%–20% for logistics. Platform gross margins use company-disclosed figures for the latest fiscal year: Pinduoduo 57.3%, JD.com about 10%, Alibaba about 40% (approximate). Temu localisation data (US fully managed share falling from about 70% to below 30%; target of 80% local warehouses by end-2026) come from third-party research and are not disclosed by the company, so error may be significant. EU fees: EUR 3 per category from 2026/7/1 plus EUR 2 per category from 2026/11/1.

## The chain has three layers, with the platform at its centre

Upstream is supply: white-label factories, industrial belts, agricultural production bases and brand owners. Midstream is platforms — Pinduoduo, Alibaba, JD.com and Douyin E-commerce — plus payments, logistics and third-party operations. Downstream splits into two groups: China's 800 million-plus price-sensitive users and Temu's overseas users.

Value distribution along this chain is extremely uneven. On a good with a factory-gate price of RMB 10, the factory earns RMB 0.5 to 1.5, express delivery earns RMB 1.5, the platform earns RMB 2 to 3 (advertising plus commission), and the rest goes to traffic and channel cost. The platform is the only link that reliably makes big money — which is why Pinduoduo's gross margin is 57% while factories' gross margin is under 15%.

## Both ends of the smile curve are already occupied

The classic smile curve has both ends high and the middle low: brand and R&D at one end, channel and users at the other. Pinduoduo has secured the channel-and-users end but holds no brand end. That is its structural weakness—it can make factories cheap, but it cannot make them valuable.

So it can only keep squeezing efficiency out of the supply side: C2M, direct links to industrial belts, and refund-only policies that force quality improvements. The ceiling of this playbook is clear: once factory gross margins are squeezed below 5%, no one is willing to improve quality for it. The low-price moat ends where supply quality begins.

## Temu has stretched the chain by an entire layer

The domestic e-commerce chain has four segments: factory—platform—courier—consumer. Temu's fully managed model has eight: factory—platform—freight forwarder—customs declaration—line haul—overseas customs clearance—local delivery—consumer. Every extra segment adds a cost item and a regulatory checkpoint.

In 2026 this layer picked up two charges: a flat EUR 3 tariff per product category in the EU from July 1, plus a EUR 2 handling fee per category from November 1, for EUR 5 per category in total. On a product with an average order value of EUR 10, that is 50% in cost. Temu's response is semi-managed plus local warehouses: the US fully managed share has already been cut from 70% to below 30%, with the goal of 80% of US orders fulfilled from local warehouses by the end of 2026.

Localisation solves tariffs but not another problem: it turns Temu from an asset-light platform into a quasi-retailer with inventory, warehousing and a compliance team. The valuation method has to shift from platform multiples to retailer multiples.

## The platform is shifting from asset-light to asset-heavy

The 2025 annual report cites the creation of "Xinpinmu" to test own-brand products; Temu is building local warehouses in Europe and the US; Duoduo Maicai runs its own front-warehouse system. All three point the same way: Pinduoduo is moving downstream into supply and fulfillment.

Moving downstream buys two things—cost control and the initiative on compliance. The price is that capex and inventory risk shift from the factories onto the company itself. If the path works, gross margin dilutes but the moat widens; if it fails, the company will have given up the asset-light model without gaining market access.

## Cycle position: not a demand cycle, a rule cycle

China e-commerce growth has slowed from over 20% to 7%–10%, with 2025 online retail sales of about RMB 1.5 trillion and penetration around 45%. This is a stock market, and share is won by taking it. Pinduoduo holds about 15% of the domestic market, Alibaba about 45%, JD.com about 20% and Douyin about 10% — it ranks third, with two larger players ahead and one faster-growing player behind.

Cross-border is in the early stage of "rule repricing": the US de minimis exemption ended in August 2025, and the CBP proposed rule of October 2026 brings all shipments under USD 2,500 into strict supervision; the EU scrapped the EUR 150 duty-free threshold in July and added a handling fee in November; product safety investigations are still proceeding. This is not a demand downturn; the cost structure is being rewritten by legislation. Until that rewrite is complete, no one knows where the new equilibrium lies.

## If the chain reorders, who gets hurt first

First, white-label factories: once platform self-operation and local procurement begin, orders will concentrate among the few factories able to meet standards, squeezing out smaller ones. Second, logistics providers such as J&T that are deeply tied to Temu: local warehouses replace direct shipping, so cross-border small-parcel volumes will fall.

PDD itself has a buffer — it holds RMB 456.4 billion in cash, while other links in the chain have none. That is the difference between the chain leader and its subordinates: when the rules change, the leader can rearrange the chain; the subordinates can only be rearranged.
