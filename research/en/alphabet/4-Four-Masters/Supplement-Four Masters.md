## Duan Yongping: This is a business where "you can make money without trying too hard," but it has gotten heavier

Duan Yongping asks only two questions about a business: is it a good business, and do I understand it. In his eyes, Alphabet's search advertising is a textbook good business—a user types a word, advertisers pay for the intent behind that word, marginal cost is near zero, and the more users there are the more data there is and the more accurate the ads become. It is a wheel that turns on its own. **"Making money without trying too hard"—on this measure, the search business has been a perfect score for twenty years.**

His hesitation is over capital expenditure. What Duan Yongping likes is asset-light, high-cash-flow businesses: Apple's capex is under 3% of revenue, and Moutai barely needs to invest money to expand capacity. **Alphabet's 2026 capex is 40% of revenue**—this is no longer the kind of business he is familiar with, but an infrastructure business. Infrastructure businesses are valued differently—not on profit margin, but on return on capital. **He would buy, but he would treat the money as "investing in a new utility" when running the numbers, not as "buying a money printer."**

## Buffett: The moat is impeccable; capital allocation makes him uncomfortable

Buffett would look at the moat first. The default entry points (Chrome at roughly two-thirds of browser share, Android at roughly 70% of mobile OS share), the data flywheel, in-house TPU development, and Gemini distribution — these four reinforce each other and were preserved by the antitrust ruling: **the DOJ's December 2025 remedy did not require divesting Chrome or Android, only data sharing and a choice screen. That is the lightest outcome obtainable.**

Then he would turn to the cash flow statement and be displeased. A company with USD 265.1 billion in cash and securities issuing USD 28.7 billion of debt, issuing 36 million new shares, and suspending buybacks solely to build more data centers — **in his eyes that is "gambling on an uncertain return cycle with external capital."** The railroads and utilities he has bought were all like this: a wide moat, but capex eats free cash flow, and shareholder returns ultimately depend on dividends and buybacks — both of which have now stopped.

Conclusion: he would buy, but demand a lower price. **He would probably say, "At USD 280 I could start buying a little; at USD 220 I would want to buy more."**

## Munger: invert the question — what could take it from 280 down to 200?

Munger does not ask "how much can it rise" but "how could it lose." Three triggers: **① A depreciation peak arrives; operating margin falls from 32% to 26% in 2027, and the market reprices it as a heavy-asset utility; ② Cloud growth drops from 103% to below 30% while capex does not fall, and free cash flow stays negative for three consecutive years; ③ AI gives answers directly and users stop clicking links, pushing search revenue growth below 5%.**

These three are entirely different in nature. The first is a math problem—RMB 200 billion of capex depreciated over six years means RMB 33 billion of new cost each year; by 2027 depreciation doubles to above RMB 50 billion. It is almost certain to happen; only the magnitude is uncertain. The second requires demand-side deterioration, and there is no evidence of that now. The third is an industry-level risk; search +12% in Q3 shows it has not happened yet.

**The most dangerous is not the third but the first—because it is "bad news that is certain to happen," and the market, pricing at 21x EV/EBIT, is still using a 32% margin.**

## Li Lu: A bet on civilization-scale infrastructure, with returns measured over five years

What Li Lu looks at is "whether this company sits on the必经 path of a civilization-level trend." Search restructured how humanity acquires knowledge, the cloud restructured how enterprises compute, and Gemini and TPU are restructuring how intelligence is produced. **None of these three is a cyclical opportunity; they are structural shifts.**

He watches two numbers: Gemini App users at 1 billion, the fastest-growing product in Google's history; and the in-house model API processing nearly 32 billion tokens per minute, four times the level of a year ago. **This shows AI is not a concept but something already being used at scale.** Two questions remain: can usage turn into revenue, and can revenue cover the investment.

His answer is usually "yes, but it takes five years, not five quarters." **So he will not care about the 2027 depreciation peak; he cares about whether this company has secured a foothold at the entrance to the next decade.** If it has, today's RMB 20 billion is the Android investment of 2010; if not, it is the Google+ of 2013.

## Risk/reward: 29% downside, 25% upside, expected value +21%

Weighted at 25%/50%/25%, the expected target price is USD 337.5, implying 20.6% upside from 279.84. **On the neutral scenario alone, 25% upside versus 29% downside gives odds of 1 to 0.86 — not attractive.** The appeal lies in the probability distribution: the bear case requires three things to happen at once — "depreciation step-up + cloud deceleration + advertising stall" — while the neutral case only requires "cloud deceleration but still above 50%".

Another angle: of the USD 3.42 trillion market cap, search advertising at 15x EBIT is worth USD 2.2 trillion, and cloud at 2027 revenue of USD 160 billion, a 25% operating margin and 25x EBIT is worth USD 1 trillion — USD 3.2 trillion combined. **The market is paying only for the existing businesses; Gemini, Waymo and external TPU sales are essentially free.** So this is not a question of "expensive or not" but of "whether the freebies turn into negative assets" — Waymo loses USD 8 billion a year (other bets lose 2.05 billion a quarter annualized), and if it keeps losing for five more years, the freebie becomes a liability.

**Actionable conclusion: scale in, do not build a heavy position. Open with half the intended position, add to full below USD 240; if cloud growth falls below 40% in Q1 2027 and the capex guidance is unchanged, halve the position.**

## The four differ on only one real point: timing.

Put the four passages together: the Duan Yongping–Buffett disagreement is over "whether capex counts as part of a good business," and the Munger–Li Lu disagreement is over "whether the depreciation peak matters." **But these two disagreements are essentially the same question: what time scale do you use to do the math.**

Duan Yongping runs a three-year clock: can the cloud business hold margins within three years? If so, buy. Buffett runs five years: can cumulative free cash flow over five years cover this RMB 200 billion? If so, buy. Munger runs two years: will depreciation hit first within two years? If so, hold off. Li Lu runs ten years: will the gateway still be there in ten years? If so, buy.

**The four time scales yield four answers: buy, buy, wait, buy.** That is why the final conclusion is "buy, in tranches"—it accommodates both "buy now" (Duan, Li) and "don't buy it all at once" (Buffett, Munger). **If you can only listen to one person, listen to Buffett: he sees both the moat and the cash flow, and he demands the smallest discount.**

One implicit disagreement is worth spelling out: **the four differ in their judgment of whether management can spend this money well.** Duan Yongping and Li Lu trust Pichai; Buffett and Munger doubt any management's discipline on large-scale capex. That disagreement cannot be settled with data—only time will verify it, which is also why half the position should be held back.
