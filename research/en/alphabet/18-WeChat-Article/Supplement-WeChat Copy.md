## I. First, a set of numbers that will split your mind

Alphabet's 2026Q3 print cut both ways.

The good: revenue of USD 127.58 billion, up 25% YoY and the 13th consecutive quarter of double-digit growth; operating income of USD 40.58 billion, up 30% YoY; operating margin of 32%, up 1 percentage point from a year earlier.

The bad: operating cash flow of USD 42.29 billion against capex of USD 51.36 billion, leaving free cash flow of negative USD 9.07 billion — the second consecutive quarter in negative territory. Over the past 12 months free cash flow totalled just USD 19.7 billion, a 0.6% yield on a USD 3.42 trillion market cap.

The company also did three more things that day: issued USD 28.7 billion of debt, sold 36 million additional shares (net proceeds of USD 12.4 billion), and cut buybacks from USD 11.5 billion a year earlier to 0.

Put the three together and it is not hard to see why the market knocked the stock down 10% after hours.

## 2. But the real bright spot is not revenue — it is cloud

Google Cloud is the most valuable read in this earnings report.

Revenue was USD 30.71 billion, up 103% YoY — it doubled. More important, cloud operating profit was USD 11.12 billion, up 209% YoY, with operating margin at 36.2% versus only 23.7% a year earlier.

Taken together, these three numbers say one thing: cloud has not just grown bigger, it has started making money, and profit is growing twice as fast as revenue.

Cloud is an asset-heavy business. Servers and data centers are bought up front; costs come first, revenue later. Once utilization passes the inflection point, each extra dollar of revenue brings far more profit than before. Alphabet is at that inflection point now.

One more number: cloud backlog was USD 56.1 billion, of which slightly more than 55% will be recognized within 24 months. Demand is backed by contracts.

## III. So what exactly is the market afraid of?

The fear is depreciation — not that the company isn't making money now, but that the money it earns two years from now will have to pay for what it is spending today.

Capex of USD 200 billion a year, depreciated over six years, adds USD 33.3 billion of cost annually. Management says it will "increase significantly" again in 2027. The depreciation peak arrives in 2027 and 2028, when operating margin could fall from 32% to 30%, or to 26% on the most pessimistic view.

The second fear is search. AI now delivers answers directly, and users no longer click links. Search advertising is priced per click, and no clicks means no revenue. Q3 search revenue rose 12% against a consensus of 14% — a 2 percentage point gap, and the first yellow light.

The third fear is debt-funded expansion. Why would a company sitting on USD 265.1 billion in cash and securities still issue bonds and sell new shares? There is only one reasonable explanation: management expects cash to flow out steadily over the next two to three years.

## IV. Why I see this as an opportunity

Because the price already reflects the bad news.

The stock trades at USD 279.84, down 30.5% from its 52-week high of USD 402.62 and just 2.3% above its 52-week low of USD 273.5. Market sentiment is already near an extreme.

On valuation, enterprise value is about USD 3.28 trillion, or 21x 2026E EV/EBIT — for a company growing revenue 25%, with cloud up 103% and a 32% operating margin.

Break it down again. Search advertising is worth USD 2.2 trillion at 15x EBIT; cloud is worth USD 1 trillion on 2027 revenue of USD 160 billion, a 25% margin and 25x EBIT. Together the two total USD 3.2 trillion.

In other words, the market is paying only for the existing search and cloud businesses. Gemini (already 1 billion app users), Waymo and external TPU sales are essentially free.

One piece of good news is widely overlooked: the worst antitrust outcome — a breakup of Chrome or Android — was ruled out in the December 2025 ruling. All that remains is data sharing and a choice screen; the moat has been thinned a little, but it is still there.

## V. But I won't go fully invested, and here's why

Depreciation is certain to arrive; only its magnitude is uncertain.

In 2027, depreciation doubles to above USD 50 billion — 10 percentage points against annualized revenue of USD 500 billion. Scale effects in cloud revenue can absorb part of that, but not all of it.

The more troublesome issue is Waymo. Other Bets lost USD 2.05 billion this quarter, an annualized USD 8 billion or more. If its unit economics do not work over ten years, that is USD 8 billion of cash outflow every year.

My judgment, then: this is a good company, and the price is reasonable — but "reasonable" does not mean "cheap." Overpaying for a good company still loses money.

## Six, how exactly to execute

My rule:

At the current price of USD 279.84, open the first position by buying half the target position. The target position is 8% of the portfolio, so buy 4% first.

If it falls below USD 240, fill to 8%. That equates to roughly 18x EV/EBIT, leaving a buffer for depreciation risk.

If it rises above USD 350, do not add; start evaluating trimming.

No price stop-loss. The stock's one-year range is 273.5 to 402.62, a 47% swing; a mechanical stop would only shake us out and force us to chase back in at a higher level.

Exit on three conditions: cloud revenue growth below 30% for two consecutive quarters with capex not falling; operating margin below 26% for two consecutive quarters; search revenue growth below 8% for two consecutive quarters. Any one triggers a halving.

## Seven, a final blunt note

Over the next year, the most likely outcome for this stock is not a surge but sideways, rangebound trading — depreciation pressure will surface quarter by quarter, and cloud revenue has to prove itself quarter by quarter.

Buying it requires patience; attractive free cash flow is unlikely to materialise for four to six quarters.

If you can't wait, don't buy. That's not the company's problem — it's a mismatch problem.

Conversely, if you can wait and believe that "AI revenue will eventually outrun depreciation," then Google at USD 280 is a level worth buying in tranches.

That's it. The above does not constitute investment advice; I'm just writing out how I think about it myself.
