Industry and competition: four leaders, one meat grinder, one cycle — Alphabet

Part Five: Who on this chain is making money, and who is burning it

Core view

Value in the AI supply chain pools at both ends: chips (Nvidia gross margin above 70%) and monetisation (Google and Meta advertising operating margin around 40%). The middle — the model layer — is the hard part: heavy investment, homogeneous products, losses across almost the entire industry. The big four (Alphabet, Microsoft, Amazon, Meta) all sit on the same capex curve, together spending hundreds of USD billion in 2026. Cycle position: year three of the investment phase, year one of depreciation recognition, and 2027 is the first real test of whether compute is in surplus or shortage.

The divergence among the top four: who has a second curve

All four are investing in AI, but their monetisation paths differ completely. Alphabet has cloud, advertising and external chip sales; Microsoft has cloud and software subscriptions; Amazon has cloud and e-commerce; Meta has advertising only. The gap widens in a demand downturn: advertising is procyclical, cloud is countercyclical (enterprises cutting costs shift more workloads to the cloud), and chip sales are project-based.

Q3 data illustrates the contrast: Alphabet capex USD 51.36 billion, FCF -USD 9.07 billion; Meta capex USD 33.94 billion, FCF -USD 2.12 billion. Meta spends less and generates better cash flow, but its AI monetises only through advertising ROI, with no second curve. Alphabet spends more and has weaker cash flow, but its cloud line is doubling.

That is the difference between spending more to buy a second curve and spending less to defend the cash cow. Neither is right or wrong; it comes down to whether you believe AI demand can persist—if so, buy the one with multiple curves; if not, buy the cash cow.

Model layer: a meat grinder, but you have to stay in it

The model layer is now the least profitable part of the business. OpenAI and Anthropic burn cash to buy share, while open-source models (Llama, Qwen, the DeepSeek family) drive closed-model pricing down towards cost. A call volume of 32 billion tokens per minute sounds staggering, but unit prices are falling fast — generation volume quadrupled while revenue only doubled.

So why does Alphabet stay in it? Because the model is the gatekeeper to the entrance. If Gemini clearly falls behind, users will take their questions elsewhere and search's traffic base will loosen. For Alphabet, then, the model layer is not a profit centre but defensive spending — it can lose money, but it cannot be absent.

That positioning matters: judge Alphabet not on whether Gemini can make money, but on whether Gemini has held the entrance. 1 billion app users is the answer: it has.

Cycle: 2027 is the real test

This AI capex cycle began in 2024, stepped up in 2025 and is running at "USD 200 billion a year" in 2026. The unusual feature: investment has grown at a high rate for three straight years, yet depreciation recognition has only just begun. 2026 financials should therefore all look good, with margins not yet squeezed by depreciation; 2027 is the real test.

Divergence signals have already appeared in the industry: CoreWeave is down more than 70% from its high, Oracle's free cash flow will not turn positive until the second half of fiscal 2028, and Meta's Q3 FCF was -USD 2.12 billion. The market is starting to distinguish "compute backed by cash flow" from "compute backed by financing." Alphabet belongs to the former, but its cash flow is thinning too.

The test for the cycle is simple: watch whether supply or demand grows faster. On the supply side, the big four will add more in 2027; on the demand side, the ROI on enterprise AI budgets has yet to be proven. If "cloud growth slowing, capex not slowing" appears in the first half of 2027, that is the cycle inflection point.

Chinese players and open-source models: model pricing gets pushed down, not cloud pricing

Many read "open-source models catching up with closed-source" as bad news for Alphabet. I think it has to be viewed in parts. What open-source models push down is the price of the model layer (API unit price), not the price of cloud compute. Quite the opposite: the cheaper the model and the larger the call volume, the more compute the cloud sells — 32 billion tokens per minute in Q3, quadrupling in a year, is exactly the volume growth that lower prices bring.

The real pressure is that "the model layer cannot charge." If Gemini's API revenue stays permanently thin-margin, Alphabet can only recover its model investment indirectly, through cloud and advertising. This structural problem applies to all model companies, including OpenAI — which is exactly why OpenAI is building applications, building hardware, and building its own compute.

Chinese players matter mainly in two directions: first, pushing down global model prices (the open-source strategies of the DeepSeek family and the Qwen family); second, capturing cloud and advertising budgets in the Chinese market. Alphabet has almost no business in China, so the second does not affect it; the first affects its model pricing but at the same time expands its compute demand. The net impact is roughly neutral and should not be treated as a major risk.

If computing power becomes oversupplied, who falls first?

The sequence is clear. First to fall is "rental compute": companies like CoreWeave that buy chips with debt and rent them out on long-term contracts. Once utilization drops, interest payments crush them — the stock has already fallen more than 70% from its high. Second comes the second-tier cloud providers: no models, no applications, selling only compute — once a price war starts, there is no gross margin left. Only the third wave is the Big Four: they have their own demand (search, advertising, e-commerce) to absorb compute, and when supply is excessive they can shift compute back to internal use.

Alphabet is the safest positioned because its compute has an internal outlet: Gemini training and inference, AI Overviews in search, and YouTube recommendations are all internal customers. That is its structural advantage over CoreWeave — when supply is excessive, it can choose not to sell.

Conclusion

Three points on the industry conclusion: Among the Big Four, Alphabet is best positioned, with exposure across all four layers; the model layer is defensive spending rather than a profit centre; the cycle sits at "the tail end of the investment phase, the eve of the return phase." Good positioning does not mean going all-in now—timing must be compensated by price.
