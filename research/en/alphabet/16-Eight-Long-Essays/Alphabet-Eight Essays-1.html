Cognitive reset: the map in your hands is ten years old — Alphabet

Part One: Why the market's view of Alphabet is stuck in 2016
Research date 2026/10/30  ·  Price $279.84 (-1.40%)  ·  Market cap About USD 3.42 trillion  ·  EV/EBIT(2026E) Approximately 21x
← Back to the hub

Core view

Most investors still see Alphabet first as an "advertising company": search sells ads, YouTube sells ads, growth comes from ads. That was right in 2016; by 2026 it is half wrong. Of Q3 revenue of USD 127.58 billion, 30.71 billion came from cloud, or 24.1%, and this segment grew 103% YoY with an operating margin of 36.2%. Meanwhile the company spends USD 200 billion a year building infrastructure. Correcting that view starts with admitting: this is not an advertising company plus a few "other bets" — it is an advertising company turning into an infrastructure company.

Old map: an advertising company, asset-light with high cash flow

The old map rested on three core assumptions: revenue comes from advertising, growth comes from user time spent, and capex is low. All three held in 2016—that year capex was under USD 10 billion, free cash flow was over USD 20 billion, and buybacks were the primary form of capital allocation. Viewed through this map, the conclusion is always "a steady cash cow, 20x PE is reasonable."

The problem is that three parts of this map have stopped working. First, cloud's share rose from under 5% to 24.1%, so the revenue mix has changed; second, capex rose from under USD 10 billion to USD 200 billion a year, so the asset base has changed; third, free cash flow fell from over USD 20 billion to USD 19.7 billion over the past 12 months. Though the absolute figure looks similar, against a market cap of USD 3.42 trillion it is a yield of just 0.6%—something that could not have appeared on the old map.

New map: one cash machine plus a water plant under construction

Under the new map, Alphabet comprises two assets. The first is the mature advertising business: Q3 Search RMB 63.35 billion, YouTube RMB 11.21 billion, Network RMB 7.46 billion, for a combined RMB 82.61 billion in revenue, with a Google Services segment operating margin of 40.7%. This is a money printer that can be sustained with almost no incremental capital.

The second is AI infrastructure: Cloud revenue of RMB 30.71 billion, quarterly operating profit of RMB 1.11 billion, plus RMB 200 billion in annual capex. This is a water plant under construction — spend first, collect rent later. Its value hinges not on how much money it makes now, but on whether its unit cost after completion can hold down competitors. The two assets require two valuation methods: discounted cash flow for the money printer, return on capital for the water plant.

The cost of a cognitive reset: valuation multiples to step down a notch

The most counterintuitive part of this reset: the company got stronger, but its valuation multiple should go lower. The strong part (cloud, AI) is capital-intensive, while the asset-light part (search) is slowing. The market awards growth stocks high multiples on the assumption that "growth costs nothing"; once growth costs money, the multiple has to move toward the range for asset-heavy companies.

Specifically: over the past five years Alphabet's EV/EBIT range was 20–30x, the range of the asset-light era. After the shift to asset-heavy, the reasonable range may be 15–22x. At 21x now, it sits right at the top of the new range — which is why I judge it "reasonably valued, not cheap" rather than "cheap."

This conclusion matters because it dictates the action: at 25x it would be a sell; at 15x, a full buy. At 21x, buy half.

Three underappreciated data points show the structure has already shifted

You do not need a complex model to judge whether "the map has failed" — three ratios will do. First, cloud at 24.1% of revenue: a quarter of the top line now comes from asset-heavy businesses, versus under 5% in 2016. Second, capex/revenue at 40.2%: for every 100 of revenue, 40 goes back into the ground, versus around 12% in 2016. Third, free cash flow of USD 19.7 billion over the past 12 months, just 0.6% of the USD 3.42 trillion market cap — versus above 5% in 2016.

All three ratios point the same way, which tells you this is not "one quarter of heavy spending" but a reconstruction of the business model. And that reconstruction is irreversible: once the cloud is built, depreciation must be recognised every year and will not fall simply because demand changes. That is the essence of an asset-heavy business — it shifts the "cycle" from the revenue line to the cost line.

Reading the new map requires one further step: asset-heavy does not mean a bad business. TSMC is asset-heavy and has sustained ROE above 25%; railways are asset-heavy, and Buffett bought them. The difference is "whether capex can build a moat." If Alphabet's USD 200 billion buys only general-purpose servers, it is ordinary asset-heavy; if it buys in-house TPUs and data-centre networking, it deepens the moat. On the Q3 cloud margin of 36.2%, it is the latter.

A testable prediction

A cognitive reset cannot stop at "my view has changed"; it must yield testable predictions. My prediction: if capex peaks in 2027 (say, within RMB 220 billion), free cash flow turns positive in 2028, because although depreciation is high, incremental investment falls. Conversely, if capex is still "rising significantly" in 2027 and FCF remains negative in 2028, then "asset-heavy transformation" is not a transition period but the new normal. The two outcomes map to two valuation approaches, a 30% difference.

The value of this prediction is that it is falsifiable: the Q4 earnings report in February 2027 will provide 2027 capex guidance, and a single number settles it. No need to wait three years.

Conclusion

Cognitive reset in one sentence: no longer "an advertising company plus a few options" but "an advertising cash machine plus a computing-power water plant under construction." The former supports current profit; the latter determines future valuation. The old map says "cheap"; the new map says "fairly valued" — the difference between buying in full and buying half.
