Core view

Condense the conclusions of the previous seven pieces into rules: open a 4% initial position at USD 279.84 (half of the 8% target), fill to full at USD 240, cap at 10% below USD 200, add nothing above USD 350. Exit is not price-driven but thesis-driven: cloud growth below 30% for two consecutive quarters with capex not falling, or operating margin below 26% for two consecutive quarters, or search growth below 8% for two consecutive quarters — any one triggers a halving. Make only one decision per quarter, and make it within 48 hours.

Why "scale in batches" rather than "wait for lower"

Both approaches are reasonable: wait for 240 before buying, or buy half now. I choose the latter because "I don't know whether it will reach 240." It sits just 2.3% above its 52-week low, which shows sentiment is already poor; if Q4 earnings capex guidance comes in below the consensus, the stock could go straight from 280 to 320, and waiting for 240 then would be waiting in vain.

The cost of scaling in is that "if it falls to 200, my cost is 260, not 200." But that cost can be offset by "adding in full at 240" — after adding in full, the cost is averaged below 260. The cost of not scaling in is that "if it doesn't fall, I have no position at all." When the payoff is close to 1:1 and the odds skew upward, getting on board early matters more than waiting for the bottom.

Why exit decisions rest on the thesis, not the price

A price stop-loss fails on this stock: the one-year range is 273.5–402.62, a 47% swing. Set a 15% stop and you get shaken out at 238 — which is exactly the add point. So a price stop is not discipline, it is noise.

A thesis stop works, because a thesis can be falsified by quarterly reports. The three thesis conditions — cloud growth, operating margin, search growth — each has an explicit number in the quarterly report, with no room for interpretation. Trigger, cut by half; no trigger, do nothing. That beats "sell because something feels off" a hundred times over.

The most important rule: if two of the three trigger, exit and re-underwrite. Two triggering at once means "input with no return + core business shrinking" happening together — a fundamental failure of the thesis, not volatility.

One last question: what is the "expected value" of this investment?

Three scenarios: bear case 200 (-28.5%), base case 350 (+25.1%), bull case 450 (+60.8%). Subjective probabilities 25%/50%/25%, weighted target price 337.5, +20.6% versus the current price. That is, with staged position building (cost of about 260), the expected return is about 30%.

This expected value is not high, but its "floor" is bearable: bear case -28.5%, an 8% position implies a -2.3% hit to the portfolio. That is why I am willing to buy, but only 8%.

If the expected value were below 15%, I would not buy — that would mean the price has already eaten the good news. Right now, a 20.6% expected value plus an 8% position cap is a "worth doing but not worth betting on" combination. This sense of scale matters more than any precise valuation model.

First 90 days: how to know you are wrong

In the first 90 days after buying, there are no new earnings reports, only fragmented information. The most common mistake during this period is "using the stock price to validate your judgment"—feeling right when it rises and wrong when it falls. The right approach is to draw up an "observable checklist" for yourself and look only at what is on it.

Four items on the checklist: ① Public cloud customer signings (large deals, renewals, price increases), which say more about demand than the stock price; ② Usage data for AI products (Gemini App rankings, developer activity), which shows whether the entry point has been lost; ③ Capex guidance from competitors (Microsoft, Amazon, Meta)—if they are also ramping up, demand is real and a price war will come; ④ How the bond market prices Alphabet's new debt—widening spreads signal concern about cash flow.

The first decision comes only with the first earnings report after 90 days. Until then, the only thing to do is "nothing"—don't touch the position, don't change the thesis, don't add or trim on a single news item. This discipline sounds simple but is the hardest to practice, because the market offers "reasons to act" every day. Only those who can resist acting are qualified to talk about scaling in.

Where is this report most likely to be wrong?

Three points. ① Depreciation estimate: I estimate RMB 36 billion of incremental depreciation on a blended five-and-a-half-year basis. If the company actually amortizes servers over four years, that rises to RMB 45 billion, and margin pressure is greater than I calculated. ② Sustainability of cloud growth: I set my 2027 neutral assumption at 55%, which may be optimistic — the 103% growth includes a one-off contribution from external TPU sales that I cannot separate out. ③ Sum-of-the-parts valuation: Search at 15x EBIT and cloud at 25x EBIT — both multiples are my own judgment calls, and different assumptions would move the conclusion from "cheap" to "expensive."

Of the three, the first is most likely to be wrong, and the third has the largest impact. I am writing this down so that when I look back in three months I know where I went wrong, rather than finding excuses.

Conclusion

The decision framework in three sentences: buy in tranches (half at 279.84, full at 240), thesis-based stop-loss (three hard conditions, triggering a halving of the position), position cap 8%. Expected return 20.6% (about 30% after tranching), with a tolerable downside. This is the complete execution version of "buy, in tranches."
