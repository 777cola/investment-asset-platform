Business essence: from selling attention to selling compute — Alphabet

Part Two: What Is This Company Actually Selling?

Core view

Alphabet's business is, at its core, "converting human intent into auctionable inventory." Search turns intent into keyword inventory, YouTube turns attention into pre-roll video inventory, and Cloud turns enterprise computing needs into hourly-billed compute inventory. Marginal cost differs entirely across the three: keywords are near zero, video revenue shares are variable, and compute requires building data centers. Understand this and you understand why advertising runs a 40.7% margin, Cloud runs a 36.2% margin, and Other Bets loses money.

First inventory type: intent, at near-zero marginal cost

Search does not sell "impressions"; it sells "intent." A user types "car repair near me," and that intent is auctioned to dozens of advertisers within 0.3 seconds. The cost structure is highly unusual: the marginal cost of serving one more user is near zero, and so is the marginal cost of selling one more ad. Revenue rises 12%, and profit can rise much more.

The moat here is not technology but the "scale + data" loop: more queries make click prediction more accurate, advertiser ROI higher and bids higher, leaving the company with more cash to buy default entry points, which bring more queries. The loop has one weakness: it assumes users will still "type keywords." AI chat shakes that assumption—the one true risk to this business.

The second inventory category is compute, where marginal cost is power and depreciation

Cloud sells "deterministic compute". Customers do not care which chip you use; they care whether it runs, what it costs and whether it is stable. The cost structure of this business: fixed costs are extremely high (data centres, chips), marginal costs very low (power, bandwidth, O&M), so utilisation determines margins. Q3 cloud operating margin was 36.2% versus 23.7% a year earlier — the difference is utilisation.

This is also why cloud vendors must keep expanding capacity: without expansion, customers leave because of insufficient capacity; with expansion, depreciation arrives first and revenue later. In an upcycle this rhythm is a virtuous cycle (revenue grows faster than depreciation); in a downcycle it turns vicious (depreciation keeps accruing, revenue does not grow). Alphabet is now in the early stage of a virtuous cycle — cloud revenue +103%, and depreciation has not yet scaled up. But the cycle will reverse; it is only a matter of time.

Part three: why it must build its own chips

If chips are the largest line item in a cloud cost structure, and chips can only be bought from Nvidia at gross margins above 70%, cloud providers are essentially working for Nvidia. The point of TPU is not "better performance" but "setting your own cost." That is Alphabet's biggest structural advantage over Microsoft and Meta — both are also building their own chips, but started later and at smaller scale.

The ecosystem bounds this advantage. CUDA has over a decade of developer accumulation behind it; the TPU software stack cannot match that. External TPU sales can therefore only go to "large customers willing to rewrite code" and cannot fully replace Nvidia. It also explains why, within doubled cloud revenue, external TPU sales are an "important contribution" yet the company does not disclose specific figures — they are not yet large enough to break out separately.

Cautionary Tale: Why Waymo Is Really in the Business of "Selling Options"

Other Bets generated Q3 revenue of USD 372 million and an operating loss of USD 2.047 billion — an annualised loss above USD 8 billion, overwhelmingly from Waymo. The problem here is not technology but unit economics that have yet to work: high cost per vehicle, high cost of safety drivers, asset-heavy city expansion, and pricing constrained by ride-hailing competition.

Contrast the business attributes of cloud. Cloud sells "replicable compute," where one more customer adds almost no cost; Waymo sells "non-replicable miles," where one more city requires another fleet of cars and another operations team. That is why cloud went from loss-making to a 36.2% margin in three years, while Waymo is still losing money after ten years.

I should therefore carry Waymo at zero in the valuation, or even as a negative asset. If it one day makes the unit economics work, that is a free option; if it fails to do so for ten years, that is USD 8 billion of cash outflow a year — equivalent to burning 18% of cloud profit. This is not a fatal problem (Alphabet's annual operating profit is USD 160 billion), but it explains why "Other Bets" should not appear in the valuation model.

Advertising's ceiling is not traffic

Most attempts to size the ceiling for the advertising business use "global ad market size × share." That approach omits a term: ad revenue = traffic × price per impression × number of monetization formats. Over the past decade of Alphabet's growth, traffic contributed only a small part; the bulk came from price increases and format additions, from text links to shopping units to video to AI-generated creative.

Two implications follow. First, search traffic peaking does not mean ad revenue peaking, so long as price per impression is still rising. Second, AI is both a threat and an opportunity: it may reduce clicks (threat), and it may raise conversion rates and thus lift price per impression (opportunity). Q3 search +12% shows the latter currently has the upper hand.

Conclusion

The business in three sentences: selling intent is the best business (marginal cost near zero), selling compute is second best (utilisation determines profit), selling options is the worst (costs with no revenue). Alphabet does all three, so its valuation must likewise be built in three parts — the subject of the next piece.
