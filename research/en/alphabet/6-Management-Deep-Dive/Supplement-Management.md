## Pichai's Three Years: From Fighting Fires to Regaining the Initiative

When ChatGPT launched in 2023, the prevailing view was that Google had fallen behind in AI—rushed model releases, internal team splits, and search questioned as ripe for disruption. **Three years later, the Q3 numbers are the answer: cloud revenue USD 30.71 billion, up 103% year on year, cloud operating margin 36.2%, 1 billion Gemini App users, and 32 billion tokens per minute through the model API.**

Three things were done right: merging Brain and DeepMind, turning TPU from an internal tool into an external product, and embedding Gemini into search and Workspace rather than launching a separate entry point. **The third matters most—rather than build an "AI search" to compete with search, it made search itself into AI search.** That reflects organizational capability, and it is why search still grew 12% in Q3.

## Capital allocation: this management team made a decision unseen in a decade

Alphabet's capital allocation philosophy over the past decade was "return cash to shareholders": hundreds of billions of dollars in cumulative buybacks, dividends initiated in 2024, and capex held within 15% of revenue. **In 2026 that philosophy has been overturned: capex at 40% of revenue, buybacks at zero, plus debt issuance and equity issuance.**

The evidence for this decision is hard: cloud revenue doubled, cloud margin rose from 23.7% to 36.2%, backlog of USD 56.1 billion. **The evidence against is equally hard: free cash flow of -USD 9.07 billion, only USD 19.7 billion over the past 12 months, and depreciation doubling to over USD 50 billion in 2027.** This is a classic heavy-asset expansion decision—if demand materializes, returns are extremely high; if demand stalls, depreciation becomes a noose.

What I focus on more is the detail of "doing three things at once": issuing debt, issuing equity, halting buybacks. If it were only a matter of heavy capex, the USD 265.1 billion of cash on the balance sheet would suffice; the need for external financing shows management expects cash burn to persist for years. **This is an implicit guide to 2027–2028 cash flow, more credible than any public statement.**

## Integrity and governance: fines are a cost, not a scandal

Alphabet has a long regulatory record, but two categories must be distinguished. **The first is "business conduct violations," mainly EU antitrust fines**: EUR 2.95 billion over ad tech in September 2025, EUR 890 million under the DMA in July 2026, EUR 460 million over self-preferencing in September 2026, plus the European Court of Justice's July 2026 rejection of the Android fine appeal, upholding EUR 4.1 billion. **These are operating costs, not integrity issues—the company concealed nothing and fabricated nothing; the rules themselves are changing.**

The second category is governance structure. Dual-class shares let the founders control about 51% of voting power with roughly 11% of economic interest, leaving outside shareholders no veto over major decisions (such as the RMB 200 billion in capital expenditure). **This is a known structure that existed at the time of listing, not a new risk, but it means "voting with your feet" is the only check.**The 2024 layoffs and the 2025 organizational flattening brought internal controversy, but no financial integrity incident emerged.

## Succession: not a problem, but a hidden risk

Pichai was born in 1968 and is now around 58, so there is no near-term succession pressure. The real risk lies in the "number two" slot: **Porat moved from CFO to President and Chief Investment Officer, and Ashkenazi only became CFO in 2024 — together they have less than five years of tenure at Alphabet.** If Pichai left, the board would either have to look externally or hand over to someone unfamiliar with the AI capex cycle.

The other risk is the founders. Page and Brin hold 51% of voting rights but do not run day-to-day operations; this "remote-control" structure works fine when the company is doing well, but becomes a decision-making bottleneck when a rapid pivot is needed. **I give management 8 points; of the 2 points deducted, half is for the pace of capital allocation and half for the opacity of this governance structure.**

## Historical capital allocation track record: 2015–2024 a plus

Before judging the 2026 aggression, look at the past decade's record. **From 2015 to 2024, Alphabet repurchased hundreds of billions of dollars of stock, its annualized share price return beat the S&P 500, and capex stayed within 15% of revenue throughout, with free cash flow consistently positive.** That is a clean capital allocation scorecard—no large goodwill impairments, no failed mega-acquisitions (aside from the historical Motorola deal).

So the 2026 pivot cannot simply be dismissed as "management getting carried away." **The more reasonable explanation: they see an opportunity window unseen in the past decade (enterprise AI compute demand) and believe it lasts only two to three years.** To test whether that explanation holds, one thing suffices: whether capex peaks in 2027. If it peaks, this is a disciplined sprint; if not, it is out of control.
