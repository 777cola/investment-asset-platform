Business analysis (Duan Yongping's perspective) — Alphabet

What makes this business good, what makes it bad, and will it still be around in ten years?

Core view

This is one of the best businesses I can understand, but it is turning from a "money printer" into a "water utility under repair." Search advertising remains a zero-marginal-cost, self-reinforcing franchise; cloud has finally proven it can make money (36.2% operating margin); YouTube is the world's second-largest pool of attention. The problem is singular: USD 200 billion of annual capex has turned this into an asset-heavy business. I am not against spending money; I am against spending it without regard to returns.

What makes this a good business: users pay nothing, while advertisers compete to pay

Search has the cleanest business model I have ever seen: users pay nothing, advertisers pay for "intent", and intent is what users type in themselves. More users means more data, more accurate ads and more advertisers willing to pay—a loop that strengthens itself without requiring additional investment. Q3 Search and other revenue was USD 63.35 billion, up 12% YoY. Growing 12% on an annualised base of over USD 600 billion is the power of that loop.

YouTube runs the same logic in another form: creators produce the content, the platform handles only distribution and ad sales, and content cost is revenue sharing rather than upfront payment. Q3 revenue was USD 11.21 billion, +9.2%. Growth is not fast, but the margin is extremely high. Together the two total USD 74.56 billion, 58% of total revenue, and are the real cash cows.

Where the business went wrong: growth became increasingly capital-intensive

What concerns me most is not profit but whether growth requires spending money. Apple's growth costs almost nothing, Moutai's expansion costs a little, and Alphabet's growth now costs USD 200 billion a year. Q3 capex was USD 51.36 billion, up 114% YoY, at 40.2% of revenue. That ratio means 40 of every 100 of revenue earned goes to building data centres and buying chips.

Cloud is a good business, I admit: USD 30.71 billion in revenue, USD 11.12 billion in operating profit and a 36.2% margin, up from 23.7% last year, show the scale effect is real. But cloud is a heavy-asset business; its moat is capex, not brand. The rule of heavy-asset businesses: you make money when demand is good, and depreciation crushes you when demand is poor. I don't like this kind of business, but I admit it is very profitable when demand is good.

The entry point remains; the form will change. Will it still be here in ten years?

I have no doubt people will still search in ten years. What I doubt is whether the "search box" will still be there. Young people already ask AI directly rather than scroll through ten blue links. Alphabet has Gemini, 1 billion app users and 32 billion tokens of query volume per minute; it is disrupting itself, which beats being disrupted by someone else.

My judgment, then: in ten years the company will still be here, and in all likelihood bigger. But its margin structure will change, from an asset-light 40% margin to an asset-heavy 30% margin. That argues for a notch of de-rating in the P/E multiple it deserves, not a re-rating. The market currently gives it 21x EV/EBIT, which I consider fair, not cheap.

At what price to buy: this is not a "must-own" business

My principle: a good business, a good price, and something I understand — all three are required. Alphabet is a good business and I understand it; the price is reasonable rather than cheap. So I won't buy all at once; I'll scale in. Buy half at USD 280, buy the other half if it falls below 240, and if it rises I won't chase it.

What if it never falls? Then I don't buy. The market offers opportunities every year, and I don't need to catch every one. I won't touch a business I don't understand, and I won't force it if I understand the business but the price doesn't fit — "missing out" is not a mistake; "buying wrong" is.

Conclusion

A good business getting heavier, at a fair but not cheap price. Conclusion: buy in tranches, half position first, add below USD 240. The company will still be here in ten years, but the multiple should step down a notch—the most important lesson this business has taught me.
