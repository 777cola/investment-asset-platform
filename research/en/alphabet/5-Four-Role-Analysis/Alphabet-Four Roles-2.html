Financial analysis (Buffett's perspective) — Alphabet

The income statement looks good, but the cash flow statement is jarring
Research date 2026/10/30  ·  Price $279.84 (-1.40%)  ·  Market cap About USD 3.42 trillion  ·  EV/EBIT(2026E) Approximately 21x

Core view

The income statement is among the best I have seen from a large-cap tech name in years: revenue +25%, operating profit +30%, a 32% operating margin that expanded 1pp even as capex doubled. The cash flow statement stopped me cold, though: operating cash flow RMB 42.29 billion, capex RMB 51.36 billion, free cash flow -RMB 9.07 billion—negative for a second consecutive quarter. A company can run without free cash flow for a while, but it cannot issue debt, launch an equity offering and halt buybacks all at once while free cash flow is negative; doing all three shows the thirst for cash is real.

Income statement: too good to be true for a company investing RMB 200 billion

Revenue RMB 127.58 billion, +25%; operating profit RMB 40.58 billion, +30%; operating margin 32%. What makes this combination rare is that depreciation has not yet scaled up. Capex rose from about RMB 50 billion in 2024 to RMB 200 billion in 2026, but depreciation is recognised with a lag and peaks in 2027–2028. The current 32% margin is therefore a point-in-time figure reflecting investment already made but cost not yet recognised.

Net profit RMB 26.17 billion, -25% — at a glance this is accounting noise: fair value on equity securities swung from +RMB 8.6 billion last year to -RMB 8.7 billion this year, a RMB 17.3 billion round trip. Excluding it, EPS is USD 2.67, higher than last year. I never look at gains and losses of this kind; they do not affect how much this company can earn next year.

Cash flow statement: doing all three at once is the problem

FCF of -RMB 9.07 billion on its own can be explained as an "investment phase." Put three things together, however, and the picture changes: RMB 28.7 billion in debt issuance, 36 million shares issued for net proceeds of RMB 12.4 billion, and buybacks cut from RMB 11.5 billion to 0. A company with USD 140 billion in net cash that truly believed this opportunity offered an extremely high return should fund it from its balance sheet rather than issue debt and equity—both of which dilute existing shareholders' future returns.

Of course, the reverse also holds: tapping the rate window to raise financing while keeping cash on the balance sheet as a buffer against uncertainty is a sign of financial discipline. But the dividend is still being paid (USD 0.21 per share quarterly), which says the company believes its cash flow is fine—the two are contradictory. Either cash flow is fine, in which case buybacks should continue, or cash flow is a problem, in which case the dividend should stop. Doing both halfway, I don't like.

Balance sheet: RMB 125.1 billion of debt is a signal, not a risk

Long-term debt of USD 125.1 billion against USD 265.1 billion in cash and securities leaves net cash of USD 140 billion. Interest coverage exceeds 40x (operating profit of USD 40.58 billion against annualised interest of roughly USD 3.5 billion, on a rough calculation). On debt service, this is not a risk; the AA+ rating from S&P is justified.

It is, however, a signal. Over the past decade Alphabet barely borrowed long-term debt because it did not need to; borrowing now shows capex has outgrown what operating cash flow can cover. I have seen this shift before—railroads, utilities and telecoms all went through it. The shift itself is not wrong; the mistake is still valuing the company as an asset-light business while it is underway.

Conclusion

The income statement deserves a 9, the cash flow statement only a 5, the balance sheet a 7. Conclusion: the moat justifies a premium, but cash flow quality demands a discount. I would be more comfortable 15% lower; at the current price I would buy, but only half a position.
