## Q: Is this a good business?

Fine. But I need to be clear about which kind of "good" this is. The best business is one that makes money without trying too hard — Moutai, for example, or Apple's ecosystem. Alphabet's search business belongs in this category: users type in their own keywords, advertisers bid against each other, and the company barely has to lift a finger for the money to flow in. Q3 Search & other came in at USD 63.35 billion, up 12% YoY; growing 12% on an annualised base of over USD 600 billion is what "without trying too hard" means.

But it is no longer purely that kind of business. Q3 capex was USD 51.36 billion, or 40.2% of revenue, with full-year capex of USD 195–205 billion. A business that must plough 40% of revenue back in just to sustain growth is not entirely a "without trying too hard" business — it is a "must keep trying" business. The valuation gap between these two kinds of business is wide.

## Q: Will this company still be around in ten years?

It will still be here, and probably bigger than it is now — that part does not worry me much. A decade from now people will still look for information, still watch video, and enterprises will still use the cloud. Those three things will not disappear. What worries me is the form: will the search box still exist? If in ten years everyone talks to AI instead of typing keywords and clicking links, how do you sell ads?

Alphabet is itself betting on this shift — the Gemini App has 1 billion users, and the model API handles 32 billion tokens per minute, four times last year's level. It is disrupting itself, which is far better than being disrupted by someone else. But self-disruption has a cost: the new form (AI answers) may monetise less efficiently than the old form (ten blue links plus ads), and nobody knows how big that gap is.

So the answer is: the company survives, the business survives, but the margin steps down. That is why I say its valuation multiple should be marked down a notch, not up.

## Q: Can you understand it?

I understand the business: how ads are sold, how cloud bills customers, how subscriptions renew — all of that I can explain clearly. What I cannot understand is the "optionality" — how much Gemini is worth, how much Waymo is worth, how large TPU external sales can become. None of the three has a standalone P&L today; there is only investment, no revenue.

My approach: assign zero value to the parts I cannot understand. Search at 15x EBIT is worth RMB 2.2 trillion; cloud at 25x 2027 EBIT is worth RMB 1 trillion — RMB 3.2 trillion together, roughly in line with the current market cap of RMB 3.42 trillion. In other words, the market is assigning zero too. That makes Gemini, Waymo and TPU free options — and with something free, you do not mind a loss, while a gain is a windfall.

But if Waymo loses RMB 8 billion a year (other bets lose RMB 2.05 billion a quarter, annualised), five years of losses would total RMB 40 billion. A free option can still be a negative asset, and that has to be acknowledged.

## Q: At what price to buy?

At USD 279.84 you can buy half. At 21x 2026E EV/EBIT against 25% revenue growth, a 36.2% cloud margin and a 32% operating margin, that combination is reasonably cheap for US large-cap tech. Not outright cheap, though: the denominator (2027 EBIT) still carries depreciation as a variable.

My rule is simple: buy half at a fair price, buy in full at a cheap price, don't buy when it's expensive. 279.84 is fair, 240 is cheap (about 18x EV/EBIT), and above 350 is expensive (about 25x 2027E EPS). So buy half now, buy in full if it falls to 240, and don't chase it up to 350.

Some will ask: what if it never falls to 240? Then I hold only half a position, and no regrets — the market offers opportunities every year, and I don't need to catch every one. Buying too little doesn't lose money; buying wrong does.

## Q: What is the biggest fear?

The biggest fear is not antitrust, nor Microsoft or OpenAI. It is "spending RMB 200 billion to turn a light-asset business into a heavy-asset business, and then having the market price it as a heavy-asset business." That is already happening: Q3 free cash flow of -RMB 9.07 billion, RMB 28.7 billion in bond issuance, 36 million shares issued, and buybacks back to zero.

The specific fear runs like this: in 2027, depreciation rises from RMB 25 billion to over RMB 50 billion, operating margin falls from 32% to 26%, and cloud growth drops from 103% to 30%. If both land at once, the market prices it at 15x instead of 21x, and the stock goes from 280 to 200. This is not alarmism; it is arithmetic.

Conversely, if cloud growth is still 60% in 2027, operating margin holds at 30%, and free cash flow turns positive, the market prices it at 25x, and the stock reaches 350. So the stock currently sits at "50/50 between two outcomes", not "a certain good opportunity." That is why we only bought half.

## Q: What do you make of the RMB 200 billion capex?

I am not against spending money; I am against spending it without regard to return. The test is simple: can this money be earned back in three years? The cloud business answers yes — operating margin rose from 23.7% to 36.2% and operating profit grew 209% YoY, evidence that the compute it bought is making money. That is good evidence, and I accept it.

But one thing makes me uncomfortable: why would a company with RMB 265.1 billion in cash issue RMB 28.7 billion of debt and 36 million new shares? If it truly believed the return was extremely high, it should use the cash on its balance sheet, or even borrow more to buy back stock. It did neither; it chose "raise financing + halt buybacks," which shows management itself is controlling risk. If management is controlling risk, why should I be fully invested?

So I follow management's rhythm: they invest in batches, I buy in batches. They will "significantly increase" capital expenditure in 2027, so I will wait for the 2027 data before deciding whether to add. That is not hesitation; that is being on the same frequency.
