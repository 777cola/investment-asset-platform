## Key person: a one-man company, or a company built on a system

Lei Jun's grip on Xiaomi is unusual: a 24% economic stake, combined with the 10-vote structure of Class A shares, gives him a majority at the shareholders' meeting. The upside is decision-making speed — the car was announced in March 2021 and the first vehicle delivered in March 2024, with no strategic wavering in between. The downside is the absence of checks and balances, which leaves the correctness of major bets resting entirely on one person's judgment.

Xiaomi, however, is not a one-man company. Zeng Xuezhong runs the phone business, Lu Weibing the car business, and IoT and the ecosystem chain sit under an independent divisional structure; Lei Jun concentrates more on strategy and external affairs. On the Q3 2026 earnings call, Lu Weibing covered the car business and Lin Shiwei the financials, while Lei Jun gave only the opening remarks — a division of labour that shows the organisation can already run on its own.

The real issue is age and succession. Lei Jun was born in 1969 and is 57 in 2026; Lu Weibing was born in 1975 and is 51. If Lei Jun retires at 65, the handover window falls in 2034, when Lu Weibing will be 59. Time is not tight, but the company has never publicly discussed succession arrangements — a governance gap.

## Capital allocation: where the money goes reveals more about management than how much it earns

Three positives. First, share-count discipline: since its 2018 listing the share count has grown just 3% over five years (the HKD 42.5 billion placement in March 2025 was the main driver) and fell on a net basis after HKD 20 billion of buybacks in 2026 — restrained by the standards of Hong Kong-listed hardware companies. Second, leverage discipline: almost no interest-bearing debt, net cash on the balance sheet and interest coverage well above 10x. Third, R&D discipline: over RMB 9 billion a quarter, up double digits YoY, with cumulative Xuanjie O1 shipments above one million units — technical assets bought with real money.

Two negatives. First, zero dividend: eight years since listing without a single cash distribution, leaving buybacks as the only route to shareholder returns. Second, opaque car-making investment: the 2021 announcement pledged "USD 10 billion over 10 years", yet annual actual spending, per-vehicle cost structure and plant capex have never been broken out in quarterly reports. Investors can therefore only look at segment gross margin and deliveries and guess at per-vehicle economics — which already produced a 140% discrepancy in definition when reconciling the m03 data.

How to judge it? On results alone, five years of car-making to 108,800 units (10.88) a quarter and a segment turned profitable mean the USD 10 billion was well spent. On process, a listed company spending five years and hundreds of billions on a new business without disclosing the spending detail is a governance discount. Score 7; all 3 points deducted are for transparency.

## Integrity and governance: one controversy, two external shocks

The only public governance controversy came before the 2018 listing, when the board granted Lei Jun a 2% equity incentive worth about RMB 9.9 billion at the then-current valuation; media framed it as "paying himself a bonus before the IPO." The company later explained that the award was part of pre-IPO long-term incentives tied to the IPO valuation, and that Lei Jun had committed to directing part of the proceeds to philanthropy. The issue was one of appearance, not integrity: procedurally compliant, poorly received.

Two external shocks better illustrate the governance response capability. In 2022, India's Enforcement Directorate froze about RMB 4.8 billion of Xiaomi's local assets; the company responded with litigation plus an accelerated shift to Southeast Asia and Europe, with no supply-chain rupture. In 2024, complications over auto qualifications and factory approvals were resolved through both acquisitions and self-built facilities. Both were handled cleanly, showing that the crisis-response mechanism works.

One more soft indicator: in 2026Q3, Lu Weibing said publicly, "This is the toughest quarter of my career." Executives at Chinese hardware companies rarely say this — most would frame -68% as "proactive adjustment" or "one-off factors." A willingness to admit difficulty makes next quarter's guidance more credible. This is a plus.

## Successor: in place, but not officially announced

Lu Weibing's trajectory is clear: he joined in 2019 to run Redmi and built it into a standalone brand line, was promoted to group president in 2022 with responsibility for phones and ecosystem, and in 2024 added general manager of the auto division, taking autos from 0 to 10.88 thousand units in a single quarter.This is the textbook heir-apparent path—run one business first, then the full line, and finally the hardest piece.

The risk is that he has never done anything "from 0 to 1." Redmi was a brand spin-off of an existing business, and autos were built with Lei Jun's strategic and resource backing.If Lei Jun retired tomorrow, whether Lu Weibing could decide alone "what to do in the next decade" is untested.

Near term, this does not affect the investment case—Lei Jun is 57, with at least a 5–8 year window; long term, it is one factor behind a valuation discount. Compare Apple: Cook's succession had a three-year transition, and Jobs stepped down as CEO two and a half years in advance.Xiaomi still has no such arrangement, and that is the most important gap to close in governance.
