## Key person: a one-man company, or a company built on a system

Lei Jun's control over Xiaomi is rare: 24% economic interest plus the 10-vote design of Class A shares gives him a majority of votes at the shareholders' meeting. The upside is extremely fast decision-making — the car was announced in March 2021 and the first vehicle delivered in March 2024, with no strategic wavering in between; the downside is the absence of checks and balances, leaving the correctness of major bets entirely dependent on one person's judgment.

But Xiaomi is not "one man's company." The phone business is run by Zeng Xuezhong, the car business by Lu Weibing, and IoT and the ecosystem chain have an independent divisional structure; Lei Jun focuses more on strategy and external affairs. On the Q3 2026 earnings call, Lu Weibing spoke on the car business and Lin Shiwei on the financials, while Lei Jun only gave the opening remarks — a division of labor that shows the organization can already run on its own.

The real issue is age and succession. Lei Jun was born in 1969 and is 57 in 2026; Lu Weibing was born in 1975 and is 51. If Lei Jun retires at 65, the handover window falls in 2034, when Lu Weibing will be 59. Time is not tight, but the company has never publicly discussed succession arrangements — a gap in governance.

## Capital allocation: where the money goes reveals more about management than how much it earns

Three positive marks. First, share-count discipline: in the five years since its 2018 listing, the share count has expanded just 3% (the HKD 42.5 billion placement in March 2025 was the main driver), and it fell net after HKD 20 billion of buybacks in 2026 — restrained by the standards of Hong Kong-listed hardware companies. Second, leverage discipline: almost no interest-bearing debt, net cash on the balance sheet, interest coverage well above 10x. Third, R&D discipline: over RMB 9 billion a quarter, up double digits year on year, with cumulative Xuanjie O1 shipments above one million units — technical assets bought with real money.

Two negative marks. First, zero dividend: eight years since listing without a single cash distribution, leaving buybacks as the only route to shareholder returns. Second, opaque car-making investment: the 2021 announcement said "USD 10 billion over 10 years", but annual actual spending, per-vehicle cost structure and plant capex have never been broken out in quarterly reports. The result is that investors can only look at segment gross margin and deliveries and guess at per-vehicle economics — which already produced a 140% discrepancy in definition when reconciling the m03 data.

How to judge it? On results alone: five years of car-making to 108,800 units (10.88) a quarter and a segment turned profitable means the USD 10 billion was well spent. On process: a listed company spending five years and hundreds of billions on a new business without disclosing the spending detail is a governance discount. Score 7; all 3 points deducted are for transparency.

## Integrity and governance: one controversy, two external shocks

The only public governance controversy occurred before the 2018 listing: the board granted Lei Jun a 2% equity incentive (about RMB 9.9 billion at the then-current valuation), which media framed as "paying himself a bonus before the IPO." The company later explained that this was part of pre-IPO long-term incentives, tied to the IPO valuation, and that Lei Jun committed to directing part of the proceeds to philanthropy. The nature of the matter was "procedurally compliant but poor in appearance," not an integrity issue.

Two external shocks better illustrate the governance response capability. In 2022, India's Enforcement Directorate froze about RMB 4.8 billion of Xiaomi's local assets; the company responded with litigation plus an accelerated shift to Southeast Asia and Europe, with no supply-chain rupture. In 2024, complications over auto qualifications and factory approvals were resolved through both acquisitions and self-built facilities. Both were handled cleanly, showing that the crisis-response mechanism is effective.

One more soft indicator: in the third quarter of 2026, Lu Weibing said publicly, "This is the toughest quarter of my career." Executives at Chinese hardware companies rarely say this — most would frame -68% as "proactive adjustment" or "one-off factors." Willingness to admit difficulty means next quarter's guidance is more credible. This is a plus.

## Successor: in place, but not officially announced

Lu Weibing's path is clear: he joined in 2019 to run Redmi and built it into a standalone brand line; in 2022 he was promoted to group president, taking over phones and ecosystem; in 2024 he added general manager of the auto division, taking autos from 0 to 10.88 thousand units in a single quarter.This is the standard "heir apparent" path—run one business first, then the full line, and finally the hardest piece.

The risk is that he has never done anything "from 0 to 1." Redmi was a brand spin-off of an existing business, and autos were built with Lei Jun's strategic and resource backing.If Lei Jun retired tomorrow, whether Lu Weibing could decide alone "what to do in the next decade" is untested.

Near term, this does not affect the investment case—Lei Jun is 57, with at least a 5–8 year window; long term, it is one factor behind a valuation discount. Compare Apple: Cook's succession had a three-year transition, and Jobs stepped down as CEO two and a half years in advance.Xiaomi still has no such arrangement, and that is the most important gap to close in governance.
