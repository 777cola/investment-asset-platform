## Duan Yongping: Decent companies, hard businesses

Duan Yongping would probably start with praise. Lei Jun is serious about products, honest with users and extremely efficient in channels—in Duan Yongping's vocabulary, these three amount to "benben" (integrity). He would also concede that the car business has succeeded: from zero to 108,800 units in a single quarter within five years, and profitable in 2026Q3. That is not luck.

Then he would talk about the nature of the business. Hardware is a business where "the more you sell, the more cost control is tested"—you earn money from efficiency, not from pricing power. A blended gross margin of 20.2% on phones spread across each unit means a RMB 2,000 phone earns less than RMB 200 in gross profit; one memory price hike wipes out a quarter's profit. He can understand this kind of business, but he would not give it a high multiple.

He would also ask one question: will this company still be here in ten years? Most likely yes—phones are top three globally, IoT is number one globally, and the car has already passed the life-or-death line. But "still here" and "worth buying" are two different things. Duan Yongping bought Apple because Apple can sell the iPhone for USD 1,000 and people still line up; what Xiaomi is doing in 2026 is the opposite—cutting the car from RMB 260,000 to RMB 210,000 to trade price for volume.

If he had to name a price, his line would roughly be "wait for a day when you don't have to calculate": when the stock falls to the point where nobody wants to discuss Xiaomi cars, or when phone gross margin recovers on its own and you no longer have to guess the memory cycle. By his habit, this is called "rather miss it than get it wrong."

## Buffett: gross margin tells you everything; capital allocation tells you the other half

Buffett applies a single test to any manufacturer: can it raise prices without losing customers. Xiaomi's phones cannot — memory prices rose 60%–70% in 2026 and the company chose to absorb the cost, compressing gross margin from 22.9% to 20.2%, because it knows a price hike would cost it share. Nor can its cars — deliveries flat, revenue -19%, effectively trading price for market.

Second, he would look at capital allocation. The good: HKD 20 billion of buybacks in 2026, 2.9% of market cap, share count up just 3% over five years, no reckless issuance; interest-bearing debt minimal, net cash on the balance sheet. The bad: eight years listed without a single cash dividend; no disclosure of cumulative investment in the car business. He likes managements that return cash to shareholders; Xiaomi has done only half the job.

Third is the circle of competence. Buffett would say he cannot understand three things: how long the memory cycle runs, where China's car price war ends, and the organizational complexity of one company doing phones, appliances, cars and chips at once. If he cannot understand it, he will not bid — that is his discipline, and the reason he has missed many tech stocks.

Forcing a number out of his framework: a manufacturer with a 3.6% net margin, 7.5% ROE and continuous heavy reinvestment deserves 12–15x. On 2026 adjusted net profit of RMB 20 billion, that implies a market cap of RMB 240–300 billion and a share price of HKD 10.3–12.9. More than half below the current price — he would not buy, but he would not call it a bad company either.

## Munger: Invert—how could this company lose money?

Munger would invert the question: how did Xiaomi go from earning RMB 11.3 billion to earning RMB 4.884 billion? The answer sits in three lines of the P&L—memory prices rising upstream, car prices falling downstream, and rigid opex in between. All three hit at once, and the operating leverage of a thin-margin business turned the squeeze into a 2.4x collapse in profit.

He would then raise a fact others dislike hearing: neither of Xiaomi's two main lines is in its own hands. Phone costs are set by Samsung, SK hynix, YMTC and CXMT; car prices are set by BYD and Huawei. For a company with pricing power on neither front, margin is simply the residual left after being squeezed at both ends.

On "the car business's first quarterly profit," Munger would press: is that scale, or cost normalisation? If the latter, opening 50 more stores next quarter puts it straight back in the red. He is innately sceptical of "one-off" items—"If you need an explanation to prove you made money, you probably didn't."

His conclusion might be the harshest line of all: this is not a company that will die, but one that will "earn hard money" for a long time. Companies that earn hard money can be bought—but only at the price where "it's so hard that nobody wants it." At 32x today, that is clearly not that price.

## Li Lu: a case study in China's manufacturing upgrade, though not the best one

Li Lu takes a longer view. What Xiaomi represents is a capability embedded in Chinese manufacturing: integrating a complex supply chain into consumer products and using efficiency and scale to drive prices down. It travels from phones to IoT to cars — building a 100,000-unit-class auto business in five years is proof.

He would also see the ceiling on this trajectory. Most winners of China's manufacturing upgrade stop at the "efficiency leadership" layer and struggle to reach "standard setting" — Apple defines the smartphone, TSMC defines the process node, Xiaomi defines "the best specs at the same price." The long-term margin gap between these two positions is three to five times.

On auto exports he would be positive but tempered: entering Europe in 2027 is the right call (no one wins the domestic price war), but compliance costs, channel costs and brand recognition in Europe all start from zero. Judging by BYD's pace in Europe, the first two years are essentially an investment phase. "Going overseas is a necessary move, not a source of profit."

His valuation approach would be conservative: RMB 25 billion in adjusted net profit for 2027 and a 20x multiple as the midpoint, implying around HKD 21. His approach is to "wait until the price enters this range, then spend two years building the position" — more willing to wait than Duan Yongping, and more willing than Buffett to give growth some weight.

## Three-scenario valuation: payoff ratio 1 : 1.3, win rate below half

Weighting the three scenarios gives an expected value of HKD 23.7 — bear case HKD 12.2 (25% probability), base case HKD 23.2 (50%), bull case HKD 36.2 (25%) — 11% below the current price of HKD 26.60. That implies 36% upside against 13% downside to the base case, or 54% to the bear case: odds of roughly 1 : 1.3, a combination of "poor odds and a low hit rate."

The base case rests on just two key assumptions: memory prices fall back in the second half of 2027 and handset gross margin returns to 9%–10%; and auto deliveries reach 480,000 units a year with the segment staying marginally profitable. Neither is aggressive. Memory is a cyclical product and after two years of gains it must eventually pause; 480,000 vehicles equals roughly 40,000 a month, more conservative than the ramp implied by Pengcheng's 70,000 locked orders.

The bull case requires three things to hold at once: memory peaks early, Pengcheng delivers more than 25,000 units a month, and Europe launches in the second half of 2027. If any one fails, the stock falls back to the base case. The bear case needs only one thing: memory rises for another year.

The odds are therefore "asymmetrically bad": good news requires three things to happen at once, bad news only one. That is why all four of them choose to wait — not because they are bearish, but because they do not want to pay a symmetric price for asymmetric risk.
