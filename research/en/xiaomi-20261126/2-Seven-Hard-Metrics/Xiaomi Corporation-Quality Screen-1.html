Core thesis

Five of seven hard criteria pass, one is in doubt and one fails. The failure matters most: 2026Q3 net margin of 3.6%, below the 5% threshold. In a negative-screen framework, this criterion exists to stop you mistaking a thin business for a cyclical bottom.

The criterion in doubt is operating cash flow. Memory price increases forced the company to build inventory ahead of schedule, and in 1H26 operating cash flow coverage of net profit has already fallen below 1x; the Q3 single-quarter figure was not disclosed. The five that pass show this is not a company that will be eliminated: ten-year average ROE of 8.5%, interest coverage far above the threshold, long-term gross margin of 17%–22%, and share count up only 3% over five years. The implication is "don't rush to exclude", not "you can buy".

The only metric that genuinely doesn't hold up: net margin 3.6%

Of the seven items laid out, item ⑥ stands out most. Q3 2026 net profit was RMB 3.876 billion on revenue of RMB 107.239 billion, a net margin of 3.6%; the year-earlier figure was 10.8% (122.7÷1,131.2). Even on adjusted net profit it is only 4.6%, also below the 5% threshold. This is not rhetoric about "the worst quarter of the year" — it pushes the five-year floor down another notch.

Why this matters. The other six items in the elimination framework all ask "will it die"; only net margin asks "does it make money". A hardware company with a 3.6% net margin has zero risk tolerance: a 1-point FX swing, memory prices rising another 10%, or another car price cut — any one of them wipes out profit. In Q3 2026, all three happened at once.

To be clear, though: failing this item does not mean the company is in trouble. Xiaomi has net cash on the balance sheet, very low interest-bearing debt and positive operating cash flow, so there is no near-term solvency risk. "Fails" means "you cannot buy it at a good company's price", not "something will blow up".

The questionable one: cash flow eaten up by inventory build

Item ⑤ is marked "questionable" rather than "fail" because the data is incomplete. Memory contract prices rose steadily through the first half of 2026; to lock in cost and supply, Xiaomi extended its inventory build cycle from the usual 6–8 weeks to more than 12 weeks, turning cash into inventory. As a result, operating cash flow coverage of net profit fell below 1x — this is an active choice, not cash that cannot be collected.

Two pieces of evidence support this. First, receivables turnover has not deteriorated: Xiaomi sells to channels on cash or short credit terms, and that has not changed in ten years. Second, inventory has risen, but the mix is memory chips and finished devices, not slow-moving models — although 2026 handset shipments face pressure, there are no signs of channel stuffing.

The real risk is Q4 and Q1 2027: if memory prices peak and roll over in the first half of 2027, this high-cost inventory will require write-downs in the financials, and that is when cash flow and profit get hit together. This is the item to watch.

The five criteria it passed: evidence that it belongs to the category that "won't be phased out"

The ten-year average ROE is 8.5%, slipping to roughly 7.5% annualised in 2026 — just above the 8% threshold, and achieved through "barely any borrowing + fast turnover" rather than high margins. Interest coverage sits well above 10x, reflecting almost no interest-bearing debt and net cash on the balance sheet. In an industry that demands continuous investment, a clean balance sheet is the most practical moat.

The long-term gross margin range of 17%–22% has held: 13.9% in 2019 (price war), 17.7% in 2021, 20.9% in 2024 and 22.5% in 2025. At 20.2%, Q3 2026 remains within that range — merely back at the lower bound. The share count has expanded just 3% over five years (the March 2025 placement of HKD 42.5 billion was the main source), while HKD 20 billion of buybacks in 2026 are shrinking it on a net basis — an excellent outcome among Hong Kong-listed hardware companies.

Conclusion: the de-rating screen passed, but only barely

Five of seven criteria met. On this framework that qualifies a company for the next round of research, not for first-class status. The seventeen folders that follow all answer one question: is a business earning a 3.6% net margin and 20% gross margin worth 32x?

My answer is no, not now — not because the single failed criterion is fatal, but because it is the one valuation leans on hardest. Net margin is the denominator of every P/E, and when the denominator is falling, no multiple, however reasonable, can rescue the stock.

Conclusion

Of the seven criteria, only net margin at 3.6% genuinely fails and only cash-flow coverage is questionable; the other five sit at or above the pass line. The combination is clear: a company that will not collapse but cannot earn excess profit, paying for both upstream memory makers and the downstream price war. The elimination verdict is "keep on watch" — not "exclude," and not "buy." The next call goes to the six gates in m01 and the valuation in m04.
