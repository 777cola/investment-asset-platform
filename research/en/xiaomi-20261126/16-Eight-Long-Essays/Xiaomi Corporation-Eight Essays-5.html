Industry and competition: two sectors, one malaise, one true rival — Xiaomi Corporation

Phones are a cost-driven industry and cars a price-driven one, with the harvest accruing to upstream players

Core view

Xiaomi straddles two industries, phones and autos, and both suffer the same disease: homogeneous products, excess capacity, and prices set by the most aggressive player. Worse, its real rivals sit not downstream but upstream — the three memory giants need do nothing at all; as long as they keep raising prices, Xiaomi's profit disappears on its own.

Handsets: a cost war in a mature market

Global handset shipments have held at around 1.2 billion units for five consecutive years, while China's replacement cycle has stretched from 28 months to 40 months. With the industry no longer growing, competition comes down to grabbing share and squeezing cost. The 2026 cost war was triggered by memory price hikes—DRAM +60%–70%, NAND +100% or more—compressing gross margins across mid- and low-end phones by 2–4 percentage points.

By structure, Apple captures the high-end profit pool (gross margin about 46%), Samsung leans on vertical integration (about 32%), and Xiaomi, Transsion and OPPO compete on efficiency in the mid- and low-end (8%–21%). That structure has not changed in a decade and most likely will not change over the next five years—a shift would require a technology gap, and phones no longer have one.

The implication for Xiaomi: share can be defended, margins cannot rise. A 30% mix above RMB 3,000 domestically is the ceiling; going higher means fighting Apple and Huawei, which requires brand strength rather than value for money. The handset business has hit its ceiling.

Auto: the price war after penetration passes half

China's new-energy passenger vehicle penetration will exceed 50% in 2026, and the industry is shifting from "penetration-driven" to "substitution-driven." In this phase total volume growth falls below 15%, per-vehicle prices decline, and the number of players keeps growing. The 2026 price war is not incidental; once penetration passes halfway, it is inevitable.

Xiaomi sits in the RMB 200,000–300,000 band, competing with BYD Han/Seal, Huawei Luxeed, Tesla Model 3 and Li Auto L series. Of these four, BYD has the lowest cost, Huawei the strongest brand, Tesla the largest global scale and Li Auto the most mature range-extender technology. Xiaomi has only one advantage — ecosystem plus value for money; its weaknesses are brand premium and channel depth.

The 2026Q3 data tells the story: 108,800 units delivered, revenue -19%, average selling price RMB 211,000. A 19% price cut bought break-even — this is "trading share for survival," not "trading product for premium."

The real rival: upstream memory makers

Downstream rivals fight for share; upstream rivals fight for profit. Global memory supply is entirely in the hands of Samsung, SK hynix, Micron, and China's YMTC and CXMT. None of them needs to target Xiaomi — they only have to keep raising prices and Xiaomi's gross margin falls on its own.

2026Q3 is a case in point: Xiaomi did nothing wrong (revenue fell only 5.2%, no share was lost), yet profit dropped 68.4%. That is the power of "industry position" — standing in the middle of the chain, you face others' pricing power at both ends.

Xiaomi's response is in-house development (the Xuanjie chip) and upstream investment (materials, packaging and testing), but both paths carry five-to-ten-year payback periods and cannot rescue gross margin in the short term. So over the next two years, the exogenous variable of "memory prices" will determine its share price more than "how well Xiaomi executes."

Cycle position: both bottoms land in H2 2027

Memory cycle: began in H2 2024 and accelerated in early 2026; on the usual 3–4 year pattern, the peak falls in H1–H2 2027, with the downturn awaiting new capacity coming online.

Auto cycle: the first shakeout after penetration passes 50%, typically lasting 2–3 years and converging on about 10 players only in 2027–2028. Xiaomi now sits "just past the breakeven line, sustainability not yet proven."

Both bottoms arrive almost simultaneously in H2 2027. If the market discounts six months ahead, the share-price bottom is in Q1 2027; if it trades on reported earnings, the bottom is in Q3 2027. That gap determines how long "waiting" has to last.

Conclusion

Sector view: handsets are a cost business and autos a price business; neither generates excess profit. The real competitor is the upstream memory makers. The Q3 2026 profit collapse stems from two overlapping cycles, and we see no systemic improvement before H2 2027. The sector is not to blame, but neither does it offer this company any premium.
