Management: one person's execution, one system's gap — Xiaomi Corporation

Lei Jun is the hardest asset; succession is the softest weakness

Core view

Xiaomi's management can be summed up in one sentence: top-tier capability, largely clean integrity, passable capital allocation, absent succession planning. Lei Jun holds about 24% and a majority of voting rights. He is 57 and in five years built the auto business to 10.88 thousand units in a single quarter—yet he has not left the company a system that works without him.

Lei Jun: product manager and operator, not a capital allocator

On product: smartphone premiumisation was mocked in 2021 but has held a steady 30% share in 2026; autos went from 0 to 108,800 units in a single quarter; Xuanjie chips went from PowerPoint to a million units shipped. He understands products, dares to bet, and can execute.

Execution is rarer still: carmaking was announced in March 2021, the first vehicle delivered in March 2024, and the segment turned profitable in Q3 2026. Five years to cover a path others take ten, with no strategic wavering along the way — no second case like it exists in Chinese manufacturing.

Capital allocation deserves a discount: eight years listed with zero cash dividend, and the HKD 20 billion buyback only came in 2026; how much was invested in carmaking and what the per-vehicle cost is have never been disclosed in detail. He is very good at "building the company"; he is average at "returning value to shareholders."

To judge a person, look at what he chooses to do and what he chooses not to do. Lei Jun chose not to cut R&D when profits looked worst (over RMB 9 billion in a single quarter) — that is long-termism; he chose not to pay a dividend for eight years — that is treating shareholders' money as his own. Both are his judgments; investors can only accept or reject them, not change them.

Lu Weibing: the de facto number two

The path is clear: he joined in 2019 to run Redmi and build it into a standalone brand line, was promoted to group president in 2022 with responsibility for phones and ecosystem, and took on the general manager role at the auto division in 2024, taking autos from 0 to 108,800 units in a single quarter. This is the textbook "heir apparent" path — run one business first, then the entire line, and finally the hardest piece.

On the November 24, 2026 earnings call he said, "This is the toughest quarter of my career." The value of that statement is underappreciated — executives at domestic hardware companies rarely admit difficulty in public; most would frame -68% as "proactive adjustment" or a "one-off factor." A willingness to admit difficulty makes next quarter's guidance more credible.

The risk is that he has never done "from 0 to 1." Redmi was a brand split off from an existing business, and autos were built on Lei Jun's strategy and resource backing. If Lei Jun retired tomorrow, whether Lu Weibing could decide alone "what to do in the next decade" is unproven.

Capital allocation: three positives, two negatives

Positive 1: Share count discipline. The share count expanded just 3% over five years, driven mainly by the HKD 42.5 billion placement in March 2025, and fell on a net basis after HKD 20 billion of buybacks in 2026 — restrained by the standards of Hong Kong-listed hardware companies. Positive 2: Leverage discipline. Almost no interest-bearing debt, net cash on the balance sheet and interest coverage well above 10x. Positive 3: R&D discipline. Over RMB 9 billion per quarter, double-digit YoY growth and Xuanjie chip shipments above one million units.

Negative 1: Zero dividend. No cash dividend in the eight years since listing; buybacks are the only route to shareholder returns. Negative 2: Opaque EV investment. The 2021 announcement cited "USD 10 billion over 10 years", yet actual annual spending, per-vehicle cost structure and plant capex have never been broken out in quarterly reports.

Investors can therefore only guess at per-vehicle economics from segment gross margin and deliveries — when reconciling the data, sell-side and our back-solved auto segment profit differed by 140%, and this is the root cause. A listed company that spends five years and hundreds of billions on a new business without disclosing the investment breakdown carries a governance discount.

Succession and governance: the key lesson

Lei Jun was born in 1969 and turns 57 in 2026; Lu Weibing was born in 1975 and is 51. Were Lei Jun to retire at 65, the handover window would fall in 2034, when Lu Weibing will be 59. The timing is not tight, but the company has never publicly discussed succession arrangements.

Apple offers a comparison: Cook's succession involved a three-year transition, with Jobs stepping down as CEO two and a half years in advance. TSMC likewise: Chang Chung-mou laid the groundwork two years ahead of both of his handovers. Xiaomi has no such arrangement to date — the most obvious gap in its governance structure.

The only public governance controversy was the 2% equity incentive granted to Lei Jun before the 2018 IPO (about RMB 9.9 billion at the then valuation), which media framed as "paying himself a bonus before going public." In substance it was "procedurally compliant but poor in appearance," not an integrity issue. Both external shocks — the 2022 freeze of RMB 4.8 billion in assets in India, and the 2024 car qualification — were handled cleanly, showing that the crisis-response mechanism works.

One final organizational fact: Xiaomi's headcount exceeded 60,000 in 2026, with front-line staff at car plants and stores accounting for a substantial share. This means it has shifted from an "asset-light internet hardware company" to an "asset-heavy, labor-heavy manufacturing company" — a transition that tests its management span more severely than its capital requirements.

Conclusion

Management 8/10: top-tier capability, clean integrity, passable capital allocation, no succession plan. Lei Jun's execution is the company's hardest asset; the lack of a succession plan and opaque investment disclosure are its softest weaknesses. Neither alters the "worth tracking" call, but both warrant a 10%–15% valuation discount.
