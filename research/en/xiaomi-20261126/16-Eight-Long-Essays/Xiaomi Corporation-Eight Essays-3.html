Moat anatomy: three real, one fake — Xiaomi Corporation

Efficiency, ecosystem and brand all hold up; pricing power does not
Research date 2026/11/26  ·  Price HK$26.60 (+0.15%)  ·  Market cap Approximately HKD 685 billion (25.741 billion shares)  ·  PE (annualized) About 32x, on our rough calculation from annualised Q3 adjusted net profit of RMB 19.5 billion

Core view

Xiaomi's moat has four components: efficiency (real), ecosystem (real but widening), brand (half real), pricing power (false). The first three ensure it will not be displaced; the fourth ensures it cannot earn excess profit — and that fourth is the pivot of the entire investment case.

Rule one: efficiency — the hardest one

Efficiency shows up in three places. Procurement: Xiaomi is one of the world's largest buyers of phones and IoT devices, giving it strong bargaining power in oversupplied segments such as panels, structural components and contract manufacturing. Channels: direct sales through Mi Home, combined with uniform online and offline pricing, strip out the 20%–25% markup of traditional channels. Turnover: inventory days are far below those of traditional hardware companies, so the same gross margin earns its money one more time over.

The evidence for this moat is "cheaper for the same spec". With the same Snapdragon chip and the same screen, Xiaomi can price 10%–15% below rivals and still make money — a direct expression of efficiency. Blended gross margin was 20.2% in Q3 2026; holding 20% against a 60%–70% rise in memory prices is itself proof of efficiency.

But the efficiency moat has a low ceiling. It can earn Xiaomi 2 percentage points more than rivals, not 20.Efficiency is a moat for "living a little better", not a moat for "making big money".

Point two: ecosystem — broadening, but slowly

An ecosystem's value lies in "the hassle of switching brands." For a household with a Xiaomi phone, robot vacuum, air conditioner, TV and car head unit, switching means switching accounts, switching apps, reconfiguring the network and relearning how to operate everything—the cost is not high, but it is annoying enough that most people cannot be bothered.

The data bear this out: Xiaomi's connected IoT devices exceed 800 million units, its user repurchase rate is high relative to the industry, and a considerable share of 2026 car buyers were already Xiaomi phone users.The ecosystem is indeed lifting retention.

It has not, however, raised willingness to pay. Automotive revenue fell 19% in Q3 2026 with deliveries flat, showing that even existing users will not pay an extra RMB 50,000 for the brand; phones dared not raise prices when memory costs rose, showing that users will not pay an extra RMB 200 for the ecosystem either.The ecosystem keeps people, but not a premium.

Article 3: Brand — Half True

Domestic models priced above RMB 3,000 now hold a steady share of more than 30% — a genuine premiumisation win. Five years ago Xiaomi had almost no presence in this price band; it now splits the segment evenly with Honor and OPPO, evidence that brand strength has genuinely improved.

That brand strength has not, however, translated into pricing power. For the same flagship, Apple commands RMB 6,000 while Xiaomi can only ask RMB 4,500; for the same RMB 250,000 SUV, Li Auto sells at list while Xiaomi must cut to RMB 210,000 to move units. The brand makes Xiaomi "worth considering," not "worth choosing first."

Such is the fate of a "value-for-money brand": the more successfully you convince users that "same specs, cheaper," the less they will pay up for you. Brand and pricing power are in conflict here.

Article 4: Pricing power — illusory, and unlikely to materialise any time soon

There is only one test of pricing power: after a price increase, do customers walk away. Xiaomi ran two experiments in 2026, and both showed it has none.

Experiment one: memory costs. DRAM rose 60%–70%, creating enormous cost pressure, and Xiaomi chose to absorb it (gross margin 22.9%→20.2%) rather than raise prices and pass it on. With pricing power, it would have raised prices by USD 100 like Apple and held gross margin flat. It did not, because any price increase costs it share.

Experiment two: car pricing. 2026Q3 deliveries were 10.88 million units with revenue -19%, and average selling price per vehicle fell from 26.0 to 21.1. To sustain deliveries, it chose a 19% price cut. Companies that can set prices do not trade price for volume; only those that cannot do.

So the moat scores are: efficiency 8/10, ecosystem 7/10, brand 7/10, pricing power 2/10. The three real ones added together still cannot buy the fourth—because pricing power is not earned through effort; it is conferred by position.

Conclusion

Moat verdict: three real, one false, composite 6.2/10. That score means Xiaomi will not be eliminated, but it also means it cannot earn excess profit. Valuation implication: value it as an "excellent manufacturer" at 15–22x, not as an "ecosystem platform" at 30x or more. The current 32x multiple assumes the latter.
