## Real signal one: Pengcheng locks in 70,000 orders

The extended-range SUV "Pengcheng," launched in October, locked in over 70,000 orders in its first month — the best news for Xiaomi in 2026 apart from its auto business turning profitable. **Why it matters: extended-range SUVs are the easiest category to scale in the RMB 200,000–300,000 price band** — Li Auto built annual sales of 500,000 units on this one category alone.

But discount it. Locked orders include refundable deposits, and the industry norm is a conversion rate to firm orders of about 70%, so 70,000 orders correspond to roughly 50,000 units of real demand; spread over a three-month delivery cycle, that is 16,000–17,000 units a month. **That is not enough to push 2027 auto revenue to RMB 120 billion — it supports only RMB 90–100 billion.**

The market reaction at the time was a rebound on heavier volume, but nothing like the run of limit-up sessions when the SU7 launched in 2025. **That shows the market has already priced in "Xiaomi can build good cars"; what it is pricing now is "can Xiaomi make money from cars."**

## Real signal two: Q3 results drew almost no market reaction

The post-close earnings on November 24 were ugly: net profit -68.4%, adjusted net profit -56.8%. Yet the stock was essentially flat on 11/25–11/26 (closed at 26.60 on 11/26, +0.15%). **This is the most important takeaway from these results — the bad news has already been priced in by the selloff.**

Compare with August: Q2 net profit was -42.6%, yet the stock rose 4.5% on 8/21. Both times the pattern was "bad news out, stock doesn't fall." That tells us two things: first, the market had already fully anticipated the memory-cycle hit; second, **the share price no longer contains any "Xiaomi is a growth stock" premium** — 32x annualized doesn't sound cheap, but it is attached to a price that has already been cut 38%.

The detail the market is truly overlooking is autos: the segment turned its first quarterly operating profit, yet the market is fixated on the -68.4%. **If autos stay profitable in Q4, this detail becomes the main story in March next year.**

## Noise: those three days in mid-November

From November 17 to 20, the global AI trade collapsed: Nvidia fell 14% in a single day, the Hang Seng Tech Index plunged, and CoreWeave retreated more than 70% from its high. Xiaomi fell in sympathy, but its business has no direct link to AI compute — **this is a classic "correlation selloff," not a "causality selloff."**

How to tell the difference? Look at how well the decline matches fundamental events. Xiaomi's drop over those days broadly tracked the Hang Seng Tech Index, with no excess decline; over the same period there was no new information on its fundamentals. **That is the definition of noise: an event that does not change future cash flows, only the discount rate.**

The rebound on November 23 was likewise noise. The right response to this kind of volatility is to do nothing — **it offers neither a buy signal nor a sell signal, only sentiment.**

## Which ones can be ignored

- Apple event: Xiaomi does not compete head-on in the above-RMB 6,000 price band; the impact is on the "premiumization narrative," not on current-period revenue.

- Broker rating changes: within three months, 5 cut target prices and 3 raised them, a net effect of zero — a lagging indicator that follows the share price.

- Buyback announcement: the HKD 20 billion plan was announced at the start of the year; monthly execution constitutes no new information — but it does put a floor under the share price.

- Index rebalancing and southbound flows: they affect short-term supply and demand, not enterprise value.

One countervailing observation: **in three months there has not been a single negative event of the "Xiaomi is finished" magnitude** — no recalls, no regulatory penalties, no core executive departures, no supply-chain cutoffs. For a company fighting on six fronts at once, that is itself proof of governance quality. The things truly worth worrying about are often not the ones in the news.
