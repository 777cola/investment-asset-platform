Core thesis

Eight Xiaomi-related public events occurred between late August and November 26. Only two genuinely changed the fundamentals: the Pengcheng launch in October (over 70,000 locked orders in the first month) and the Q3 report on November 24. The other six—spillover from the AI trade collapse, the Apple event, index rebalancing, broker ratings and the buyback announcement—were noise or already-priced information.

The stock fell from HKD 29.02 on August 21 to HKD 26.60 on November 26, down 8.3%. The path of the decline carries more information than its magnitude: most of the drop occurred over three days in mid-November and had nothing to do with Xiaomi's fundamentals.

Real signal one: Pengcheng locks in 70,000 orders

The Pengcheng extended-range SUV, launched in October, locked in over 70,000 orders in its first month — the best news for Xiaomi in 2026 outside its auto business turning profitable. Why it matters: extended-range SUVs are the easiest category to scale in the RMB 200,000–300,000 price band — Li Auto built annual sales of 500,000 units on this one category alone.

Discount it, though. Locked orders include refundable deposits, and the industry norm is a conversion rate to firm orders of about 70%, so 70,000 orders equate to roughly 50,000 units of real demand; spread over a three-month delivery cycle, that is 16,000–17,000 units a month. That is not enough to push 2027 auto revenue to RMB 120 billion — it supports only RMB 90–100 billion.

The market reaction at the time was a rebound on heavier volume, but nothing like the run of limit-up sessions when the SU7 launched in 2025. The market has already priced in that Xiaomi can build good cars; what it is pricing now is whether Xiaomi can make money from cars.

Real signal two: Q3 results drew almost no market reaction

The post-close print on November 24 was ugly: net profit -68.4%, adjusted net profit -56.8%. Yet the stock was essentially flat on 11/25–11/26, closing at 26.60 on 11/26 (+0.15%). That is the key takeaway from these results — the bad news was already priced in by the selloff.

The August quarter offers the comparison: Q2 net profit fell -42.6%, yet the stock rose 4.5% on 8/21. Both times the pattern was "bad news out, stock doesn't fall." Two conclusions follow. First, the market had fully anticipated the memory-cycle hit. Second, the share price no longer embeds any "Xiaomi is a growth stock" premium — 32x annualised doesn't sound cheap, but it sits on a price that has already been cut 38%.

What the market is truly overlooking is autos: the segment delivered its first quarterly operating profit, yet attention remains fixed on the -68.4%. If autos stay profitable in Q4, this becomes the main story in March next year.

Noise: those three days in mid-November

Global AI trades sold off hard from November 17 to 20: Nvidia fell 14% in a single day, the Hang Seng Tech Index plunged and CoreWeave retreated more than 70% from its high. Xiaomi fell in sympathy, yet its business has no direct link to AI compute — this is a classic "correlation selloff," not a "causality selloff."

How do you tell the two apart? By how closely the decline maps to fundamental events. Xiaomi's drop over those days broadly tracked the Hang Seng Tech Index with no excess decline, and no new fundamental information emerged over the same period. That is the definition of noise: an event that does not change future cash flows, only the discount rate.

The November 23 rebound was noise as well. The right response to this kind of volatility is to do nothing — it offers neither a buy signal nor a sell signal, only sentiment.

Which ones can be ignored
  • Apple event: Xiaomi does not compete head-on in the above-RMB 6,000 price band, so the impact falls on the premiumisation narrative rather than current-period revenue.
  • Broker rating changes: 5 target price cuts against 3 hikes in three months nets to zero — a lagging indicator that follows the share price.
  • Buyback announcement: the HKD 20 billion plan was announced at the start of the year, so monthly execution carries no new information — but it does put a floor under the share price.
  • Index rebalancing and southbound flows: these affect short-term supply and demand, not enterprise value.

One countervailing observation: in three months there has not been a single negative event of the "Xiaomi is finished" magnitude — no recalls, no regulatory penalties, no core executive departures, no supply-chain cutoffs. For a company fighting on six fronts at once, that is itself proof of governance quality. The things truly worth worrying about are often not the ones in the news.

Conclusion

Attribution: within the three-month decline, only the memory price increase leg was fundamentals-driven; the mid-November move was systematic noise. Both genuine signals — Pengcheng order lock-in and auto turning positive — were buried under the -68.4% headline.Investor takeaway: selling Xiaomi on 20 November on the AI selloff meant selling on a reason unrelated to Xiaomi.
