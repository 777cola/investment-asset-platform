Core view

Xiaomi's risk list is long, but only two items can truly impair principal: memory price increases running through end-2027 (40% probability; impact: handset gross margin down another 2 points), and a second round of the auto price war (50% probability; impact: the segment swings back to a loss). The remaining three are chronic: geopolitics, organisational complexity and valuation.

Impact estimate: each 1pct decline in handset gross margin equates to roughly RMB 840 million of gross profit (revenue of RMB 83.6 billion × 1%); after a 25% tax rate and minority interests, the net profit impact is about RMB 600 million.
Fatal risk one: the memory cycle runs longer than expected

This is the most concrete risk today. DRAM contract prices rose 60%–70% in early 2026 and NAND more than 100%, and the Street widely expects the shortage to run into 2027, with HBM potentially up another 80%–100% in 2027. Phones do not use HBM, but HBM crowds out capacity, and ordinary DRAM supply is equally tight.

Xiaomi has responded on three fronts—advance stockpiling, annual supply-chain cost reductions and price increases on selected models. All three are already in place, yet gross margin still fell 2.7pct. If prices do not fall in 2027, phone gross margin could drop below 6%, at which point the phone business would be loss-making.

Li Lu would ask a more fundamental question: why would a company hand its fate to three memory makers? Because it has no choice—in-house memory capex runs into the hundreds of billions, and Xiaomi cannot fund it. This is not a management failing; it is a problem of industry position.

Fatal risk two: the second round of the auto price war

The first round (2024–2025) wiped out the gross profit of the new entrants; the second round (2026–2027) will wipe out the fuel-car profit of traditional automakers; only the third round is the elimination stage. Xiaomi now sits between the first and second rounds, having just turned the segment profitable on cost and expense control.

The danger is that it has no fallback. The RMB 200,000–300,000 price band is the most fiercely contested: moving up, it lacks brand strength (matching BBA would take a decade); moving down, it cannot match BYD on cost (vertical integration). Its "first single-quarter profit" may prove to be the peak of this round rather than the starting point. If so, 2027 segment profit expectations must be cut by 70%.

Chronic illness: geopolitics, organization, valuation

Geopolitics is the hardest variable to quantify. Entering Europe in 2027 is the right call, but EU tariff policy on Chinese EVs can shift at any time, and the 2022 asset freeze in India already showed once how geopolitical risk actually lands. On BYD's pace in Europe, the first two years are pure investment, and Xiaomi should budget for losses in the RMB 10 billion range on this business.

Organizational complexity is underestimated. In 2026 Xiaomi will be running six initiatives at once: smartphone premiumization, IoT overseas expansion, auto capacity expansion, Xuanjie chips, robotics and AI large models. Six lines burning cash simultaneously means any one going wrong drags down the whole. Li Lu's experience: "When a company does six things at once, usually two of them go badly, but you don't know which two."

Valuation risk is the most direct. 32x annualized P/E, against a backdrop of the Hang Seng Tech Index falling more than 25% in 2026. If Hong Kong liquidity keeps contracting, or the market reclassifies Xiaomi as "manufacturing," the multiple compresses from 32x to 18–20x — even with profits unchanged, the stock falls 40%.

Under what circumstances would these risks erupt simultaneously?

The worst case: memory prices fail to normalise in 2027 (handset gross margin 6%), a second round of the auto price war pushes the segment into a loss, and Hong Kong liquidity contracts (multiple 18x). On 2027 net profit of RMB 15 billion at 18x, market cap is RMB 270 billion and the share price HKD 11.5 — 57% below the current price.

This scenario is not highly probable (roughly 10% chance all three occur together), but it sets the position cap. Li Lu's approach: first work out how much you can lose in the worst case, then decide how much to buy. At the current price, the worst case is a 57% loss, and that payoff requires a very high win rate to hold up.

Conclusion

Risk conclusion: both fatal risks are cyclical and cannot be researched away, only priced. A 32x multiple compensates for neither. Of the five risks, only the Pengcheng ramp-up can be tracked and verified; the other four must wait for macro variables to turn. The "under review" stance reflects the difficulty of the cycle, not of the company.
