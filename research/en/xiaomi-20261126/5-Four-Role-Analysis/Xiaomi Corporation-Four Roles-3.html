Industry Research (Munger's Lens): Two Industries, One Disease — Xiaomi Corporation

Smartphones are a zero-sum fight for existing demand; autos are a price war for incremental demand. Neither generates excess profit.

Core view

Xiaomi straddles two industries, and the same disease afflicts both: homogeneous products, excess capacity, and prices set by whoever is most aggressive. Global smartphone shipments have held at around 1.2 billion units for five straight years, while China's replacement cycle has stretched to 40 months. NEV penetration has passed 50%, ushering in a phase where anyone can build one and no one can make money.

Mobile phones: not a growth industry, a cost industry

Global smartphone shipments are expected at around 1.2 billion units in 2026, flat with 2021, while China's replacement cycle has stretched from 28 months to 40 months. The industry is no longer growing; what remains is share and cost.

Memory broke the cost line in 2026. DRAM contract prices rose 60%–70% early in the year and NAND by more than 100%, with institutions judging the shortage will persist into 2027. Handset makers have only three choices: raise prices and lose share, cut specs and lose reputation, or absorb it and lose profit. Xiaomi chose the third — consistent with its long-standing approach, and the direct cause of its gross margin falling from 22.9% to 20.2%.

Munger would point to the inevitability of this choice: in a commoditized industry, rising costs ultimately fall on the player with the least pricing power. Apple can raise iPhone prices by USD 100; Xiaomi cannot.

Auto: After Penetration Passes Half, Only Price Remains

New-energy passenger vehicle penetration in China passed 50% in 2026, and the industry has shifted from "penetration-driven" to "substitution-driven": every new-energy vehicle sold takes a unit of share from a fuel-burning car and, at the same time, takes customers from another new-energy brand. This phase is characterised by slowing total volume growth, declining per-vehicle prices and a still-growing number of players.

Xiaomi's position is unusual. It is the fastest among new entrants to pass 10.0 thousand units, and it has the most concentrated price band (RMB 20.0–30.0 thousand) — precisely the main battleground for BYD Han, BYD Seal, Huawei Luxeed and Tesla Model 3. Four rivals, three of them cheaper or stronger in brand.

The 2026Q3 data shows the outcome: 10.88 thousand units delivered, revenue -19%, and average selling price down from 26.0 thousand to 21.0 thousand. Price cuts bought the narrative of "first quarterly profit" — the profit came from costs and expenses, not from price.

The overlap of two cycles: 2026 is the worst convergence point

The memory cycle has peaked (year two of price increases) and the auto price war is mid-stage (the first round of consolidation since penetration passed 50%). Both cycles are turning down together, squeezing Xiaomi's profit from both ends. Munger's inversion: this is not "something wrong with operations" but "the cycles happen to coincide" — yet precisely for that reason, current profit cannot be valued as the norm.

When does it improve? Memory: look to 2H27 (new capacity coming online plus a marginal slowdown in AI server demand). Autos: look to 2027–2028 (the field consolidating from 30 players to 10). Both timing points are one half-year later than the market expects.

Long-term industry margins: don't expect miracles

Handset industry history is clear: outside Apple, no handset company has sustained a net margin above 8%. Samsung leans on memory and displays; Xiaomi has neither. Auto industry history is harsher still—global automakers run long-term net margins of 5%–7%, with Toyota's best year at 10%.

So Xiaomi's long-term fair net margin is roughly 5%–7%, implying net profit of RMB 65–90 billion on 2027 revenue of RMB 1.3 trillion. That number sounds good, but it requires 2027 revenue to grow 25% over 2026—optimistic assuming zero handset growth and slowing auto growth.

Munger sums it up: "In a business that generates no excess profits, even the best company earns only an average return, unless it has pricing power." Xiaomi does not.

Conclusion

Industry view: phones are a cost business, autos a price business; neither generates excess profit. Xiaomi is a share contender and a price taker in both. The Q3 2026 profit collapse reflects two cycles overlapping, and a systemic improvement is unlikely before the second half of 2027. The industry does not carry the blame, but it gives this company no premium either.
