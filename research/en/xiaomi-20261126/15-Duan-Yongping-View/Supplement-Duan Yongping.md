## Question 1: Is this a good business?

Not bad, not great. The good: no debt needed, fast turnover, user trust. The bad: it earns from efficiency, not pricing power. 2026Q3 blended gross margin 20.2%, net margin 3.6% — sell RMB 100 of goods and keep RMB 3.6. Not a bad number for manufacturing, but far from a "good business."

What is a good business? My old formulation: raise prices and customers still don't leave — that is a good business. Moutai raises prices and customers keep buying; Apple raises prices and customers keep buying. Xiaomi dares not raise prices — memory is up 60%–70% and it chooses to absorb the cost, because it knows a price hike means lost share. Not that it doesn't want the profit; it can't take it.

So I won't call this a bad business. I'll call it a "hard-working" business. A hard-working business can still make money, but only hard-earned money — it doesn't deserve a high multiple.

## Question 2: Will Xiaomi still be around in ten years?

Most likely it will still be here: top three in phones globally, number one in IoT globally, and cars have already crossed the make-or-break line (108,800 units in a single quarter, segment profitable). Put those three together and within ten years no one can take it down.

But "still here" and "still looking the way it does now" are two different things. In ten years it could be a car-led company with phones reduced to a traffic funnel, or cars may not pan out and it retreats to phones plus home appliances. Which one it will be, I don't know. That doesn't affect whether it is "still here," but it does affect what it is worth.

I don't like guessing at what things will look like in ten years. What I look at is: will anyone still need what this company does in ten years? Phones, home appliances, cars — all will be needed. So it will still be here. That's enough; leave the rest to the price.

## Question three: Can you understand it?

Half of it I understand. The product I understand — Xiaomi's stuff is good value, the design isn't ugly, the system is decent; that's real skill. The channel I understand — Mi Home stores have high sales per square metre, online and offline prices are the same; that's efficiency.

But the cycle I don't understand. Whether memory prices rise or fall next year, I don't know; how many years the car price war runs, I don't know; how European tariffs change, I don't know either. Those three "don't knows" together are why I dare not take a heavy position.

With things you don't understand, the best approach is not to touch them, or to touch only a little. I often say "better to miss than to get it wrong" — missing out on Xiaomi is no disgrace; getting it wrong is.

## Question 4: At what price to buy?

At HKD 26.60, or 32x annualised, I am not buying. Not because the valuation is absurd, but because it is expensive for no reason — why should a quarter with net profit down 68% YoY and gross margin still sliding command 32x?

The maths is simple. On RMB 25 billion of earnings in 2027 at 20x, the market cap is RMB 500 billion, or around HKD 21; on RMB 18 billion at 16x, it is HKD 12. Split the difference and HKD 23 is my ceiling bid.

Some will ask: what if it rallies? Then it rallies — I won't chase it. The market has a new story every year; my money only buys what I understand, what I can calculate, and what leaves room for error.

## Question 5: What worries you most?

The biggest fear is that it "keeps working hard and never makes money." The company pushes hard from top to bottom: Lei Jun, at 57, still speaks for two hours at launch events, Lu Weibing calls this "my toughest quarter," and engineers have driven Xuanjie chip shipments to a million units.Yet after five years of effort, net margin has fallen from 10.8% to 3.6%.

Working hard without making money is usually not a people problem but a position problem. Xiaomi sits in the middle of the industrial chain, with oligopolies upstream (memory, chips, batteries) and fragmented consumers downstream — it has leverage over neither end.From this position, no amount of effort earns more than average returns.

The second fear is Lei Jun's retirement. He is 57, with at least 5 to 8 years left, but the company has never disclosed a succession plan. Lu Weibing is the de facto number two, yet he has never independently made a "0 to 1" decision.This is a question without an answer, and I treat it as a discount factor.

## Question six: What kind of person is Lei Jun?

A top-tier product manager and top-tier execution, but an average capital allocator.

On product: Xiaomi's phone premiumisation, the definition of the car and the investment in the Xuanjie chip all show he understands products and dares to bet. On execution: the car was announced in March 2021, first deliveries came in March 2024, and a single quarter in 2026 hit 108,800 units — a pace rarely seen globally.

Capital allocation deserves a discount: eight years listed with zero dividends, and the HKD 20 billion buyback only came in 2026; how much was invested in the car and the per-unit cost have never been disclosed in detail. He is very good at "building the company"; he is average at "returning value to shareholders."

This does not affect my view of him personally — he is a man who gets things done. But buying a stock means buying "company + price," not "person + sentiment." Sentiment cannot be used as a discount rate.
