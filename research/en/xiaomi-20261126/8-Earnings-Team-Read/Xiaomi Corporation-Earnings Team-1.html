Duan Yongping Reads Financial Reports: Profit Is Not Taken by Competitors, but by Upstream — Xiaomi Corporation

Read where the money went first, then who took it.

Core view

The most important takeaway from this earnings report is not the -68.4%, but the RMB 4.24 billion drop in gross profit. Revenue fell only 5.2% and deliveries were roughly flat, showing Xiaomi lost no customers; but gross margin fell from 22.9% to 20.2%, with the money taken by upstream memory makers.

First, check whether the business itself has changed.

Revenue RMB 107.239 billion, down 5.2% YoY, of which automobiles were about RMB 22.9 billion (-19%) and smartphones x AIoT about RMB 83.6 billion (broadly flat). What it sells hasn't changed, who sells it hasn't changed — what changed is the cost of goods. Duan Yongping's habit when reading financial statements is to first ask "is this still the same business it was?" — and the answer is "yes": Xiaomi is still the Xiaomi that trades efficiency for share.

Now look at where the profit went. Gross profit RMB 21.66 billion, down RMB 4.24 billion; net profit RMB 3.876 billion, down RMB 8.39 billion. Of the remaining RMB 4.15 billion, half is expenses (R&D hasn't been cut, stores are expanding) and half is a reversal in investment items. Not one bit of it is "goods that won't sell."

Now look at who took the money

Memory. DRAM contract prices rose 60%–70% in 2026 and NAND by more than 100%, with memory accounting for 15%–25% of a phone's BOM. Xiaomi chose to absorb the cost rather than pass it on through price hikes—because raising prices means losing share. In Duan Yongping's vocabulary, that choice is "benfen" (doing one's duty): not taking advantage of users. But benfen comes at a price, and the price is RMB 4.2 billion of gross profit this quarter.

So I do not see this as an operating misstep. It is the inevitable result for a company without pricing power when hit by a cost shock. It deserves respect, but not a 32x multiple.

Finally, look at the auto segment.

Delivered 108,800 vehicles, revenue -19%, segment posts first quarterly operating profit. Duan Yongping would ask: "Is this profit scale effect or expense normalization?" That question decides whether the auto business is a business or a numbers game.

My read is "half and half": the annual supplier price-downs are real (scale effect), and the absence of Q2's one-off provisions in Q3 is also real (expense normalization). So watch two consecutive quarters — if Q4 stays profitable, it is a business; if Q4 swings back to a loss, it is numbers.

Conclusion

Duan Yongping-style conclusion: the business hasn't changed, customers haven't been lost, the money was taken by upstream. This is an "ugly but not broken" earnings report. He would keep watching, not judge on a single quarter, and not call it cheap just because it fell 38% — his standard is "discounted cash flow ten years out," not "how much it has fallen."
