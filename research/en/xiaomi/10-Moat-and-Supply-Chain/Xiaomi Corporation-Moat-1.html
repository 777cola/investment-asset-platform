Core thesis

Item-by-item scoring: brand 8, channels 8, ecosystem synergy 7, cost control 7, in-house technology 6, switching costs 6, weighted 7/10. That is not a low score — Xiaomi's moat is real, but it protects share, not gross margin.

The most dangerous rival is neither BYD nor Huawei but memory makers: wresting share from BYD takes three years of fighting, whereas a single quarter of memory price increases can strip away 3 points of gross margin. Second is Huawei: HIMA collides head-on with Xiaomi in the RMB 200,000–300,000 price band and carries brand premium, leaving Xiaomi to answer with cost-effectiveness alone.

Position in the value chain: strong brand on the demand side, weak buyer on the supply side

Upstream: memory (Samsung, SK hynix, YMTC, CXMT), SoC (Qualcomm, MediaTek, in-house Xuanjie), displays, batteries and structural components. Midstream: device assembly and car plants (Beijing Phase 1 and Phase 2). Downstream: online and offline channels, carriers and overseas markets.

Xiaomi's role in the chain is that of "demand integrator": it translates user needs into product definitions, then places orders with dozens of suppliers.That gives it brand power over users but no bargaining power over suppliers.The 2026 memory price hike exposed this most starkly.

Cars changed its position. For the first time Xiaomi owns heavy assets—plants, lines, tooling—and large-scale manufacturing capability; for the first time it carries fixed costs.Moving from "asset-light integrator" to "asset-heavy manufacturer" changes the nature of the moat: it once ran on turnover, now it also runs on capacity utilisation.

The essence of a moat: efficiency, not pricing power

Xiaomi's moat can be summed up in one sentence: deliver a better product for the same money, or the same product for less money. This is an efficiency moat, not a pricing-power moat.

The difference is clearest under stress testing: when costs rise 10%, a pricing-power company (Apple) raises prices 5% and absorbs 5% itself, leaving gross margin roughly unchanged; an efficiency company (Xiaomi) cannot raise prices, so gross margin drops a full 3 points. 2026Q2 is the result of this stress test.

The value of an efficiency moat should not be underestimated: it lets Xiaomi outlast rivals in a red ocean, and it lets the company keep entering new categories (from phones to TVs to air conditioners to cars). But its ceiling is "industry-average gross margin + efficiency premium," not "brand premium."

The most dangerous rival: not BYD, but memory makers

BYD and Huawei are fighting for share; memory makers are fighting for profit. DRAM contract prices rise 60%–70% in 2026, and Xiaomi's smartphone gross margin falls from 11.5% to 8.5%, three points gone in a single quarter, equivalent to RMB 3 billion in annualized profit — worth more than the market share any automaker has taken.

Why can't it be defended? Memory is a three-player oligopoly (Samsung, SK hynix, Micron) plus two Chinese players (YMTC, CXMT), with an 18–24 month capacity expansion cycle and rigid near-term supply. Xiaomi's only levers are to lock in supply early and to raise per-device memory capacity so it can sell at higher prices; it is doing both, but neither solves the root problem.

The second-biggest danger is Huawei: HIMA collides head-on with Xiaomi in the RMB 200,000–300,000 price band. Huawei has brand premium and the smart-driving label; Xiaomi can only respond with "same specs, RMB 30,000–50,000 cheaper." This is not a failure of product strength but a gap in brand strength.

Supply chain: in-house build versus outsourcing

Xiaomi EV's supply-chain strategy is "core in-house plus key outsourcing": die-casting, body and e-drive developed in-house; batteries from CATL and BYD; autonomous-driving compute from Nvidia Orin/Thor; cockpit chips from Qualcomm. That let it build a car within two years, but it also leaves a large chunk of per-vehicle BOM priced by others.

BYD, by contrast, supplies batteries, motors, electronic controls and chips in-house, with vertical integration near 75% — which is why it can hold gross margin at 18% through a price war.Xiaomi's integration is roughly 30%–40% (rough estimate), which is why its gross margin sits below BYD's and why it is more exposed to upstream price increases.

On the phone side it is even clearer: SoC from Qualcomm and MediaTek, memory from the three oligopolists, panels from BOE — what Xiaomi controls is product definition, the system and channels.In the supply chain it has no ability to "choke others", only the risk of being choked. The Xuanjie O1 is the sole exception, but it covers only some flagships and the baseband is still outsourced.

Is the moat widening or narrowing?

Three forces are widening: the ecosystem (a rising car parc and tighter human-car-home linkage), channels (Xiaomi Home paired with car delivery centres), and technology (XRing shipments above 1 million units, with iteration continuing).

Two are narrowing: cost advantage (memory price increases are eating into the efficiency dividend) and switching costs (the car price war makes it easier for users to be pulled away by a rival's discounts).

Net effect: the moat's width is unchanged, but its depth has shallowed. It still keeps out new entrants (no one can build 100,000 cars in two years), but it cannot stop price wars among incumbents, nor upstream cost pass-through.That is why gross margin is so volatile—the moat blocks "who gets in," not "who can make money."

Conclusion

Moat verdict: 7/10 — an efficiency-based moat, real but not deep enough. Brand, channels and ecosystem all hold up, and in-house technology is improving, but the company has no pricing power: a 10% cost increase has to be absorbed on its own. The most dangerous competitor is memory makers, not car companies, because the former directly decide the life-or-death of gross margin in the handset business. Investment implication: this moat supports "surviving long term", not "sustaining high gross margin long term".
