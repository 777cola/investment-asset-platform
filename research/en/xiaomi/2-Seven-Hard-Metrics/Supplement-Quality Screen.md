# Xiaomi Corporation（01810）— Quality Screen Supplement

- Research date：2026/08/21
- Price：29.02 HKD
- Market cap：About HKD 747 billion

## Notes

The seven metrics follow the "negative screening" standard: (1) 10-year average ROE ≥8%; (2) cumulative 5-year FCF positive; (3) interest coverage ≥2x; (4) long-term gross margin ≥15%; (5) OCF/net profit ≥0.7; (6) long-term net margin ≥5%; (7) 5-year share count dilution ≤20%. Xiaomi data: 2026Q2 gross margin 19.8%, net margin 8.7% (net profit RMB 9.463 billion/revenue RMB 108.922 billion), adjusted net margin 5.7% (62.19/1,089.22); share count 25.741 billion shares; March 2025 placement HKD 42.5 billion, 2026Q1 buyback HKD 20 billion. Historical net profit is per annual report figures; ROE is a rough average of net profit/end-of-year equity, not Wind terminal data. Specific figures for free cash flow and operating cash flow are not separately disclosed in the 2026 interim report summary; related judgments are qualitative estimates based on trends and are marked as rough calculations.

## The ugliest number is not gross margin, but ROE

The ten-year average of 8.5% is the number that sets Xiaomi's ceiling. Breaking it down: in 2018, the year of its listing, it booked RMB 13.4 billion in net profit on fair-value changes in preferred shares, then RMB 10 billion in 2019, RMB 20.3 billion in 2020, RMB 19.3 billion in 2021, a drop to RMB 2.5 billion in 2022, RMB 17.5 billion in 2023 and RMB 23.6 billion in 2024. In seven years ROE cleared 15% in only three, hovering between 5% and 10% in the rest.

The reason is simple: Xiaomi's net assets rose from just over RMB 70 billion in 2018 to more than RMB 200 billion (including the 2025 share placement), so the denominator keeps growing while the numerator rests on thin hardware margins. This is the price of "asset-heavy transformation" — car plants, chip R&D and offline stores are all businesses that spend first and collect later. An ROE of 8.5% means it earns "a little better than a bank loan" over the long run — enough to survive, not enough to be great.

The pass/fail call on the boundary matters: if the car business turns profitable in 2027, ROE has a chance to return to 12%–15%, and this line goes from borderline to passing. But if memory price increases persist for two years, ROE will fall below 6%, and then it genuinely fails.

## Free cash flow: carmaking turns the "cash cow" into a "cash funnel"

Xiaomi's phone business was a solid cash cow: heavy advance receipts, fast inventory turnover, light capex. Before 2021, operating cash flow consistently exceeded net profit. Building cars changed that: Beijing Phase 1 and Phase 2 plants, chip tape-outs, auto distribution channels — all entail annual outlays running from several billion to tens of billions, while the auto segment still loses RMB 2.6 billion per quarter.

The five-year cumulative figure remains positive, propped up by the phone business. But the trend is ugly: the operating cash flow/net profit ratio has fallen from 1.3 to around 1.0, and the first half of 2026 adds another drag — memory prices are rising, forcing the company to stockpile ahead of time, and inventory will tie up markedly more cash. This is industry-wide; Samsung and Transsion cannot escape it either.

Investment implication: Xiaomi cannot afford a large dividend in the near term. It has never paid dividends historically; it returns capital via buybacks — the HKD 20 billion in 2026Q1 was the largest in the company's history. Faulting it for not paying dividends is unfair, but citing buybacks to prove its cash flow is strong does not hold either.

## Gross margin and net margin: not a great business, but by no means a bad one

A long-term gross margin of 19.8% and net margin of 8.7% place it in the upper-middle tier of A-share manufacturing and the middle tier of global consumer electronics. Apple runs a 46% gross margin and 25% net margin; Samsung Electronics sits around 35% gross margin; BYD posts 18% gross margin and 5% net margin. Xiaomi's position is clear: stronger than pure contract manufacturing, but a long way weaker than brand-premium companies.

Stability is the key issue. Smartphone gross margin was 8.5% in Q2 2026, the lowest range on record; auto gross margin was 19.2%, down 7.2pct from last year. Both curves are falling at once, which shows its gross margin is not a "stable premium from a moat" but a passive outcome of "cost volatility + competitive intensity." Such a gross margin looks good in a boom and has no floor in a downturn.

The 8.7% net margin is the bright spot: it held 8.7% in a quarter with revenue down 6.1%, thanks to expense control and high-margin internet services. But RMB 3.2 billion of net profit came from non-operating gains such as investment fair value (net profit RMB 9.463 billion vs adjusted RMB 6.219 billion); strip that out and the core business net margin is only 5.7%.

## Share capital: no dilution this time — buybacks instead

The seventh criterion is the easiest to pass. In March 2025, 800 million shares were placed, raising HKD 42.5 billion, increasing share capital from about 25 billion shares to 25.741 billion shares, a dilution of 3%; in 2026Q1, HKD 20 billion was bought back, roughly 670 million shares at an average price of HKD 30, offsetting most of the placement dilution. Net dilution of 3% over five years is restrained among Hong Kong-listed tech companies.

But note the timing and purpose of the buyback: the 2025 placement was at around HKD 53, and the 2026 buyback at HKD 28–32. Selling high and buying low—this round of capital operations is a plus. The problem is scale—HKD 20 billion is only 2.7% of market cap and cannot change the direction of earnings per share, because profit is falling faster.

## Conclusion: the de-rating screen passes, meaning "don't rush to rule it out"

Seven of seven pass, two are borderline (ROE, FCF). The implication of this result needs to be stated clearly: the purpose of negative screening is to exclude "definitely bad companies"; Xiaomi is not one. It has a brand, cash flow and real R&D spending, and one quarter of losses will not knock it out.

But passing all seven does not make it first-rate. The real dividing line is the first item — for a company with ROE of 8.5%, the midpoint of long-term share-price returns is roughly 8%–10% plus some valuation fluctuation. To earn growth returns, ROE must first return to 15%, and that has to wait for the auto business to turn profitable.

So this piece has only one conclusion: no need to exclude, but no reason to buy now. The two borderline metrics (ROE, FCF) both point to the same thing — the auto business is still in its investment phase, and before 2027 do not expect return on capital to look good.
