Business analysis (Duan Yongping's perspective) — Xiaomi Corporation

What the business actually earns: unit economics across the three segments
Research date 2026/08/21  ·  Price HK$29.02 (+4.54%)  ·  Market cap About HKD 747 billion  ·  PE(TTM) Approximately 19.6x (2026E approximately 28x, estimated)

Core view

Xiaomi is not one business but three bolted together: smartphones (a turnover business), IoT (an ecosystem business) and autos (an asset-heavy business). Each makes money in a completely different way, so valuing them on a single P/E is wrong by construction.

Mobile phones: RMB 112 profit per unit, driven by turnover rather than premium pricing

2026Q2 handset revenue RMB 42.1 billion, gross margin 8.5%, shipments -26.5% YoY, ASP +25.9% YoY. Backing out shipments and ASP from revenue gives roughly 31.85 million units and RMB 1,322, implying gross profit of about RMB 112 per unit. (2025Q2 corresponds to about 43.30 million units and an ASP of RMB 1,050; both figures are rough estimates derived from revenue and growth rates.)

What does this unit economics tell us? That handsets are essentially a turnover business: thin per-unit profit, with cash set in motion by 130 million units a year of scale, by 30-day inventory turnover and by channel float. Its moat is efficiency, not brand premium. So when memory prices rise 60%–70%, an efficiency moat cannot hold — a 10% rise in BOM wipes out the 8.5% gross margin entirely.

Premiumisation is real: models priced above RMB 3,000 account for 32.1% of domestic volume, and ASP rose from RMB 1,050 to RMB 1,322. But premiumisation rescues the revenue mix, not the gross margin, because premium handsets carry higher memory configurations (12GB/16GB + 512GB/1TB), so price increases hit premium models with a larger absolute cost impact.

IoT: an undervalued cash cow, now being dragged down

IoT revenue reached RMB 31.3 billion in 2026Q2, down 19.2% YoY — the steepest decline of the three segments. The business has long been Xiaomi's profit stabiliser: white goods (air conditioners, refrigerators, washing machines), wearables, routers and robot vacuums have consistently carried gross margins of 15%–20%, above smartphones.

Two factors explain the decline: first, the rollback of national subsidies — the 2025 trade-in programme for home appliances pulled demand forward, so 2026 sees a natural payback; second, disintermediation by ecosystem companies — Roborock, Ninebot and others Xiaomi incubated now run their own brands and compete for the same users.

The Duan Yongping-style question: does this business have pricing power? Partly — in price wars over air conditioners and TVs, Xiaomi is the initiator, not the responder, since its "20% cheaper at the same spec" playbook keeps the efficiency advantage on its side. But the ceiling is equally clear: hardware is hardware, and once scale tops out, growth can only come from category expansion.

Automotive: gross profit of RMB 44,000 per vehicle, but a net loss of RMB 25,000 after cost allocation

This is the section that needs the most careful work. Q2 2026 deliveries were 104,199 vehicles, revenue RMB 23.9 billion, ASP RMB 229,300, gross margin 19.2% and segment operating loss RMB 2.6 billion.

Three numbers in a row tell the story: gross profit per vehicle of RMB 44,000 (22.93 × 19.2%), operating loss per vehicle of RMB 25,000 (RMB 2.6 billion ÷ 104,200), and the RMB 69,000 in between is allocated fixed costs and expenses per vehicle. In other words, for every car Xiaomi sells, gross profit covers 64% of fixed costs; the remaining 36% depends on scale.

That defines the problem clearly: whether the auto business can make money does not depend on whether gross margin can climb back to 26%, but on whether capacity utilization can move up another step. At annual sales of 400,000 vehicles, fixed costs per vehicle halve and the business breaks even; at 600,000 vehicles, it earns RMB 10,000–20,000 per vehicle. Annualized deliveries for 2026 are about 420,000 vehicles (rough Q2 annualization), already close to the breakeven line—but ASP is still falling, and that line will move up with it.

What the three businesses share: all are efficiency businesses

Put the three together and they are the same business: make an 80-point product at a lower cost, and earn money on scale and turnover. That holds for phones, for IoT and for cars (same specs, RMB 30,000–50,000 cheaper than the Model Y).

The virtue of this business is replicability — Xiaomi can carry its methodology from one category to the next, which is the underlying reason it built 100,000 cars in 2 years. The drawback is that there is never excess profit: whoever is cheaper than you pushes your gross margin back down. BYD is more vertically integrated, Huawei carries more brand premium, and Xiaomi sits in the middle, squeezed from both sides.

Duan Yongping would call this a "hard-earned money" business. Done well it earns a lot of hard-earned money (adjusted net profit of RMB 30 billion in 2025, for instance), but do not expect the high gross margin that pricing power brings. Value it as hard-earned money, because that is what it is.

Conclusion

Business essence: three segments, one model — efficiency-driven, scale wins, no pricing power. Phones earn RMB 112 per unit. Autos generate RMB 44,000 of gross profit per vehicle but lose RMB 25,000 per vehicle. IoT is the only segment with a decent gross margin, yet it is dragged down by the rollback of national subsidies. Scale utilisation sets the ceiling; BOM cost sets the floor — the company has pricing power at neither end. Deserving of respect, not of a growth-stock multiple.
