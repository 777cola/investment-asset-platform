Industry Research (Munger's Lens) — Xiaomi Corporation

Smartphones are a zero-sum game; EVs are a knockout tournament. Xiaomi is fighting on both fronts.

Core view

Xiaomi stands at the intersection of two industries: smartphones is an industry of "mature installed base plus cost shock," while autos is an industry of "incremental growth plus price war." The former determines how much it earns today; the latter determines how much it is worth tomorrow. What the two industries share is this — neither has a winner-take-all structure.

Smartphones: a global installed base of 1.2 billion units, with the top five vendors locked in a tight share battle

Global annual smartphone shipments have hovered around 1.2 billion units for five years without growth. What makes 2026 unusual is memory price inflation: DRAM contract prices rose 60%–70% early in the year and NAND by over 100%, pushing device makers' BOM costs up 8%–15% while end-product prices could not follow.

The result is industry-wide gross margin compression: Samsung's handset division margin fell, Transsion was forced to raise prices in Africa and lost share, and Xiaomi's gross margin dropped from 11.5% to 8.5%. This is not a Xiaomi-specific operating problem but a cost shock across the industry. The difference lies in whose gross margin is thick enough to absorb it — Apple's 46% gross margin was untouched, while Xiaomi's 8.5% hit the floor.

On structure, Samsung, Apple, Xiaomi, Transsion and OPPO/vivo together take 70% of share, with none above 20%. Munger would point out: none of them can set prices, so this is an industry where the most efficient survives, not one where the strongest brand wins.

China market: Huawei owns premium, Xiaomi owns value-for-money, and the middle is collapsing

China's market structure is the clearest: above RMB 6,000, Huawei and Apple take the bulk; between RMB 3,000 and 6,000, Xiaomi, Honor, OPPO and vivo fight it out; below RMB 3,000, Xiaomi and Honor battle for first place.

Xiaomi's premiumization has made progress — models above RMB 3,000 account for 32.1% of domestic unit sales, double the level three years ago. But note: this 32.1% is a share of units sold, not of revenue and not of profit. Once memory price hikes eat into premium-model gross margin, the incremental profit from premiumization is far smaller than the incremental revenue.

Huawei's return is structural pressure. The HarmonyOS ecosystem plus in-house chips plus brand premium exerts direct pressure on Xiaomi in the above-RMB 4,000 market. Munger's inversion: if you wanted Xiaomi's phone business to fail, what would be the fastest way? The answer is "memory prices keep rising + Huawei keeps taking premium share" — and both are happening at once in 2026.

Autos: the RMB 200,000–300,000 price band is the world's most fiercely contested battleground

Xiaomi Auto is positioned in the RMB 200,000–300,000 battery-electric sedan/SUV segment, a price band that in China is a meat grinder: BYD Han/Tang/Sealion, Tesla Model 3/Y, Huawei Aito M5/M7, Li Auto L6, XPeng G6/G9 and Zeekr 007/7X all compete here.

The industry in 2026 is characterized by penetration above half, slowing growth and a permanent price war. Overall passenger-vehicle growth is in the single digits, NEV penetration exceeds 55%, and incremental volume can only be taken from competitors. That is why Xiaomi's ASP fell from 25.37 to 22.93 (RMB 10,000) — not because it wanted to cut price, but because the benchmark for this price band is drifting down.

The scale threshold is equally clear: Chinese automakers generally break even at annual sales of 300,000–500,000 units. Xiaomi's 2026 run rate is roughly 42 (RMB 10,000) units (a rough annualization of Q2), just past the threshold, but gross margin has already fallen to 19.2%, showing that this is scale bought with price. Such scale is not solid — once it stops conceding on price, deliveries will fall.

Munger-style challenge: will these two industries still exist in ten years?

Phones will still exist in ten years, but their form may change (AI glasses, wearables, and car cockpits taking over some scenarios); cars will still exist in ten years, but half the brands will be gone. In neither industry is Xiaomi the "indispensable" player.

Its distinguishing feature is being present in all of them at once: the phone is the entry point, the car is the scenario, IoT is the network. Munger would concede the synergy is real—for someone who uses a Xiaomi phone, drives a Xiaomi car and has Xiaomi appliances at home, switching costs are genuinely high. But he would also ask: how much gross profit can that synergy convert into? For now, the answer is "it lifts retention, not pricing."

The industry conclusion, then: Xiaomi sits in a position of "decent structure, thin margins." It will not disappear, and it will struggle to earn outsized profits. Investing in it earns money from "share × industry growth × efficiency"; an annualized return of 8%–12% is a reasonable expectation, not 30%.

Conclusion

Industry conclusion: smartphones are a cost war in a mature market, cars a price war in a growth market, and neither battle generates excess profit. Memory price hikes are cutting through industry-wide gross margins, and Xiaomi's 8.5% is among the most fragile tier; the benchmark line for car price bands shifts down every year, the scale threshold is 300,000–500,000 units, and Xiaomi has just crossed it but its gross margin is already retreating. The industry does not reward it; it only allows it to survive. That is why a 28x P/E is unreasonable — it gives growth-stock multiples while buying a manufacturing business.
