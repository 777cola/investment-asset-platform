Munger Close Read: Cars Are Working, So Why the De-rating? — Xiaomi Corporation

Think in reverse: under what circumstances would Xiaomi fail?

Core view

Munger's method is "invert, always invert": rather than ask how Xiaomi succeeds, ask how it fails. Three answers: memory prices keep rising, car margins keep falling, and the organisation cannot support four lines of business. Two of the three have already happened.

Options have become heavy assets, so the valuation method has to change

How did the market value Xiaomi in 2025? It gave the phone business 15x and the auto business "option value" — ignoring losses and pricing in the upside. Back then the auto business was losing RMB 300 million a quarter, and the market said "losses in the investment phase are normal."

By 2026 the auto business is losing RMB 2.6 billion a quarter, and the market has changed its tune. Why? Because the scale is now too large to explain away as an "option" — 100,000 units and RMB 23.9 billion of revenue in a single quarter makes it a mid-sized automaker, and it must be valued on "deliveries × per-vehicle gross profit − fixed costs."

That math yields a negative result. The market is not being emotional; it is doing arithmetic. This is how success de-rates a stock: the more successful the business, the more it must be scrutinized under asset-heavy rules.

Think in reverse: three failure paths

Path one: memory prices rise through 2027. Smartphone gross margin falls from 8.5% to below 6%, the smartphone business swings from profit to loss, and the valuation anchor shifts from "hardware + internet" to "pure hardware," warranting 10x P/E. Probability is not low: HBM crowding out capacity is structural.

Path two: the auto price war escalates further. BYD keeps pressing on cost in 2026, Huawei defends its premium on smart driving, and Xiaomi can only match on price. ASP falls from 22.93 to 21, gross margin drops below 15%, and the quarterly loss widens from RMB 2.6 billion to RMB 4 billion. Probability is moderate, and hinges on Pengcheng's pricing strategy.

Path three: the organisation cannot hold. Four lines — smartphones, IoT, autos and chips — with one person calling the shots. Trouble in any one of them (a failed chip tape-out, an auto recall) drags down the whole. Probability is low, but the impact is the largest.

Moat test: can it raise prices?

Munger judges a moat by a single test: after a price increase, are the customers still there? Xiaomi is cutting prices on both phones and cars, which tells you its moat is efficiency, not pricing power.

The efficiency moat is real—Xiaomi can build the same specs cheaper, and that is organisational capability. But an efficiency moat is fragile: it protects share, not profit. When upstream costs rise, the cost increase eats the efficiency advantage and profit goes to zero.

A company with true pricing power can raise prices when costs rise and pass the pressure on to customers. Xiaomi cannot, because its customers are price-sensitive. That is a function of the business model, not something effort can change.

Conclusion: a good company at a bad moment

Munger would not call Xiaomi a bad company. He would call it a formidable operator pursuing the hardest form of diversification in an industry that earns no excess profit.

But investing is not about picking "the hardest-working company"; it is about picking "the opportunity with the highest return." Buying a business with 8.5% ROE and a 43% profit decline at 28x P/E, no matter how good the company, gives this investment a negative expected return.

His advice would probably be: put it in the "too hard" pile and wait until it is cheap enough that you do not need to forecast the future.

Conclusion

Munger-style conclusion: the more successful the car business, the more it must be judged as a heavy-asset business; the moat is efficiency, not pricing power; two of the three failure paths are already under way. The company deserves respect; the price does not. Watch, do not act.
