Masterclass Close Read (Buffett): a 2.7-point gross margin decline equals RMB 4.5 billion — Xiaomi Corporation

Three numbers tell you all you need to know about an earnings report: gross margin, cash conversion, capital expenditure

Core view

If I could look at only three numbers, I would look at gross margin, operating cash flow/net profit, and capex. In this report, the first fell 2.7pct, the second dropped from 1.3 to around 1.0, and the third sits at a historical high. All three point the wrong way.

Gross margin: down 2.7 points, equivalent to RMB 4.5 billion less profit

19.8% vs 22.5%. That looks like just 2.7 points, but multiplied by revenue of RMB 108.9 billion, it is RMB 2.9 billion of gross profit; add the RMB 7 billion decline in revenue itself, and gross profit is down a total of RMB 4.52 billion.

Compare adjusted net profit: down RMB 4.61 billion. Gross profit down RMB 4.52 billion, net profit down RMB 4.61 billion — one to one. This shows almost no buffer on the cost side: the company did not cut expenses to offset the gross profit decline, and instead raised R&D by 18.9%.

Is this good or bad? Bad in the short term (profit fully exposed), good in the long term (the future was not sacrificed for the present). Buffett would approve of this choice, but would not pay a higher price for it.

Cash conversion: from 1.3 to 1.0, still acceptable

Xiaomi's operating cash flow/net profit has historically run at 1.2–1.4, a healthy range (profit converts to cash, and it can still float on upstream and downstream money). In 2026 this ratio is falling, driven by inventory — memory price increases require locking in supply early, and work-in-progress on autos ties up heavy capital.

1.0 means "profit basically turns into cash," not yet in dangerous territory (below 0.7). But watch the direction: if Q3 drops to 0.8, inventory is piling up — that's not "stockpiling" but "can't sell."

This is the second metric I think deserves the closest watch; it warns earlier than gross margin. Gross margin is the result; inventory is the cause.

Capex: the shift to asset-heavy is irreversible

Xiaomi in the smartphone era was asset-light: capex of a few billion per year, manufacturing outsourced to contract manufacturers (Foxconn, Wingtech), with the company focused only on R&D and branding. Building cars changed this completely: Beijing Phase 1 at 150,000 units/year, Phase 2 doubling that, plus chip tape-outs and AI compute.

The implication of becoming asset-heavy: depreciation will rise every quarter, and the break-even point will move higher.Annual sales of 400,000 vehicles could break even because depreciation was not yet fully loaded; once Phase 2 reaches full capacity, fixed costs will be higher and 500,000–600,000 vehicles will be needed to break even.

That is why "the bigger the scale, the bigger the loss" appeared in 2026Q2 — not because scale is insufficient, but because scale is not yet enough to cover the added fixed costs.

Valuation: paying 28x for a business with 8.5% ROE

Buffett's principle: long-term return roughly equals ROE (if valuation stays constant). Xiaomi's ten-year average ROE of 8.5% implies that holding it long term yields annualized returns of roughly 8%–10%, plus valuation swings.

A 28x P/E embeds a requirement: net profit must compound at over 25% for the next three years to bring the valuation down to 15x. The actual growth rate in 2026 is -43%. This gap is not "slightly expensive" — it is "the direction is reversed."

What would he do? Put it in the "too hard" pile and wait on price. As he himself said: "We don't buy a company because it's good; we buy it because it's good and cheap."

Conclusion

Buffett-style conclusion: gross margin 19.8%, cash conversion 1.0, capex at a high level, ROE 8.5% — four numbers paired with a 28x P/E do not hold up. The company has no financial risk (interest-bearing debt is very low), but it has valuation risk. A reasonable buying point is 15–18x 2026E, corresponding to HKD 13–16; or 20x when profit recovers above RMB 30 billion, corresponding to HKD 25.
