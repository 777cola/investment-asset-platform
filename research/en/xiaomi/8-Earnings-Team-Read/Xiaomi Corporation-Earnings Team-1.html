Core view

The most effective way to read financial statements is to convert every number into "how much is earned per unit sold." Converted this way, Xiaomi's report looks like this: RMB 112 profit per phone, RMB 44,000 gross profit per car but a net loss of RMB 25,000, and a few dozen yuan per IoT device.

Break the big numbers down into single digits first

Revenue RMB 108.92 billion, adjusted net profit RMB 6.22 billion, net margin 5.7%. Sounds decent. Break it down by unit and it looks worse: phones RMB 42.1 billion, 8.5% gross margin, 31.85 million units, gross profit of RMB 112 per unit; autos RMB 23.9 billion, 19.2% gross margin, 104,199 vehicles, gross profit of RMB 4.4 ten-thousand yuan per vehicle, but after allocating the RMB 2.6 billion loss, a net loss of RMB 2.5 ten-thousand yuan per vehicle.

What does RMB 112 mean? Logistics, warehousing and after-sales for one phone cost roughly RMB 50–80. In other words, after deducting those, the amount that actually reaches net profit per phone may be just a few dozen yuan. That is why a USD 30 rise in memory prices can wipe out an entire quarter of phone profit.

Where did gross profit go: three simultaneous leaks

Gross profit RMB 21.57 billion, down RMB 4.52 billion year on year. Three leaks: handset tariffs (memory), about RMB 1.6 billion (handset gross profit fell from 5.2 billion to 3.6 billion, rough calculation); the auto price war, about RMB 1.6 billion (per-vehicle gross profit fell from 67,000 to 44,000 × 104,000 vehicles); the IoT scale decline, over RMB 1 billion.

None of the three leaks is unfair: memory is priced by a three-player oligopoly, and Xiaomi can only accept it; autos face a downward shift in the price-band benchmark, and refusing to cut prices means losing orders; IoT faces the phase-out of national subsidies, with demand pulled forward.Not one of the three is a management failure.

But equally, not one of them is something management can fix. This is what Duan Yongping calls the nature of a business — you can make mistakes in a good business, or run a bad business perfectly, and the outcomes differ enormously.

R&D RMB 9.2 billion: money well spent, but shareholders bear the cost first

Quarterly R&D of RMB 9.2 billion, up 18.9% YoY, or RMB 36.8 billion annualized — 1.5x 2026 adjusted net profit (about RMB 24.6 billion). The money goes into the car platform, the Xuanjie chip and AI large models.

From a business standpoint this is necessary: for a hardware company, R&D is not optional — it is the price of admission. Without in-house chips, Qualcomm sets your prices forever; without autonomous driving, the car has no differentiation. But from a shareholder's standpoint, it means profits stay suppressed for the next two years.

Duan Yongping's rule has always been "don't touch what you don't understand," but he also said "dare to be last to the world" — Xiaomi is a latecomer in cars, yet reached 100,000 units in two years, which counts as success in the "latecomer" category. The problem: the tuition a latecomer pays is usually dearer than the pioneer's. This RMB 2.6 billion quarterly loss is the tuition.

Conclusion: good product, good management, bad business

These three statements do not contradict each other. The good product is real (SU7, XRING O1, record-high ASP); the good management is real (100,000 units ramped in two years, HKD 20 billion buyback at low prices); the bad business is also real (19.8% gross margin, 5.7% net margin, 8.5% ROE).

Investing earns money from "good business × good price," not from "good product." The market routinely conflates the two—in 2025 it gave Xiaomi a 40–60x PE, buying "good product + good story"; in 2026 the multiple fell to 28x, selling "bad business."

My view: this company deserves a top spot on the watchlist, but not a buy at 28x. Wait until it proves "a bad business can still make money"—auto turning profitable, handset gross margin back to 10%—then even 25x is acceptable.

Conclusion

Duan Yongping-style conclusion: a business that earns RMB 112 per phone should not command a 28x P/E. But the company's product strength and execution deserve respect; when the industry cycle turns and it proves its auto business can fund itself, it is a buy at a reasonable price. The best move now is to wait, not to bet.
