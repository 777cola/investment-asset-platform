Four Masters — Xiaomi Corporation

Core thesis

All four frameworks lead to a rare consensus: the company qualifies, the price does not. Duan Yongping credits its integrity and execution but not the hardware business's ability to make money; Buffett asks only one question—"can it raise prices?"—and Xiaomi is cutting prices on both phones and cars; Munger notes the valuation method has shifted from "options" to "heavy assets"; Li Lu sees it as a specimen of China's manufacturing upgrade but demands a lower price.

The odds work out to roughly 1:1—HKD 25.3 neutral, HKD 12.7 bear case, HKD 40.1 bull case. 1:1 odds paired with an uncertain win rate is a gamble, not an investment.

Duan Yongping: dutiful companies, gruelling businesses

Duan Yongping looks at two things first in a company: whether the people are trustworthy, and whether the business is a good business. Xiaomi scores full marks on the first and fails the second.

The people are trustworthy. Lei Jun is obsessive about product rigor — before the SU7 launch he personally drove hundreds of thousands of kilometers in testing, and that kind of detail cannot be faked; he is honest with users, with no inflated specs; channel efficiency is pushed to the extreme, with Xiaomi Home pricing online and offline the same, earning distributors' money on turnover rather than on price spreads. These are all hallmarks of "benfen" (doing one's duty). Taking autos from zero to 10.42 million units in a single quarter in two years is not luck — it is organizational capability.

The business, however, does not hold up. Hardware is a business where "the more you sell, the more cost is tested" — it earns money from efficiency, not from pricing power. Smartphone gross margin is 8.5%; at an ASP of RMB 1,322, that is RMB 112 of gross profit per unit — a USD 30 rise in memory chip prices wipes out a quarter's smartphone profit. Autos are even more blunt: gross profit per vehicle is RMB 4.4 million, but after allocated expenses it is a loss of RMB 2.5 million.

How would he conclude? Roughly: this company deserves respect, I would buy the products, but I would not buy the stock at 28x. A good company is not the same as a good stock — price is the final gate.

Buffett: Gross margin tells you everything

Buffett has only one criterion for manufacturing: can it raise prices without losing customers? That criterion is pricing power, and it determines whether a company can thrive under inflation and cost shocks.

Xiaomi cannot. Raise phone prices and Honor, OPPO, vivo and Transsion immediately fill the gap, so its choice is "structural price increases" — lifting ASP from RMB 1,050 to RMB 1,322 by raising the mix of high-end models, not by selling the same phone for more. Autos are more obvious: deliveries +28.2%, average price -9.6%, gross margin -7.2pct, the textbook "trading price for share," and the kind of growth he dislikes most.

He would run the numbers: market cap HKD 747 billion, 2026 adjusted net profit of about RMB 24.6 billion, 28x. At the same 28x, he would rather buy a company with gross margin above 50% and predictable cash flow. In his framework, Xiaomi's problem is not "will it grow" but "can that growth become shareholders' money."

One thing would give him pause: Xiaomi has almost no interest-bearing debt and ample cash on the balance sheet, which resembles the companies he likes. But that cash is not enough to fund the car business — the HKD 42.5 billion share placement in 2025 is proof.

Munger: Why did automotive success end up killing the valuation

Munger would dissect this coldly. In 2025 the market priced Xiaomi's auto business as an "option" — big upside, losses excluded from the valuation denominator, winner takes all. In 2026 the auto business is on the books, and the market has switched to pricing it as "deliveries × per-vehicle gross profit − fixed costs." An option has become a heavy asset, so the valuation method has to change — that is not sentiment, it is arithmetic.

Worse, the quality of the auto business is deteriorating: more units sold (+28.2%), lower unit price (-9.6%), larger losses (RMB -2.6 billion vs RMB -300 million). He would say: this is not "auto success," this is "buying scale with losses." Scale can be bought; a moat cannot — because BYD can sell the same car cheaper, Huawei can sell at the same price with a higher brand premium, and Xiaomi is stuck in the middle.

He would also flag one more thing: Xiaomi is simultaneously running four businesses — phones, IoT, autos and chips — each of world-class difficulty. Munger spent his life favoring "simple, focused" businesses; diversification is an automatic markdown in his book. He would ask: if the company did only phones and IoT, would its ROE be 20% instead of 8.5%?

Li Lu: a case study in manufacturing upgrading, but buy at the right price

Li Lu takes the longest view. He does not care about next quarter's gross margin; he cares about "which industries this country has genuinely built capability in." Xiaomi is a sample he would study closely: brand, supply-chain integration, in-house chip design (Xuanjie O1 cumulative shipments above one million units), and whole-vehicle manufacturing — no more than three Chinese companies possess all four at once.

The "human-car-home" ecosystem has no second anywhere in the world — Apple has phone and home, but no car; Tesla has car, but no home or phone; Huawei has phone and car, but is constrained by chip process nodes. If this ecosystem truly works, its value lies not in hardware gross margin but in the user lifecycle.

But he would distinguish "surviving long" from "earning much." Long-term returns in manufacturing are determined by capital turnover, not by stories. ROE of 8.5%, free cash flow consumed by carmaking, 28x P/E — these three together show the market has already paid in advance for the optimistic case of "the ecosystem works."

His approach is usually this: put it on the watchlist and wait for a moment when "bad news is exhausted and a good company is marked down." Below HKD 24, corresponding to 23x 2026E, is what fits his way of buying.

Odds assessment: a 1:1 bet is not worth taking

Put the three scenarios side by side: bull HKD 40.1 (+38%), base HKD 25.3 (-13%), bear HKD 12.7 (-56%). Upside 38%, downside 56% — the risk/reward is actually negative, 1:0.68. Expected value only turns positive if enough probability is assigned to the bull case.

How should probabilities be assigned? The base case requires "handset gross margin back to 10%, auto holding at 19%" — both need memory prices to stop rising and the auto price war not to escalate, and both are exogenous variables. The bear case requires only "memory stays at highs," which is a linear extrapolation of the current trend and carries no low probability. My subjective probabilities are 35% bear, 45% base, 20% bull, giving an expected value of about HKD 22.6, 22% below the current price.

This is the answer all four of us converge on: the company isn't bad — the price is treating the bull case as the base case. Wait for below HKD 24 (23x 2026E, expected value turns positive), or wait for handset gross margin to recover sequentially for two quarters — the latter is a fundamental signal, the former a price signal, and either arriving first is fine.

Conclusion

Four masters in consensus: a good company, a hard business, a wrong price. Duan Yongping backs people over businesses, Buffett backs businesses over prices, Munger backs arithmetic over stories, and Li Lu backs trends but backs entry points more — four paths converge on the same place: 28x 2026E, negative 1:0.68 odds, expected value HKD 22.6. Rating stays "Watch, wait for handset gross margin to bottom"; two triggers to buy: handset gross margin ≥10% for two consecutive quarters, or share price ≤HKD 24.
