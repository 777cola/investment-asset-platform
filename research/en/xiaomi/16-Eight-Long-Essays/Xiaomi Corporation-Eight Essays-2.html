The essence of the business: RMB 112 profit per phone versus a loss of RMB 2.5 ten-thousand per car — Xiaomi Corporation

Core view

The cleanest way to read Xiaomi is to convert every number into unit economics: RMB 112 of gross profit per phone; RMB 44,000 of gross profit per car against a net loss of RMB 25,000; a few hundred RMB of gross profit per air conditioner; and a 70% gross margin per ad slot. Four numbers, four businesses.

Smartphones: a volume business whose thin per-unit profit leaves little buffer against shocks

2026Q2 smartphone revenue was RMB 42.1 billion with an 8.5% gross margin; shipments fell 26.5% YoY while ASP rose 25.9% YoY to a record high. Backing out from ASP, shipments were about 31.85 million units at an ASP of about RMB 1,322, implying gross profit of RMB 112 per unit.

What does that RMB 112 have to cover? Logistics, warehousing, channel rebates, after-sales and allocated system R&D. After those, only a few dozen yuan may reach net profit. That is why something as seemingly modest as "memory up USD 30" can wipe out an entire quarter of smartphone profit — USD 30 is about RMB 215, nearly twice the per-unit gross profit.

Premiumisation is real: models priced above RMB 3,000 accounted for 32.1% of domestic volume, and ASP rose from 1,050 to 1,322. But premium phones carry higher memory configurations (12GB/16GB + 512GB/1TB), so price increases hit premium models with a larger absolute cost impact — premiumisation rescues revenue, not gross profit.

Auto: a capital-intensive business where gross profit covers 64% of fixed costs

2026Q2 deliveries reached 104,199 vehicles, with revenue of RMB 23.9 billion, ASP of RMB 229,300, gross margin of 19.2% and an operating loss of RMB 2.6 billion. Three numbers stand out: gross profit per vehicle of RMB 44,000, loss per vehicle of RMB 25,000, and the RMB 69,000 gap between them representing allocated fixed costs and expenses per vehicle.

The structure tells us autos are not a "lose money on every car sold" business but a "not enough scale" business. Gross profit of RMB 44,000 covers 64% of fixed costs; breakeven comes at annual sales above 600,000 vehicles. 2026 annualised, Xiaomi is at 417,000 vehicles — still 40% short of that line.

But the line moves. ASP fell from RMB 253,700 to RMB 229,300 (-9.6%), gross profit per vehicle fell from RMB 67,000 to RMB 44,000, and breakeven was pushed from 400,000 vehicles to 600,000. Every round of price war pushes that line up again — the most dangerous feature of the auto business.

IoT vs internet services: one retreats, one props things up

IoT revenue was RMB 31.3 billion in 2026Q2, down 19.2% YoY — the steepest decline of the three segments. The rollback of national subsidies pulled forward demand, and ecosystem-chain players (Roborock, Ninebot) siphoned off share. Historically the profit stabilizer, this business is now a drag.

Internet services is the only high-margin business (historically above 70%), but it was folded into "smartphones x AIoT" this quarter, obscuring its standalone growth rate. It tracks the smartphone installed base and engagement, and with smartphone shipments down 26.5%, it cannot be doing well.

Together the four numbers tell the whole story: smartphones earn hard-won money, autos are still buying scale, IoT is ebbing, services are holding the floor. None can deliver excess profit, which is why ROE is only 8.5%.

Why this business is worth owning, but not worth buying at a high price

Worth owning, because demand is real: 1.2 billion phones and 30 million new energy vehicles a year worldwide, and Xiaomi is a top-three player in both. A company that can survive in both markets will not disappear.

Not worth buying at a high price, because what it earns is efficiency money: making the same thing cheaper, winning on scale and turnover. The long-run return on this kind of business equals ROE, and ROE is 8.5%. Paying 28x for a business with 8.5% ROE means paying a growth-stock price for the fate of a manufacturer.

Duan Yongping once said: "Business model first, management second, price third." Xiaomi's management scores 9; its business model scores only 6. A 6-point business only works if the price is cheap.

Where is the ceiling for gross margin?

A natural question: if auto scale ramps and memory prices fall back, how high can Xiaomi's gross margin go?

Take the historical best as the reference: consolidated gross margin of 22.5% in Q2 2025, combining 11.5% for phones and 26.4% for autos — a double-good year of cheap upstream costs and high auto premium. On a neutral assumption, 2027 can return to 21%–22%, but not to the 2025 level, because the baseline of the auto price band has already shifted down.

Going higher requires structural change: internet services rising from 10% to above 15% of revenue, or auto gross margin holding stably above 22%. The former needs ecosystem monetisation, the latter needs brand premium, and neither happens in a single year.

So the gross margin ceiling for this business is roughly 23%–25%, 3–5 points above the current level. On quarterly revenue of RMB 110 billion, 3 points is RMB 3.3 billion per quarter, RMB 13.2 billion a year — that is the entire upside of "everything goes right." Against a market cap of HKD 747 billion, that is not much.

Conclusion

Business essence: three segments, one model—efficiency-driven, scale wins, no pricing power. Phones earn RMB 112 per unit; autos generate RMB 44,000 gross profit per vehicle but lose RMB 25,000; IoT is receding; services underpin. Capacity utilization sets the ceiling, BOM cost sets the floor, and the company has no say at either end. Worth doing, not worth buying at 28x.
