Industry and competition: upstream collects rent, downstream fights battles — Xiaomi Corporation

Xiaomi stands at the intersection of two curves

Core view

In 2026, Xiaomi is caught between two industry cycles at once: memory is on the upswing (costs rising), while autos are mid-price-war (prices falling). Where these two curves intersect is its current gross margin.

Smartphone industry: a 1.2 billion unit installed base, with cost shocks hitting the entire industry

Global annual smartphone shipments have hovered around 1.2 billion units for five years without growth. What makes 2026 unusual is memory price inflation: DRAM contract prices up 60%–70%, NAND up more than 100%, pushing device BOM costs up 9%–15%, while retail prices cannot be raised.

The result is industry-wide gross margin compression: Samsung's handset division margin declines, Transsion is forced to raise prices in Africa and loses share, and Xiaomi's gross margin falls from 11.5% to 8.5%. This is not a Xiaomi-specific operating problem; it is a cost shock across the entire industry.

The difference lies in who can absorb it: Apple's 46% gross margin is untouched, Samsung can shrug off a few points from 35%, while Xiaomi at 8.5% hits the floor. The industry does not reward thin margins; it eliminates them.

Landscape: no single player can set prices

Samsung, Apple, Xiaomi, Transsion and OPPO/vivo together hold 70% of the market, and none exceeds 20%. In an industry with this structure, price is set by marginal capacity, not by the leader.

China's market is more clearly segmented: above RMB 6,000 it is Huawei and Apple; between RMB 3,000 and 6,000 it is a melee among Xiaomi, Honor, OPPO and vivo; below RMB 3,000 Xiaomi and Honor fight for first place. Xiaomi's 32.1% premium share comes mainly from the RMB 3,000–4,000 band, which is not yet truly "premium".

Huawei's return is structural pressure: the HarmonyOS ecosystem + in-house chips + brand premium directly suppress Xiaomi above RMB 4,000. Xiaomi's only response is "same specs, cheaper", which leads straight back to efficiency competition.

Auto industry: penetration passes 50%, price war becomes the norm

By 2026, NEV penetration in China's passenger car market will exceed 55%, and industry growth will fall from 30% to single digits. Incremental volume can only be taken from competitors, and that is the root of the price war.

Xiaomi's RMB 200,000–300,000 price band is a meat grinder: BYD Han/Tang/Sealion, Tesla Model 3/Y, Huawei AITO M5/M7, Li Auto L6, Xpeng G6/G9 and Zeekr 007/7X all compete here. ASP fell from 25.37 to 22.93 (RMB 10,000), not because Xiaomi wanted to cut prices, but because the benchmark for this price band is shifting down.

Scale threshold: Chinese automakers generally reach breakeven at annual sales of 300,000–500,000 vehicles. Xiaomi's annualized 41.7 vehicles (10,000 units) have just crossed the threshold, but gross margin has already fallen to 19.2%. This is scale bought with price, and it is not solid — stop conceding on price and deliveries fall.

The intersection of the two curves: improvement only possible in 2027

By industry convention, the memory cycle runs 4–6 quarters; prices began rising in early 2026 and will likely loosen at the earliest in the first half of 2027. Historically, the auto price war ends only when capacity clears (second-tier brands exit), on a 2–3 year timescale.

The two curves overlap, meaning the second half of 2026 through the first half of 2027 is the most painful window: upstream costs are still rising while downstream prices are still falling. Only in the second half of 2027 is a dual improvement — "memory pullback + auto capacity clearing" — likely to emerge.

The market staged a rebound in August, essentially trading the 2027 reversal in advance. But between trading ahead and the actual reversal lie two quarters of earnings reports. That is the time cost of "waiting and watching" — possibly a year, not a month.

What rivals are doing: BYD and Huawei's two paths

To assess competition, look beyond share — look at where rivals are heading.

BYD pursues "vertical integration + full price coverage": in-house batteries, motors, electronic controls and chips, with integration near 75%, which lets it hold gross margin at 18%.Its playbook is to crush rivals on cost, and Xiaomi cannot win on that dimension.

Huawei pursues "brand premium + smart-driving label": HarmonyOS Intelligent Mobility collides head-on with Xiaomi in the 20–30 price band, and Huawei holds two differentiators — brand and smart driving — leaving Xiaomi to respond only with "same specs, 3–5 cheaper."Its playbook is to overwhelm rivals with brand, and Xiaomi cannot win on that dimension either.

Xiaomi sits in the middle: costs worse than BYD, brand weaker than Huawei. Its only winnable edge is the combination of ecosystem + design + value for money.That combination works in the 20–25 price band and fails both above and below it — the industry reason its ASP keeps drifting down.

Conclusion

Sector conclusion: phones are a cost war in a saturated market, autos a price war in a growth market; neither battle generates excess profit. Xiaomi pays rent to memory on the phone side and to the price war on the auto side; the intersection of the two curves is the 8.5% and 19.2% gross margins. The industry does not reward it, only lets it survive. Dual improvement is unlikely before the second half of 2027.
