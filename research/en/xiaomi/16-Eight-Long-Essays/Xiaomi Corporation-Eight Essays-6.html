Management: Lei Jun alone carries four lines — Xiaomi Corporation

Execution 9, governance structure 5, succession 3
Research date 2026/08/21  ·  Price HK$29.02 (+4.54%)  ·  Market cap About HKD 747 billion  ·  P/E (TTM) Approximately 19.6x (2026E approximately 28x, estimated)

Core view

Xiaomi's management is both its hardest asset and its most concentrated risk. Lei Jun holds about 23% of the equity and controls more than half the voting power through dual-class shares, while serving as chairman, CEO and de facto head of the auto business. That structure is highly efficient on offense and a single point of failure on defense.

Execution: 100,000 vehicles in two years — that is hard evidence

Xiaomi announced its carmaking push in March 2021, launched the SU7 in March 2024 (over 80,000 locked orders within 24 hours), completed the SUV line-up with the YU7 in June 2025 and delivered 104,199 units in a single quarter in Q2 2026. Going from zero to 100,000 units a quarter in five years is rare in the history of the global auto industry.

Over the same period the Xuanjie O1 entered mass production, with cumulative shipments above one million chips — the first time a mainland device maker has put a self-developed SoC into a mass-production flagship. Both landing at once shows this is not luck but organisational capability.

Execution scores 9; the one point deducted is for "one person carrying too much." He himself has called cars his "last major venture" — the flip side of that line: there is no second margin for error.

Capital allocation: one correct high-sell, low-buy move

In March 2025 it placed 800 million shares at HKD 53.25, raising HKD 42.5 billion; in Q1 2026 it bought back HKD 20 billion at HKD 28–32. Within one year the same company sold high and bought low — a round of moves that earns credit — with the proceeds going into autos and chips, not into plugging holes.

Two points need to be clear, however: eight years since listing and it has never paid a dividend, so shareholder returns depend entirely on the share price; and the HKD 20 billion buyback is only 2.7% of market cap, not enough to offset the pace of profit decline.

R&D spending best illustrates the strategy: RMB 9.2 billion in a single quarter, +18.9% YoY, RMB 36.8 billion annualized, 1.5x 2026 net profit (about RMB 24.6 billion). Raising R&D 19% in a quarter when profit is falling is long-termism — and also why current-quarter profit looks ugly.

Integrity and governance: questioned three times

Added to the US CCMC list in 2021 (removed the same year); in 2022 India's Enforcement Directorate froze about RMB 4.8 billion (legal proceedings ongoing) — both were external shocks, not integrity issues.

The third is different: a high-speed SU7 crash in March 2025 killed 3 people, and the company's initial disclosure cadence drew criticism for lagging "half a beat." This exposed the fragility of the "founder is the brand" structure — Lei Jun's personal Weibo became the sole crisis-communication channel.

On governance, dual-class shares give Lei Jun over half the voting power on roughly 23% equity. The upside: long-termism is not held hostage by short-term shareholders. The downside: minority shareholders have no counterbalancing lever. HKEX imposes a ten-year sunset clause on WVR companies — put this on the risk list.

Successor: the only blank spot

Lu Weibing is clearly the number two: he joined from Gionee in 2019, took over Redmi, and has run the phone business since 2024. He is the direct executor behind the ASP rising from RMB 1,050 to RMB 1,322 and the domestic high-end share reaching 32.1%.But he is close in age to Lei Jun, and both are near retirement age—"waiting for him to succeed" does not work on timing.

Yu Liguo of the auto business is an outside hire responsible for manufacturing and supply chain, not a general-management talent; the chip business has no publicly named head.The real question is not "who succeeds Lei Jun" but "whether this one-man decision-making mechanism can be institutionalized."

Metrics to watch: whether the auto business produces a number two who can speak independently to the outside world over the next two years; whether the board adds genuine independent industry experts.If neither happens, Xiaomi's valuation should not carry a "governance premium."

How many people remain in the partnership system?

At its 2018 listing Xiaomi had 8 co-founders: Lei Jun, Lin Bin, Zhou Guangping, Liu De, Hong Feng, Wang Chuan, Huang Jiangji and Li Wanqiang. By 2026 only Lei Jun and Lin Bin remain publicly visible and actively running the business; most of the others have stepped back from day-to-day operations.

The new power structure is "Lei Jun + Lu Weibing + a group of business unit general managers": smartphones (Lu Weibing), IoT (the Zhang Feng line), autos (Yu Liguo) and chips (directly under Lei Jun). Highly efficient, but it lacks a layer of "people who can argue with Lei Jun."

Munger argued that a good board should include people who can make the CEO uncomfortable. Xiaomi's board is dominated by strategic investors and financial experts, and lacks independent directors who "understand hardware and dare to vote no." That is not a legal problem but a decision-quality problem — the successive downward shifts in 2026 auto pricing are the result of a missing dissenting voice.

Conclusion

Management verdict: execution 9/10, capital allocation 7/10, governance 5/10, succession 3/10. Lei Jun is both the biggest asset and the biggest single risk. Capital allocation got it right once—selling high and buying low—but there has been no dividend for eight years. Succession is a blank, and Lu Weibing is the same age as Lei Jun. Investment implication: execution justifies paying 10%–15% more, governance problems demand a 10% discount, and the two roughly offset—so management is not the reason to buy or sell; price is.
