## Industry-chain position: self-build upstream, bypass downstream, collect rent midstream

Eli Lilly has done three distinctive things along this chain. Upstream, it has brought peptide API and fill-finish in-house: in February 2025 it announced USD 27 billion for four US sites (three API, one injectable, to be completed by 2030), plus USD 1.7 billion for a single Concord plant, taking its US investment since 2020 above USD 50 billion—**at a stage where capacity is share, this is the hard way to shut competitors out.**

Downstream, it uses LillyDirect direct sales and telehealth (a November 2026 partnership with Hims & Hers) to bypass some PBMs. In Foundayo's early launch, 45% of prescriptions came from LillyDirect, 35% from telehealth and only 20% through traditional pharmacies.**This is not about saving money; it is about seizing the position of who touches the patient.**But while it can bypass PBMs, it cannot bypass Medicare—from 2026, weight-loss GLP-1s enter reimbursement at a net price of USD 245 per month, with the price written into the agreement.

## Five moats: four solid, one hollow

**License 10.0/10** — the hardest factor, and the most fragile: granted by law, it disappears on expiry, in May 2036. Until then, generics cannot enter, and Eli Lilly can sustain an 84% gross margin. **Brand 8/10** — in injectables, tirzepatide has already won the wording battle (Zepbound's new obesity prescriptions in the US have overtaken Wegovy), but in oral, weekly prescriptions for oral Wegovy are three times those of Foundayo; brand momentum has not carried over into the new category.

**Cost 7.0/10** — the dilution effect from in-house manufacturing is real (price -15%, gross margin +0.8pct), but heavy assets are also a burden. **Switching cost 6.0/10** — switching injectables requires renegotiating insurance and readjusting; oral drugs carry almost zero switching cost. **Network 3.0/10** — essentially absent: LillyDirect is a distribution advantage, not a network effect; one more patient does not benefit another. **Total 38/50.**

## Supply-chain risk: costs locked into the US

There is another risk on the supply-chain side that is rarely discussed: **Eli Lilly is building capacity in the US, which locks its cost structure into the US.** Four sites at USD 27 billion mean civil works, labor and compliance all priced at US rates, while competitors (peptide manufacturers in China and India) carry a depreciation base less than half as large. During the patent term this is not a problem, because the brand sets the price; once patents expire, cost becomes the only battleground, and by then Lilly's cost will be among the highest in the world.

The second risk is single-point dependence. GLP-1 fill-finish lines are specialized equipment, and a problem on one line puts billions of dollars of revenue at stake. Lilly spreads this risk through a "multiple sites, multiple regions" approach, but all four sites will not be fully operational until 2030, making 2027–2029 the tightest capacity window.**Any supply disruption during this period will be traded by the market as demand peaking.**

## How risk is allocated along the chain

Look again at "who is bearing the risk for Eli Lilly." Upstream API suppliers earn 30%–40% gross margins on multi-year contracts; once capacity is built, it must be bought. CDMOs earn 35%–45%, likewise on long-term contracts. **Eli Lilly has shifted part of the capacity risk upstream and retained part itself (the four self-built sites).** This "long-term contracts + self-build" combination is a moat when demand rises and double the fixed cost when demand falls.

Compare Novo: it pursued "self-build first + aggressive expansion," and in 2026 insufficient capacity utilization showed up directly as price cuts to defend share. Eli Lilly is on the same path, just two years later and at smaller scale. **If industry capacity is 30% oversupplied in 2028, both will suffer, but Eli Lilly's balance sheet is stronger—interest-bearing debt under USD 30 billion and interest coverage above 50x, so it can withstand it.**

## The most dangerous rival: not Novo, but the payer

Novo Nordisk's threat is out in the open: it started the price war because roughly 75% of semaglutide revenue rests on a single molecule, its China patent expires in 2026, and Zepbound is taking injectable share, leaving it no option but to cut prices to hold volume. The harder it cuts, the harder Eli Lilly's pricing is to defend. **But this competition is predictable and priceable.**

What is genuinely unpriceable is the payer. A Medicare net price of USD 245/month, a USD 50 patient out-of-pocket cap, and TrumpRx launching in January 2026 — these terms are not the product of market negotiation but of political decisions. Eli Lilly's role in them is that of a taker. **For a company with 84% gross margin, the real fear is not a rival taking 5 points of share but a payer cutting 20 points off the price in one stroke.** The -29% overseas pricing in Q3 was the preview.
