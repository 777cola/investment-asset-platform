## Initial screen: six of twenty retained

Step one is a whole-market scan. Roughly twenty innovative drugmakers have a global market cap above USD 5 billion. A rough screen on three criteria: 1) has a GLP-1 or metabolic-space asset; 2) gross margin above 60%; 3) revenue has not declined for four consecutive quarters. Six of the twenty pass all three: Eli Lilly, Novo Nordisk, Amgen, Pfizer, Merck and Innovent Biologics.

Of the fourteen eliminated, three types are most typical. The first is "has drugs but no metabolic assets" — several oncology-focused names, whose gross margins are irrelevant to this space no matter how high. The second is "has assets but too early" — preclinical or Phase I biotechs betting on a single binary outcome, which do not fall within the "first-tier company" category. The third is "revenue declining" — including several established pharma companies whose patent cliffs have already hit; their low P/E is a value trap, not cheapness.

## Second screen: three hard criteria eliminate four companies

Screen the six again against three criteria: ① metabolic revenue exceeds 30% of total, proof this is the core battlefield rather than an option; ② commercialised products in both the oral and injectable lines; ③ room to grow after discounting market cap and pipeline.

Merck fails the first outright—it has no core GLP-1 asset and the Keytruda patent cliff is imminent; a 10x P/E is the market pricing in falling revenue, not cheapness. Pfizer and Amgen fail the second—oral GLP-1 remains in clinical trials and will not launch before 2027, so buying them means buying an option. Innovent passes the first and third, but mazdutide is largely a China product and the ceiling is set by centralized procurement—a regional champion, not a global leader. That leaves Lilly and Novo, both with injectable plus oral and both centred on metabolic disease.

## Final pick: why Eli Lilly, and why not buy now

Eli Lilly wins on three fronts. Growth: Q3 revenue +38.6%, while Novo posted negative growth. Share: the global weight-loss market splits 60% vs 40% and is still widening — Zepbound has already overtaken Wegovy in new US obesity prescriptions. Pipeline: retatrutide, a triple agonist, delivered 20.8%–25% weight loss at 80 weeks in Phase III; the company plans to file in Q1 2027, making it the mainstay of the 2030s, while Novo's key backup (CagriSema) lags on progress.

Novo wins on one front: price. A 10.6x P/E reflects a blended valuation of "declining revenue + patent cliff + oral leadership." If oral Wegovy holds up, 10.6x is cheap; if it doesn't, 10.6x gets even cheaper. A textbook case of "cheap for a reason."

The funnel's conclusion therefore comes in two parts: within this space, buy Eli Lilly (quality first), but Lilly's current price has already paid in full for "quality first." 48.2x vs 10.6x — a 4.5x gap within the same industry. The market is not unaware that Lilly is better; it has priced "better" too fully. Final pick: Eli Lilly, rating under review.

## Why not choose the cheaper Novo

Some will ask: if Novo trades at only 10.6x, why not pick Novo? Because there are two kinds of low P/E: one where the market is wrong, and one where the company is about to be wrong. Novo is the latter. Semaglutide is about 75% of revenue, its China patent expires in 2026, Zepbound is taking injectable share, and the only way to defend volume is to cut price — first-quarter 2026 net sales fell 4% YoY. A company like this keeps a low P/E until revenue stops declining.

Eli Lilly is the mirror image: its P/E stays high until growth stops rising. Both are single-molecule companies; the difference is that one molecule is in its upcycle and the other is in its defence phase. The funnel screens for quality, so it picks Eli Lilly; if we ever want to play a distressed turnaround, Novo is the target — but that is a different framework.
