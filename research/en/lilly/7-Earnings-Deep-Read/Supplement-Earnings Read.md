## Layer one: revenue mix — one molecule supports two-thirds

Tirzepatide (Mounjaro USD 10.723 billion + Zepbound USD 5.352 billion) totaled USD 16.075 billion, 65.9% of revenue, up another 8.6 percentage points from 57.3% a year earlier. The first nine months cumulated to USD 43.768 billion. **This concentration is a double-edged sword**: the good news is the moat is deep enough that no one can breach it; the bad news is the entire company's fate rests on a single molecule.

The structure of the remaining 34% needs a clear look: other products totaled USD 8.138 billion, up only +8.4% year on year (derived). In other words, **Eli Lilly's business outside tirzepatide has slowed to growth rates typical of a mature pharma company**. Verzenio, Taltz and Kisunla are all solid in their respective fields, but they "hold the base," not "drive growth." There is only one growth engine.

A comparison with Novo makes it more tangible: Novo stakes about 75% of revenue on semaglutide, Lilly stakes 65.9% on tirzepatide—**both are single-molecule companies, except Lilly's molecule is younger and still growing above +50%.** This is not an industry of "diversifying risk"; it is an industry of "betting on the right molecule."

## Second layer: gross profit — a 15% price cut still lifts margin by 0.8 percentage points

This is the most counterintuitive point this quarter. Company-wide realized price fell -15%, yet non-GAAP gross margin rose from 83.6% to 84.4%. There is only one reason: **production dilution.** Peptide manufacturing carries extremely high fixed costs (fermentation tanks, purification lines, fill-finish lines), so every step up in capacity utilization pulls unit cost down. The dilution from +53% volume growth outweighed the -15% price erosion.

But this mechanism has limits: dilution is one-time, while price cuts persist. Once capacity utilization approaches full and prices keep falling, gross margin will start to decline. **I prefer to read 84.4% as "the top of this cycle" rather than "the new normal"**—modeling 82% for 2027 and 79% for 2028 is the more prudent approach.

There is also a structural angle: gross margin is a weighted average. US revenue was RMB 15.1 billion with prices down only 7%; overseas revenue was RMB 9.3 billion with prices down 29%. The higher the overseas mix, the worse the overall gross margin looks. This quarter overseas growth (+47%) outpaced the US (+34%), lifting the overseas mix from 36% to 38%—**if this trend continues, overall gross margin will be gradually dragged down even if US prices hold. This is the most easily overlooked slope in the financials.**

## Tier 3: Costs and profit — where the 23-percentage-point gap lies

Revenue +38.6%, net profit +16% — a 22.6 percentage point gap. Breaking it down: a one-time charge of in-process R&D from the USD 4.2 billion acquisition, which at 941 million shares knocked off USD 4.57 per share; without it, Non-GAAP EPS would have been the low end of the USD 39.57–40.57 range rather than USD 35.00. The second piece is selling expenses — LillyDirect direct-to-consumer, telehealth partnerships (with Hims & Hers in November), and physician education for the oral drug all cost money.

The third piece is R&D. Lilly's R&D expense ratio has long stood above 15%; Phase 3 trials for retatrutide, indication expansion for the oral drug (sleep apnea), and the Alzheimer's rollout all require spending. **Together these three pieces explain why a company with an 84% gross margin runs a net margin of just 26.4%.** This is not inefficiency — in this therapeutic area, defending your position requires sustained investment.

## Tier 4: Cash flow and balance sheet — free cash flow consumed by capex

Operating cash flow roughly matches net income (on a rough calculation), showing profits are real money. But capex intensity has reached over 20% of revenue, squeezing free cash flow to 0.6–0.7x net income. The USD 27 billion across four sites and the USD 1.7 billion single plant in Concord will gradually turn into depreciation over 2027–2030.

The balance sheet is clean: interest-bearing debt under USD 30 billion, interest coverage above 50x, ROE of 74.6% in 2024.**In other words, Eli Lilly can easily do three things at once—expand capacity, make acquisitions, pay dividends—without borrowing.**This is where it has more room than Novo Nordisk: Novo must pay for both idle capacity and price cuts, while Lilly's cash flow still covers expansion.

Only one risk to watch: if volume growth falls from 39% to below 15% in 2027–2028, utilization of the new capacity will decline, and depreciation will become a drag on gross margin.**That day won't give advance notice—it will only show up in the "gross margin quarter-on-quarter" line.**

## Five questions to ask after reading this earnings report

**First question: did this quarter's beat come from volume or price?** Volume. Company-wide sales volume rose 53% and price fell 15%; the 7.9% revenue beat versus consensus came entirely from volume. **Second question: is volume growth sustainable?** US volume rose 41% and overseas 76%; the overseas surge rests on a 29% price cut and is unsustainable. The US 41% is healthier, but it has held near 40% for four straight quarters, meaning the base is widening and growth will naturally decelerate. **Third question: is the gross margin improvement efficiency or accounting?** Efficiency (output dilution), but it works only once. **Fourth question: is profit growth lagging revenue a one-off or a trend?** This time it is a one-off (USD 4.2 billion acquisition cost); if it were a trend, the tell would be the selling expense ratio, which did not deteriorate noticeably this quarter. **Fifth question: what did management clarify, and what did it sidestep?** It clarified revenue guidance and capacity; it sidestepped 2027 guidance and the ramp schedule for the oral drug.
