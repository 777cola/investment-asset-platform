## Layer one: revenue mix — one molecule supports two-thirds

Tirzepatide (Mounjaro USD 10.723 billion plus Zepbound USD 5.352 billion) generated USD 16.075 billion, 65.9% of revenue, up a further 8.6pp from 57.3% a year earlier. The first nine months totalled USD 43.768 billion. That concentration cuts both ways: the moat is deep enough that no one can breach it, but the entire company's fate now rests on a single molecule.

The composition of the remaining 34% warrants a closer look: other products totalled USD 8.138 billion, up just +8.4% YoY (derived). In other words, Eli Lilly's non-tirzepatide business has slowed to growth rates typical of a mature pharma company. Verzenio, Taltz and Kisunla are all solid franchises in their respective fields, but they hold the base rather than drive growth. There is only one growth engine.

The comparison with Novo makes this tangible: Novo derives about 75% of revenue from semaglutide, Lilly 65.9% from tirzepatide—both are single-molecule companies, except Lilly's molecule is younger and still growing above +50%. This is not an industry that diversifies risk; it is one that bets on the right molecule.

## Second layer: gross profit — a 15% price cut still drives 0.8pp of margin expansion

This is the quarter's most counterintuitive point. Realised price fell -15% company-wide, yet non-GAAP gross margin rose from 83.6% to 84.4%. There is only one explanation: production dilution. Peptide manufacturing carries extremely high fixed costs (fermentation tanks, purification lines, fill-finish lines), so every step up in capacity utilisation pulls unit cost down. Dilution from +53% volume growth outweighed the -15% price erosion.

The mechanism has limits, however: dilution is one-time, while price cuts persist. Once capacity utilisation approaches full and prices keep falling, gross margin will start to decline. We prefer to read 84.4% as "the top of this cycle" rather than "the new normal"—modelling 82% for 2027 and 79% for 2028 is the more prudent approach.

There is also a structural angle: gross margin is a weighted average. US revenue was RMB 15.1 billion with prices down only 7%; overseas revenue was RMB 9.3 billion with prices down 29%. The higher the overseas mix, the worse the overall gross margin looks. This quarter overseas growth (+47%) outpaced the US (+34%), lifting the overseas mix from 36% to 38%—if this trend continues, overall gross margin will be gradually dragged down even if US prices hold. This is the most easily overlooked slope in the financials.

## Tier 3: Cost and profit — the source of the 23-percentage-point gap

Revenue +38.6% against net profit +16% — a 22.6 percentage point gap. Three things explain it. First, a one-time in-process R&D charge tied to the USD 4.2 billion acquisition, which across 941 million shares cost USD 4.57 per share; excluding it, Non-GAAP EPS would have landed at the low end of the USD 39.57–40.57 range rather than USD 35.00. Second, selling expenses: LillyDirect direct-to-consumer, telehealth partnerships (with Hims & Hers in November) and physician education for the oral drug all cost money.

Third, R&D. Lilly's R&D expense ratio has long stood above 15%, and Phase 3 trials for retatrutide, indication expansion for the oral drug (sleep apnea) and the Alzheimer's rollout all require spending. Together these three explain why a company with an 84% gross margin runs a net margin of just 26.4%. This is not inefficiency — in this therapeutic area, defending your position requires sustained investment.

## Tier 4: Cash flow and balance sheet — capex absorbs free cash flow

Operating cash flow roughly matches net income on a rough calculation, confirming that profits are real cash. Capex intensity, however, has reached over 20% of revenue, compressing free cash flow to 0.6–0.7x net income. The USD 27 billion across four sites and the USD 1.7 billion single plant in Concord will gradually convert into depreciation over 2027–2030.

The balance sheet is clean: interest-bearing debt under USD 30 billion, interest coverage above 50x, ROE of 74.6% in 2024.In other words, Eli Lilly can easily do three things at once—expand capacity, make acquisitions, pay dividends—without borrowing.This is where it has more room than Novo Nordisk: Novo must fund both idle capacity and price cuts, while Lilly's cash flow still covers expansion.

Only one risk to watch: if volume growth falls from 39% to below 15% in 2027–2028, utilization of the new capacity will decline and depreciation will become a drag on gross margin.That day won't give advance notice—it will only show up in the "gross margin quarter-on-quarter" line.

## Five questions to ask after reading this earnings report

First question: did this quarter's beat come from volume or price? Volume. Group-wide sales volume rose 53% while price fell 15%, so the 7.9% revenue beat versus consensus came entirely from volume. Second question: is volume growth sustainable? US volume rose 41% and overseas 76%; the overseas surge rests on a 29% price cut and is unsustainable. The US 41% is healthier, but it has held near 40% for four straight quarters, which means the base is widening and growth will naturally decelerate. Third question: is the gross margin improvement efficiency or accounting? Efficiency (output dilution), but it works only once. Fourth question: is profit growth lagging revenue a one-off or a trend? This time a one-off (USD 4.2 billion acquisition cost); if it were a trend, the tell would be the selling expense ratio, which did not deteriorate noticeably this quarter. Fifth question: what did management clarify, and what did it sidestep? It clarified revenue guidance and capacity; it sidestepped 2027 guidance and the ramp schedule for the oral drug.
