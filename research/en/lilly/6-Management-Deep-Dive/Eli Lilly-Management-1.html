Management — Eli Lilly

Core thesis

Lilly's management is "engineer-type" rather than "deal-type": CEO David Ricks joined in 1996, rose through sales and regional markets, and took over in 2017. What best captures its character this quarter: revenue guidance was raised by USD 5.5 billion, while EPS guidance was cut because of USD 4.2 billion in acquired in-process R&D expense, without propping up a number to support the share price. Marks are deducted for capital allocation leaning increasingly on M&A and for a completely blank succession plan.

Capital allocation: where the money goes matters more than rhetoric

Over the past three years, Eli Lilly's money has flowed mainly to three places: capacity, M&A, shareholder returns. Capacity is the largest—in February 2025 it announced at least USD 27 billion to build four sites in the US (three for API, one for injectables, completed before 2030), plus an additional USD 1.7 billion single plant in Concord to be completed in 2027, bringing Lilly's total US investment since 2020 to more than USD 50 billion. This is the largest single manufacturing commitment in the history of US pharma, a bet that "GLP-1 demand over the next decade can fill this capacity."

M&A is the second big bucket. In 2025 it acquired Verve Therapeutics (base editing, about USD 1.3 billion) and Adverum Biotechnologies (ophthalmic gene therapy, about USD 225 million); in Q3 2026 it recognized about USD 4.2 billion of acquired in-process R&D expense in one go, directly cutting EPS by USD 4.57.The hallmark of such deals: expensed in the current period, returns three to five years out. The upside is no goodwill buildup; the downside is that quarterly profit is disturbed every time it "buys pipeline."

Shareholder returns: symbolic, not the primary return channel

Eli Lilly pays an annual dividend of about USD 4.5 per share, a 0.3% yield at USD 1,411.95 — a token payout that sustains the consecutive dividend-increase record, not a cash return to shareholders. Buybacks are modest too, mainly offsetting equity-incentive dilution (share count down from 950 million to 941 million over five years, -1%).

That policy is right for a growth phase: the marginal return on investing in capacity and pipeline far exceeds buying back stock at 48x PE. Conversely, if management ever launches large-scale buybacks, that is a signal — they see fewer growth opportunities. No such signal today.

Incentive structure: you get what you measure

Incentive structure drives behavior. Over 80% of Ricks's compensation package is stock and performance shares, with metrics typically "revenue growth + EPS + pipeline milestones" and rarely a "relative shareholder return" clause. That explains why he is willing to spend USD 27 billion on capacity — revenue and share are measured; return on capital is not. For a growth stock this incentive is appropriate, but if the company ever enters maturity, the same incentive set will encourage blind expansion.

Another detail: Eli Lilly does not write "M&A spend" into executive long-term incentives, but the revenue increment from acquisitions rolls into the next year's assessment base — a hidden channel encouraging M&A. The metric that truly matters is "ROIIC three years post-acquisition," and the company does not disclose it.

Track record: three guidance raises vs one EPS cut

The 2026 revenue guidance was raised three times: from the start of the year to 85–87 billion, maintained mid-year, then lifted directly on October 29 to USD 91–92 billion (midpoint +5.5 billion). In the same earnings report, Non-GAAP EPS guidance was cut from 35.50–36.50 to 35.00–36.00. Raising one while cutting the other is the move that best reveals management's character—those looking to dress up the statements would have moved the USD 4.2 billion acquisition cost into "one-time items" and excluded it; Lilly chose to let it flow into Non-GAAP.

There are two points of contention. First, drug-pricing politics: in November 2025 it reached an MFN agreement with the White House—Medicare net price of USD 245/month, patient out-of-pocket cap of USD 50—and TrumpRx launched in January 2026. This was passive compromise, not voluntary concession, but Lilly accepted it and did not litigate. Second, on November 9 the FDA required GLP-1 labels to add the NAION eye-disease risk; the company complied and added the warning without argument. In regulatory relations, Lilly has consistently been "cooperative" rather than "confrontational," which is a plus in the pharmaceutical industry.

Succession: the biggest governance gap

Ricks turns nearly 60 in 2026. Public information shows no succession plan, nor has the COO role been explicitly designated as the heir apparent. That is not unusual among large US pharma companies — the stock has risen more than tenfold during his tenure, and the board has no incentive to replace him — but for long-term shareholders it is a real risk: the company's strategy (building capacity in-house + buying pipelines + direct sales channels) depends heavily on the path forged during his tenure, and a change of leadership could bring wobble.

The indicators to watch are specific: if a new "President and COO" appointment appears in the next 12–24 months, or if business heads other than the CFO begin communicating externally on their own, that is a signal the succession process has started. Currently there is none.

Conclusion

Management: 8.0. Positives: honest guidance (revenue raised, EPS cut), willingness to commit heavily to capacity, regulatory cooperation, no dual-class structure. Negatives: capital allocation increasingly reliant on M&A (expensed in the period, returns lag three to five years), no succession plan, and a 0.3% dividend yield that holds no appeal for income investors. This management team will not wreck a good business, but do not expect them to hit the brakes for you at 48x PE.
