## Capital allocation: where the money goes matters more than rhetoric

Over the past three years, Eli Lilly's cash has gone mainly to three places: capacity, M&A and shareholder returns. Capacity is the largest. In February 2025 the company announced at least USD 27 billion to build four US sites (three for API, one for injectables, all completed before 2030), plus a further USD 1.7 billion single plant in Concord due in 2027, taking Lilly's total US investment since 2020 to more than USD 50 billion. That is the largest single manufacturing commitment in the history of US pharma — a bet that "GLP-1 demand over the next decade can fill this capacity."

M&A is the second big bucket. In 2025 Lilly acquired Verve Therapeutics (base editing, about USD 1.3 billion) and Adverum Biotechnologies (ophthalmic gene therapy, about USD 225 million); in 2026Q3 it recognised about USD 4.2 billion of acquired in-process R&D expense in one go, directly cutting EPS by USD 4.57.The hallmark of such deals: expensed in the current period, returns three to five years out. The upside is no goodwill buildup; the downside is that quarterly profit is disturbed every time it "buys pipeline."

## Shareholder returns: symbolic rather than the primary return channel

Eli Lilly pays an annual dividend of about USD 4.5 per share, a 0.3% yield at USD 1,411.95 — a token payout that sustains the consecutive dividend-increase record rather than a cash return to shareholders. Buybacks are modest too, mainly offsetting equity-incentive dilution (share count down from 950 million to 941 million over five years, -1%).

That policy suits a growth phase: the marginal return on investing in capacity and pipeline far exceeds buying back stock at 48x P/E. Conversely, if management ever launches large-scale buybacks, that is a signal — they see fewer growth opportunities. No such signal today.

## Incentive structure: you get what you measure

Incentives drive behaviour. Over 80% of Ricks's compensation is stock and performance shares, with metrics typically built around "revenue growth + EPS + pipeline milestones" and rarely a "relative shareholder return" clause. That explains his willingness to spend USD 27 billion on capacity: revenue and share are measured, return on capital is not. For a growth stock this incentive is appropriate, but should the company ever enter maturity, the same incentive set will encourage blind expansion.

Note also that Eli Lilly does not write "M&A spend" into executive long-term incentives, yet revenue added by acquisitions rolls into the next year's assessment base — a hidden channel encouraging M&A. The metric that truly matters is "ROIIC three years post-acquisition," and the company does not disclose it.

## Track record: three guidance raises vs one EPS cut

2026 revenue guidance was raised three times: from the initial range at the start of the year to 85–87 billion, held at mid-year, then lifted outright on October 29 to USD 91–92 billion (midpoint +5.5 billion). In the same earnings report, Non-GAAP EPS guidance was cut from 35.50–36.50 to 35.00–36.00. Raising one while cutting the other is the move that best reveals management's character—those looking to dress up the statements would have moved the USD 4.2 billion acquisition cost into "one-time items" and excluded it; Lilly chose to let it flow into Non-GAAP.

Two points of contention stand out. First, drug-pricing politics: in November 2025 the company reached an MFN agreement with the White House—Medicare net price of USD 245/month, patient out-of-pocket cap of USD 50—and TrumpRx launched in January 2026. This was passive compromise, not voluntary concession, but Lilly accepted it and did not litigate. Second, on November 9 the FDA required GLP-1 labels to add the NAION eye-disease risk; the company complied and added the warning without argument. In regulatory relations, Lilly has consistently been "cooperative" rather than "confrontational," which is a plus in the pharmaceutical industry.

## Succession: the biggest governance gap

Ricks turns nearly 60 in 2026. Public disclosures show no succession plan, and the COO role has not been explicitly designated as the heir apparent. That is not unusual among large US pharma companies — the stock has risen more than tenfold during his tenure and the board has no incentive to replace him — but for long-term shareholders it is a real risk: the company's strategy (building capacity in-house + buying pipelines + direct sales channels) depends heavily on the path forged during his tenure, and a change of leadership could bring wobble.

The signals to watch are specific: a new "President and COO" appointment within the next 12–24 months, or business heads other than the CFO beginning to communicate externally in their own right, would indicate that the succession process has started. Currently there is none.
