Core view

The industry structure is clear: Eli Lilly 60%, Novo Nordisk 40%, and both are cutting prices. Demand penetration remains low, yet pricing and capacity have already run ahead. That is the biggest perception gap of this cycle—and exactly where Munger's "invert, always invert" deserves the closest attention.

Landscape: duopoly, both cutting prices

The industry's current structure in one sentence: a duopoly with a combined share above 95%, yet both players are cutting prices. In Q1 2026, Eli Lilly held 60% of the global GLP-1 weight-loss market and Novo Nordisk 40%. Over the first nine months, semaglutide sold USD 26.864 billion globally and tirzepatide USD 43.768 billion — the gap at the single-product level has widened to 1.6x.

Leading on share does not mean leading on profit. Novo Nordisk's Q1 2026 net sales fell 4% YoY; Wegovy injection ceded the lead to Zepbound, leaving the company to defend its position with oral Wegovy and price cuts. It has staked roughly 75% of semaglutide revenue on a single molecule, while the Chinese semaglutide patent expires in 2026 and generics are lining up. Novo Nordisk is in a more dangerous position than Eli Lilly, so it is cutting prices harder — that is the engine of the industry's price war.

The first year of oral GLP-1s: Novo wins round one

2026 is the year of oral GLP-1s. Oral Wegovy launched in the US in January; Foundayo (orforglipron) was approved in April as the world's second oral GLP-1 weight-loss drug and the first small molecule that need not be taken on an empty stomach. Q3 results: oral Wegovy USD 811 million, 183,000 weekly prescriptions; Foundayo USD 184 million, 63,000 weekly prescriptions. Novo won the first round on brand continuity and insurance access, not on efficacy.

Looking ahead, competition shifts from "who cuts more weight" to "who keeps patients on treatment for a full year." Oral Wegovy requires fasting dosing and 30 minutes without food, a ritual that erodes adherence in the real world. Foundayo can be taken at any time, but its convenience edge will take 12–24 months to show up in retention rates. Munger would say: the moat in this business is not the molecule, it is whether patients can stick with it — and sticking with it is a habit problem, and habits are fed by channel and convenience.

Cycle position: early-stage demand, mid-stage pricing, mid-to-late-stage capacity

How do you judge where we are in the cycle? Demand is early-stage, price is mid-stage, capacity is mid-to-late stage. More than 100 million obese Americans are eligible for medication, but only a few million actually take it — penetration sits in the single digits. Yet price has already entered a downtrend (MFN agreements, Medicare at USD 245/month, TrumpRx). On capacity, Eli Lilly is spending USD 27 billion and Novo Nordisk is expanding tenfold, with ramp-ups concentrated in 2027–2029 that will likely turn shortage into major oversupply.

These three curves are not at the same point, and that is the biggest perception gap this cycle. The market focuses on demand penetration, which is still very low; my concern is price and capacity, which are already running ahead. If global peptide capacity exceeds demand by 30% in 2028, what happens that year is something we should be thinking about now. Every historical pharma cycle in which "capacity was king" ended in oversupply.

Conclusion

Score the industry 8 out of 10: demand is real, the competitive structure is stable, and unit economics are thinning. The Munger-style conclusion: this is a good track, but the good days on a good track are not in 2026 — they come after the price war ends and capacity clears out. Eli Lilly is very likely a winner that survives to that day; the problem is that its current price already discounts a "winner takes all" outcome.
