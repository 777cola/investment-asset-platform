Risk Assessment (Li Lu's perspective) — Eli Lilly

Ranked by probability × impact, competition ranks below the most dangerous factor

Core view

There are five levels of risk: valuation comes first, the payer second, and competition only third. The market debates "who takes share" daily, but the real damage comes from two things: "someone else sets the price" and "48x leaves no margin for error."

Tier one: valuation — the only slow knife

Rank risks rather than list them. On a probability × impact basis, Eli Lilly's first tier contains a single item: valuation itself. At 48x TTM and 39.8x 2026 guidance EPS, the multiple assumes 39% growth persists for at least two years before decelerating smoothly. One quarter of revenue 5% below consensus compresses the multiple from 48x to 38x and the stock falls 20% — pure valuation risk, with no bearing on how good the company is.

The second tier is payers. The MFN agreement takes effect in 2026: Medicare net price USD 245/month, patient out-of-pocket cap USD 50, TrumpRx launching in January. Overseas is harsher still, with Q3 pricing -29%. Price is the one variable this company cannot control, and it determines how long the 84.4% gross margin holds.

One further risk is easily overlooked: single-molecule dependence. Tirzepatide accounts for 65.9% of revenue, USD 43.768 billion in the first nine months. Any clinical, regulatory or supply mishap for this one molecule hits the core of a USD 1.33 trillion market cap directly — and such mishaps are unpredictable. That is what concentration really means.

Second and third tiers: competition demands clarity on which battle is being fought

The third tier is competition, but the key is to distinguish which battle, if lost, is fatal. Lilly has won the injectable battle, with Zepbound's new US prescriptions overtaking Wegovy's; for now it has lost the oral battle (184 million vs 811 million, a threefold prescription gap), and oral is the growth engine from 2028 onward—losing oral is tantamount to dismantling the bridge for the transition after the 2036 patent expiry. Analysts have already cut Foundayo's 2026 revenue forecast from USD 3 billion to USD 1.3 billion, and that downgrade cycle is not over.

The fourth tier is capacity and impairment. Four sites under the USD 27 billion program will be completed before 2030, with the Concord single plant finishing in 2027. If growth falls below 15% in 2027–2028, these fixed assets will become a depreciation burden, and gross margin sliding from 84% toward 75% is not out of the question.

Tier 4 and Tier 5: the end of capacity and patent terms

The fifth tier is the long-term structure: the US patent cliff in May 2036, Chinese generics and improved new drugs, and the FDA's newly added NAION eye warning (on November 9 it required Mounjaro, Zepbound, Ozempic and Wegovy to add a label warning). None of this affects cash flow today, but all of it is compressing the imaginative headroom for a "patent-period premium."

The Li Lu-style question is not "will it fall" but "in ten years, will this company still be able to earn as much money as it does today." My answer: it will earn a lot, but unit profit will certainly be thinner. So the reasonable multiple for it should be 25–30x, not 48x. The most dangerous item on the risk list is the assumption itself that "all risks fail to materialize at the same time."

Conclusion

Risk score: 3 out of 10 (the lower the score, the higher the risk). At the company level it is nearly unassailable; at the price level it is all risk. If you remember only one thing, make it this: Eli Lilly's risk is not "will Novo beat it," but "how much is the market willing to pay for a race that has already been won."
