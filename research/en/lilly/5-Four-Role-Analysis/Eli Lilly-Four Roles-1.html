Business analysis (Duan Yongping's perspective) — Eli Lilly

What drives profitability here, and will the business still be around in ten years?
Research date 2026/11/09  ·  Price $1,411.95 (+4.28%)  ·  Market cap About USD 1.33 trillion  ·  PE(TTM) Approximately 48x

Core view

A good business, and precisely the kind Duan Yongping likes most: demand is physiological, repeat purchases happen automatically, and customers do not need to be re-convinced every year. The core risk is not competition but the payer — prices are negotiated by PBMs and health insurers, not set by Eli Lilly.

Demand: reframe willpower as a pharmacological problem

What Eli Lilly sells is not a drug but “not having to fight yourself.” Obesity and type 2 diabetes were long put down to willpower; GLP-1 turned them into a pharmacological problem—one injection, appetite falls, weight comes off. The value of that shift: patients no longer blame themselves for “not being able to control their mouth,” and paying becomes a dignified act. That is the rarest thing on the demand side, worth more than the efficacy itself.

The unit economics are simple: tirzepatide starts at USD 149 per month at the US self-pay price (Foundayo is priced the same), the Medicare channel nets USD 245 per month, and commercial insurance channels are higher. Peptide API cost is a low share of the selling price, so gross margin reaches 84.4%. The real cost is not production but customer acquisition and channel—selling expenses, PBM rebates and insurance negotiations consume more than half of gross profit.

Repeat purchases and channel: LillyDirect bypasses PBMs

Repeat purchases are the heart of this business. Weight-loss drugs are not taken for three months and then stopped: stopping causes rebound, and patients know it. Once a drug works, prescriptions continue year after year at USD 1,800–3,000 a year. That explains why tirzepatide, three years after launch and already annualising above USD 60 billion, can still grow 57% YoY. Not because new patients are flooding in, but because existing patients have not left and new ones keep adding.

On distribution, Eli Lilly has done two things differently. First, LillyDirect direct sales, letting patients bypass PBMs and buy straight from the manufacturer. Second, a November 2026 partnership with telehealth platform Hims & Hers, plugging Foundayo into an entry point with a large self-pay weight-loss population. In Foundayo's early launch, 45% of prescriptions came from LillyDirect, 35% from telehealth and only 20% from traditional pharmacies. This is using the internet to bypass traditional prescribing inertia, betting on "convenience = adherence = retention".

Soft spot: margins set by third parties

The soft spot is equally clear: margins are set by third parties. US pricing is not Eli Lilly's to decide — it is negotiated between PBMs and insurers. The November 2025 MFN agreement with the White House pushed the Medicare net price down to USD 245 per month, with a patient out-of-pocket cap of USD 50, and TrumpRx launches in January 2026. Overseas is more direct still: European national health systems negotiate prices down outright, and overseas pricing fell 29% in Q3.

The essence of the business, then: monetise "physiological demand" as hard as possible within a nine-year patent window, while hoping payers don't cut prices too quickly. In Q3, company-wide volume rose 53%, price fell 15% and revenue rose 38.6% — a mix that shows the company is still in the sweet spot of trading price for volume, but sweet spots have expiry dates.

Conclusion

This business scores 10 out of 10 — not because it makes a lot of money, but because the way it makes money is clean: it solves a real problem, repeat purchases need no selling, and growth does not rely on subsidies. But "good business" and "good price" are two different things, and at USD 1,411.95 the price has already collected the value of the good business in advance.
