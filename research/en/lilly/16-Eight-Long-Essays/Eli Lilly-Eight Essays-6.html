Part Six: Management — Eli Lilly

The CEO Grown from Within Got Three Long-Term Decisions Right

Core view

Ricks joined Eli Lilly in 1996 and became CEO in 2017. Three key decisions—betting on dual-target drugs, building manufacturing capacity in-house, and going direct to sales channels—were all correct and each took five years to pay off. Most impressive was the guidance revision that raised revenue but cut EPS—he did not prop up the numbers to support the share price.

Three long-term decisions

David Ricks joined Lilly in 1996, starting in U.S. sales and marketing, and became CEO in 2017. He is a CEO grown from within, not a parachuted professional manager—which matters in pharma, because pipeline judgment requires long-accumulated knowledge; outsiders cannot tell which molecule is worth betting on.

He made three key decisions during his tenure: ① betting on tirzepatide (doubling down on the dual agonist even with semaglutide already in the lead); ② building manufacturing capacity in-house (USD 27 billion, betting that "capacity is share"); ③ direct sales channels (LillyDirect + telehealth, bypassing some PBMs). All three look right in hindsight, and none could have paid off in the short term.

The most remarkable guidance

Nothing reveals character more clearly than a move that does not court the market. In Q3 2026, revenue guidance was raised from 85–87 billion to 91–92 billion (midpoint +5.5 billion), while the same earnings report cut Non-GAAP EPS guidance from 35.50–36.50 to 35.00–36.00, driven by USD 4.2 billion in acquired in-process R&D expense. Anyone looking to flatter the numbers has a hundred ways to strip that money out; Eli Lilly chose to let it hit Non-GAAP.

The significance lies not in the number but in whether management is willing to let near-term EPS look ugly. Willing means they are looking at the pipeline three to five years out; unwilling means they are looking at next quarter's share price. Eli Lilly belongs to the former. That is also why management scores 8 rather than 6 among the six gates.

Capital allocation ledger

The capital-allocation ledger needs to be laid out in full. Capacity: USD 27 billion across four sites plus USD 1.7 billion in Concord, with more than USD 50 billion invested in the US since 2020. M&A: Verve (about USD 1.3 billion, base editing) and Adverum (about USD 2.25 billion, ophthalmic gene therapy) in 2025, and USD 4.2 billion of acquired IPR&D confirmed in Q3 2026. Shareholder returns: annual dividend of about USD 4.5 per share (0.3% yield), with buybacks only offsetting dilution.

The logic behind this allocation is "put every dollar where the return is highest," and the highest returns right now are in capacity and pipeline, not buybacks. Buying back stock at 48x PE burns money; management has not done so, and that is right. The deduction is that M&A returns are not yet visible—Verve's base editing and Adverum's ophthalmic gene therapy will take three to five years to produce data.

Governance and succession: the only gap

On governance, Eli Lilly has a single share class with no dual voting rights—rare among founder-controlled pharma, and it means shareholders hold real constraints over management.But the succession plan is a blank: Ricks turns 60 in 2026, with no public COO or "heir apparent" arrangement.

Why does this matter? Because Lilly's strategy (build capacity in-house + buy pipelines + direct sales) is a path dependency formed during Ricks' tenure.A new CEO would likely mean a different playbook—outsourcing capacity, cutting M&A, stepping up buybacks; each move would change the valuation model. What to watch: whether a "President and COO" appointment emerges in the next 12–24 months.

Another signal worth noting: Lilly's board has not brought in an activist investor, nor has any "breakup" voice emerged.For a company with a USD 1.33 trillion market cap where a single molecule accounts for 66% of revenue, that alone shows the market's strong endorsement of the current strategy.Strong endorsement is a good thing—and the other side of "consensus too strong."

Conclusion

Management 8 points. Positives: honest guidance, willing to make bold bets, no dual-class structure, no buybacks at high valuations. Negatives: rising reliance on M&A, blank succession plan. Conclusion: this management will not destroy a good business, but it will not hit the brakes for you at 48x — that is up to shareholders themselves.
