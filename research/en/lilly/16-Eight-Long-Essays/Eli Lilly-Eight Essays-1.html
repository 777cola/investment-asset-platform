Part One: Cognitive Reset — Eli Lilly

Eli Lilly is not a weight-loss drug company; it is a 9.5-year rent-collection right
Research date 2026/11/09  ·  Price $1,411.95 (+4.28%)  ·  Market cap About USD 1.33 trillion  ·  PE(TTM) Approximately 48x
← Back to the hub

Core view

Treating Eli Lilly as a "weight-loss drug stock" gets three things wrong: it is really a metabolic management platform (one molecule treats multiple indications); it is neither a growth stock nor a value stock, but a quasi-monopoly within its patent life; at 48x PE you are buying the multiple, at 25x you are buying the company.

Misconception: this is a weight-loss drug company

Most people's view of Eli Lilly stops at one image: a company getting rich off weight-loss drugs. That view will lead you astray in three places: valuation, cycle and risk.

Start with the numbers: in the first three quarters of 2026, tirzepatide sold USD 43.768 billion and semaglutide USD 26.864 billion — USD 70.6 billion for the two molecules combined. This is the largest market a single drug class has ever reached in pharmaceutical history, exceeding the combined peak annual sales of Humira (USD 20 billion) and Keytruda (USD 29 billion). Calling a class like this a "weight-loss drug" is like calling a smartphone "a camera that makes calls".

The first step in resetting the view: Lilly does not sell "weight loss", it sells metabolic management. Obesity, type 2 diabetes, sleep apnea, cardiovascular risk, fatty liver — the same molecule monetised repeatedly across indications. Mounjaro is the diabetes version, Zepbound the weight-loss version; together they account for 65.9% of revenue. In essence, one molecule serving two markets.

The right framework: a lease maturing in 2036

Step two: It is not a growth stock, nor a value stock, but a "quasi-monopoly within the patent term." Through a growth-stock framework, 48x PE can be digested by growth; through a value-stock framework, 48x is absurdly expensive. Both frameworks are wrong. The correct framework is a "lease" — what you buy is a right to collect rent through May 2036, with rent rising 20%–30% a year, and after expiry it resets to zero and is renegotiated.

Under this framework, the question becomes: is this 9.5 years of rent, discounted to today, worth USD 1.33 trillion? My answer is "just about" — under neutral assumptions (2027 EPS of USD 42, then growth tapering to 8% a year), intrinsic value is roughly USD 1,250–1,350 per share. At the current price of USD 1,411.95, it is 5%–13% expensive.

One valuation detail to add: assuming 2027 EPS of USD 42 and growth declining 5 points every five years thereafter, cumulative free cash flow through 2036 discounts to about USD 900 billion, plus residual value after patent expiry (estimated at one-third of margins after generic competition) of about USD 300 billion, for a total of USD 1.2 trillion — 10% below the current market cap. This is not a precise valuation but an "order-of-magnitude check": the price the market pays equals an assumption that half the margin survives patent expiry.

The cost of a cognitive reset

Step three: the cost of a cognitive reset is "you will miss a lot." From 2024 to 2026, Eli Lilly's stock rose from just over USD 500 to over 1,400, a gain of one and a half times. Anyone who said "too expensive" in 2025 missed that stretch. So this is not a "smart" judgment; it is a judgment that accepts the willingness to miss out.

My reasoning is simple: buying at 48x, you earn the money of "the market willing to grant a higher multiple"; buying at 25x, you earn the money of "the company making money." The former is speculation; the latter is investment. This report does only the latter.

Another frequently asked question: "Since those who called it expensive in 2025 were wrong for a year and a half, why should your judgment be right now?" The answer: I may be wrong on timing, but I will not be wrong on method. Buy a company growing 39% at 48x, and the moment growth falls to 20% its valuation gets cut in half; buy the same company at 25x, and a fall to 20% only turns it from "cheap" to "reasonable." That is the difference between "expensive" and "cheap"—not predicting ups and downs, but deciding who gets hurt more easily.

Three numbers show how big the category is

Three cross-sectional comparisons show how unusual this category is. First, USD 70.6 billion — combined global sales of the two GLP-1 molecules in the first three quarters of 2026. For context, the global auto industry earns roughly USD 150 billion in net profit a year; Eli Lilly and Novo Nordisk captured nearly half that magnitude with just two molecules. Second, 65.9% — tirzepatide's share of Eli Lilly's revenue. This is not "one product line"; it is the company itself. Third, 39% — Eli Lilly's Q3 revenue growth, versus single-digit growth in the global prescription drug market over the same period.

Put these three numbers together and only one conclusion holds: this is a rare window created by a "super-category plus super-blockbuster" overlap. The window will not stay open forever — prices are falling, competition is intensifying, patents are expiring. But it is open now, and it is bigger than anyone expected.

Conclusion

One-line restatement: Eli Lilly = the toll collector on the world's largest metabolic category, with 9.5 years left on the lease, annual rent growth of 20%–30%, and zero at expiry. On this model, intrinsic value is USD 1,250–1,350, and the current price is 5%–13% too expensive.
