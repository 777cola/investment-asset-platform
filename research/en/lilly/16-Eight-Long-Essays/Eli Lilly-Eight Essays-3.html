Part Three: Anatomy of the Moat — Eli Lilly

Four legs hold up; the fifth is empty
Research date 2026/11/09  ·  Price $1,411.95 (+4.28%)  ·  Market cap About USD 1.33 trillion  ·  P/E (TTM) Approximately 48x
← Back to the hub

Core view

Mechanism, capacity, prescribing habits and patents: four legs stacked to 38/50, near-unbeatable in injectables. In oral, however, the moat narrows from four legs to two—and oral is the growth engine from 2028 onward.

The mechanics of four legs

Lilly's moat has four legs; pull out any one and it topples. The first is mechanism: tirzepatide is a GIP/GLP-1 dual agonist that beats single-target drugs head-to-head on weight loss and cardiovascular endpoints—clinical data, not marketing. The second is manufacturing capacity: scaling solid-phase peptide synthesis is extremely difficult and a commercial line takes three to four years to bring up; since 2020 Lilly has invested more than USD 50 billion in US capacity.

The third is prescribing habit: once doctors establish a pathway, switching drugs requires a reason. Zepbound has already overtaken Wegovy in new US obesity prescriptions, and this leg is thickening. The fourth is patents: US expiry in May 2036 is the foundation under all the legs—without it, the first three would collapse within two years.

Capacity: a moat in 2023, a burden in 2028

The 2023–2024 shortage shows how hard the capacity leg really is. Global GLP-1 supply fell short of demand, and Novo Nordisk, short of fill-finish lines, was forced to cap new-patient starts. Lilly was also short, but capacity locked in ahead of time let it capture more new prescriptions, and Zepbound overtook Wegovy over those two years. When capacity is share, building plants is how you take the market.

By 2028, however, the same leg becomes a burden. With Lilly's USD 27 billion and Novo's tenfold expansion, combined new capacity will likely outrun demand growth. The direct consequence of overcapacity is not unsold product; it is prices that cannot hold—because plants cannot stop, and stopping pushes unit costs up. At that point, "price for volume" shifts from a proactive strategy to reactive defence.

A note on Novo: its capacity expansion is more aggressive than Lilly's, and by 2026 its utilisation rate is already insufficient. That is the physical reason it is "cutting price to defend share"—once a plant is built it cannot stop, and stopping collapses unit costs. Lilly's high utilisation is an advantage now; but after four sites come online in 2028, it will face the same problem.

Why is the fifth leg empty?

The fifth leg is missing: no network effects, no switching costs. This is especially clear in oral drugs—for a patient to switch from Foundayo to oral Wegovy, the doctor only needs to change one prescription; there is no need to readjust or renegotiate insurance. That is why in the oral battle, Eli Lilly's advantage shrinks from "four legs" to "two legs" (brand + cost).

Scoring: mechanism 9, capacity 8, prescribing habits 8, patents 10, payer relationships 6, network 3. Weighted score 38/50, which qualifies as "wide but time-limited." That score is enough for Eli Lilly to survive to 2036 and make a lot of money, but not enough to guarantee a return at a 48x P/E—a wide moat and a reasonable valuation are two different things.

The moat's enemy is not Novo, it is time

Strip the moat back to its core and only one element is truly durable: patents. Mechanisms get overtaken by the next molecule (retatrutide, a triple agonist, delivered 20.8% weight loss at 80 weeks, stronger than tirzepatide). Capacity gets matched by capital (Novo's tenfold-scale expansion). Prescribing habits reset with each new drug class (the oral battle has already reset them once). Only patents are a timetable written into law.

So the moat's real enemy is not Novo Nordisk; it is time. In 2026 there are still 9.5 years left; in 2029 only 6.5; in 2033 only 2.5. Each passing year shortens the lease by a year, and the multiple the market assigns should step down in tandem. Today's 48x corresponds to "9.5 years left and 39% growth." If growth falls to 15% in 2029 with only 6.5 years of patent life remaining, a reasonable multiple is roughly 25x. This is not pessimism; it is arithmetic.

Conclusion

Moat verdict: wide, but with a lease term, and narrowing in the oral direction. It ensures Eli Lilly earns substantial returns through the patent period; it does not ensure the 48x valuation is justified.
