Part Seven: Risk Checklist — Eli Lilly

Five risk tiers, with valuation itself the most dangerous
Research date 2026/11/09  ·  Price $1,411.95 (+4.28%)  ·  Market cap About USD 1.33 trillion  ·  PE(TTM) Approximately 48x
← Back to the hub

Core view

Ranked by probability × impact: valuation (high/high), payer (high/high), oral competition (medium/high), capacity impairment (medium-low/medium), patents and regulation (certain but distant). The market obsesses daily over "who takes share," yet the real damage lies in "pricing set by others" and "zero margin for error at 48x."

Tier 1 and Tier 2: Valuation and payers

Rank risks by "probability × impact", not by "how scary it sounds". Tier one contains a single item: valuation. At 48x TTM, 39.8x 2026 guidance and 33.6x 2027 neutral, the multiple already assumes "39% growth persists for at least two years". One quarter of revenue 5% below the consensus of 5% would compress the multiple from 48x to 38x and take the stock down 20%. That is price risk, not company risk — and it is the most likely risk to materialise.

Tier two is payers. Medicare net price of USD 245/month, a USD 50 out-of-pocket cap, the TrumpRx launch; overseas pricing is already -29%. Price is the one variable Eli Lilly cannot control itself, and it determines how long the 84.4% gross margin can hold.

One probability judgment to add: I put the probability of a drawdown of more than 20% within the next 12 months above 60%. The basis is 31.6% annualized volatility — under a normal distribution, a 20% drawdown within a year is close to a certainty. That does not mean the company deteriorates, only that the price fluctuates, and volatility is a gift to the patient.

Third and fourth tiers: competition and capacity

The third tier is competition, but be clear about which battle. Lilly has already won injectables (Zepbound has overtaken Wegovy in new US obesity prescriptions); it has temporarily lost oral (184 million vs 811 million, a threefold prescription gap). Oral is the growth engine after 2028; losing it is equivalent to dismantling the bridge for the transition after the 2036 patent expiry. The impact of this point is not "earning less this year" but "no story after 2028."

The fourth tier is capacity and impairment. USD 27 billion across four sites, with the Concord single site to be completed in 2027. If growth falls below 15% in 2027–2028, fixed assets will become a depreciation burden, and it is not impossible for gross margin to move from 84% toward 75%.

The fifth tier and the most easily overlooked point

The fifth tier is the long-term structure: the patent cliff (US, May 2036), Chinese generics and the FDA's NAION eye-disorder warning (label update on November 9). None of these affects cash flow today, but all of them are compressing the imaginative headroom for a "patent-period premium." The eye warning itself has minimal impact—rare, and it does not change the indication—but it opens a door: regulators can add to the label at any time.

One more point, the easiest to overlook: single-molecule dependence. Tirzepatide accounts for 65.9% of revenue, so any clinical, regulatory or supply mishap strikes directly at the core of a USD 1.33 trillion market cap. Such risk is unpredictable and can only be managed through position sizing—which is why the single-name cap is 8%.

A note on the November 9 FDA warning: NAION is a rare but real risk, and the label update will go into the prescribing information, but it will not change prescribing logic. Its impact on fundamentals is close to zero; its impact on sentiment is that the other shoe has dropped—which is why the stock rose 4.28% that day. Treating it as bad news fully priced in is dangerous, because it proves the market is no longer willing to price bad news.

Three layers of hedging

How to hedge? Three layers. Price layer: wait to buy below USD 1,200 and let price absorb valuation risk. Position layer: cap any single stock at 8% and build in four tranches rather than all at once. Thesis layer: set three falsification conditions (Foundayo below 300 million for two quarters, US pricing down more than 12%, 2027 guidance growth <15%) and cut on trigger.

What cannot be hedged is the assumption that "all risks fail to materialize at once." The 48x valuation embeds that assumption holding. Risk management in investing is not forecasting risk; it is ensuring you are not knocked out even if the assumption is wrong — that is the entire meaning of margin of safety.

One last honest point: on this risk list, only valuation can be solved by waiting; the other four can only be handled by tracking. So "wait for a pullback" is not passivity — it is controlling the one variable you can control. Price is the only variable you get to choose; everything else is the company's business.

Conclusion

Risk score 3/10 (lower is riskier). At the company level it is nearly unassailable; at the price level it is all risk. If you remember only one thing, make it this: Eli Lilly's risk is not "will Novo beat it" but "how much is the market willing to pay for a race already won."
