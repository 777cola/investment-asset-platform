Part Five: Industry and Competition — Eli Lilly

Duopoly, the inaugural year of oral therapy: payers are the real judge

Core view

Eli Lilly 60%, Novo 40% — together above 95%. Lilly won injectables; Novo took round one in orals with a 3x prescription gap. The real referee is the payer: Medicare at USD 245/month plus a USD 50 out-of-pocket cap makes price declines one-way.

Duopoly: Novo was forced to cut prices

The 2026 industry landscape is "a duopoly plus a new battleground opening up." Duopoly: Eli Lilly 60%, Novo 40% (2026Q1 global weight-loss share), the two combined above 95%. New battleground: oral. Oral Wegovy launched in January 2026 and Foundayo was approved in April, the watershed for GLP-1 moving from "injection" to "pill."

The duopoly's stability rests on capacity and patents; its instability on price. Novo is being forced to cut prices: semaglutide accounts for about 75% of its revenue, its China patent expires in 2026, Zepbound is taking its injection share, and Q1 2026 net sales fell 4% YoY. Its price cuts are not a strategy but a fight for survival. That makes it the hardest competitor to deal with, because it is not afraid of losses.

Oral GLP-1 round one: where Novo wins

Novo won the first round of the oral battle, and won decisively: Q3 oral Wegovy USD 811 million and 183,000 weekly prescriptions versus Foundayo USD 184 million and 63,000. A threefold prescription gap is not "a little slower." Three reasons. Brand: the semaglutide name eliminates patient education. Reimbursement: oral Wegovy entered multiple commercial insurance plans at launch, while Foundayo was almost entirely cash-pay initially. Strategic commitment: Novo treats oral as a must-win campaign, while Lilly still has injectables to defend.

But Lilly has two cards left to play. First, convenience: Foundayo can be taken at any time without fasting, whereas oral Wegovy requires fasting plus a 30-minute wait; the adherence gap will take 12–24 months to show up in retention rates. Second, cost: small-molecule synthesis is far simpler than peptides, giving Lilly a lower marginal cost in price wars in overseas cash-pay markets.

China and the payer: two external forces

The third force is China. Semaglutide's China patent expires in 2026, with generics and improved novel drugs lining up; the US FDA has also received multiple GLP-1 generic applications now under review. Tirzepatide's US patent runs to 2036, so it is unaffected near term, but China is another story — Lilly's China revenue reached USD 2.582 billion in Q3, up 75% YoY, and much of that growth reflects "harvesting ahead of patent expiry."

The fourth force is payers, and it is the biggest. From 2026, Medicare will cover at a net price of USD 245 per month, with a patient out-of-pocket cap of USD 50, and TrumpRx launches in January.This is not market behaviour; it is political behaviour.The result: volume up (US sales +41%), price down (-7%), and the "down" is one-way.

One overlooked number: Lilly's overseas revenue share rose from 36% to 38%, while overseas pricing fell 29%.This means the revenue mix is tilting toward lower-margin regions, and even if US prices hold, overall gross margin will be slowly dragged down.This is the least conspicuous but most certain slope in the financials.

Three-year script: referencing HCV drugs

On a three-year view, our base case is that the oral battle takes shape in 2027 (Eli Lilly regains some share on convenience but not the top spot), capacity comes online in a concentrated wave in 2028, the price war intensifies, and in 2029 industry growth falls from 30% to below 15%. Consider the history of hepatitis C drugs: two years after Sovaldi launched, its price had halved and the category's valuation multiple fell from 20x to 8x.

GLP-1 is better positioned than hepatitis C — chronic disease, long-term use, a patient base ten times larger. But the two dynamics of "one-way price declines + concentrated capacity coming online" are the same. So the endgame for the industry is not "who wins" but "how much the winner can earn." Our call: Eli Lilly wins share, but not the 2026 margin.

One more detail: Novo's oral Wegovy can "inherit the throne" because it shares the semaglutide name. Eli Lilly's Foundayo carries an entirely new molecule name, so patient education and physician trust start from zero. This is the value of brand equity when a category switches — the brand Eli Lilly built on injectables did not migrate automatically to oral. If the next round (retatrutide) changes the name again, the same problem will resurface.

Conclusion

Rate the industry 8 out of 10. Demand is real and the competitive structure is stable, though unit economics are thinning. Eli Lilly will win share, but not the 2026 margin—the two statements are not contradictory; they answer "who wins" and "how much they win" respectively.
