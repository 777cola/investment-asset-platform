Part Two: The Nature of the Business — Eli Lilly

Four prices, three cost layers, one toll-road business
Research date 2026/11/09  ·  Price $1,411.95 (+4.28%)  ·  Market cap About USD 1.33 trillion  ·  PE(TTM) Approximately 48x

Core view

The same drug trades at four prices: a list price of USD 1,000–1,350, a Medicare net price of USD 245, a cash price of USD 149, and a patient out-of-pocket cap of USD 50. Who makes money depends on how that spread is split. Eli Lilly uses patents to defend the list price, channel leverage to negotiate the net price, and manufacturing capacity to press costs down — all three legs are still standing.

Four prices: who takes the spread

Three prices explain this business: list price, net price and patient out-of-pocket. In the US, tirzepatide carries a list price of USD 1,000–1,350 per month, a Medicare net price of USD 245 per month and a patient out-of-pocket cap of USD 50, while self-pay channels (LillyDirect, TrumpRx) start at USD 149 per month. One pen of drug, four prices, a 9x spread.

The gap between these four prices maps how profit is allocated across the value chain. The discount between list and net price funds rebates to PBMs and insurers; the spread between net price and manufacturing cost is Eli Lilly's gross margin; the USD 50 patient out-of-pocket cap is a politically determined "decent price."

Another angle: the business has three "customers"—patients, physicians and payers. Patients want efficacy, physicians want safety, payers want low cost, and those interests are not fully aligned. Eli Lilly can satisfy the first two and negotiate with the third. When the three conflict, the drugmaker always loses—because the payer is the only party that can say "no."

Unit economics: how much profit per patient per year

The business model rests on three levers: patents to defend list price, channel leverage to negotiate a higher net price, and scale to push down costs. The first depends on the law, the second on negotiation, the third on capex. Q3 data maps directly onto this: US list prices held stable, net prices fell 7% in the US and 29% overseas, and volume growth of +53% diluted costs (gross margin +0.8pct).

Run the unit economics: assume a Medicare net price of USD 245/month, or USD 2,940 per patient per year; at an 84.4% gross margin, gross profit is about USD 2,480; deduct selling expenses (estimated at 15%–18% of revenue) and R&D (over 15%), leaving net profit of about USD 780 per patient per year. The eligible US population exceeds 100 million; just 5 million on long-term treatment yields USD 39 billion in net profit—which is why the market is willing to pay USD 1.33 trillion.

When does reverse leverage run out?

But this business has a "reverse leverage" that peers lack: price cuts do not immediately hit profit, because costs are falling too. In Q3, the selling price fell 15% while gross margin still rose 0.8pp on capacity utilisation. Eli Lilly therefore still has room to "cut price for volume" — room that runs out once capacity reaches full utilisation.

When does it run out? A rough calculation: all four bases will be fully operational before 2030, with 2027–2028 the capacity ramp-up period and full utilisation after 2029. In other words, before 2028, "trading price for volume" can still make money; after 2029, profit can only come from "volume growth itself." That point in time is the watershed for valuation.

The limits of the business: why it cannot scale like software

Software has near-zero marginal cost and can scale without limit. Drugs cannot—every new patient consumes physical goods: an injection pen, a vial of API, a stretch of cold chain. Lilly is spending USD 27 billion on plants in 2026, and that is the bill for "marginal cost is not zero."

This boundary determines two things. First, scale has a ceiling: revenue can grow as fast as capacity is built, but capacity takes three to four years to build, so growth will stall abruptly in one year and then jump. Second, the moat must be maintained with capital: unlike Coca-Cola, which collects money on the strength of a formula, Lilly must plow more than 20% of revenue back into fixed assets every year. That is why, despite a ROE of 74.6%, its free cash flow is only 0.6–0.7x net profit.

Conclusion

Essence: use the law to hold up price, use scale to push down cost, and earn the spread between the two. The model holds through 2028 and breaks in 2029, once capacity is fully ramped — after that, Eli Lilly can grow only through "more patients," not "a better spread."
