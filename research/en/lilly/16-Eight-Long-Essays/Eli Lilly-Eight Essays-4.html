Part Four: The Financial Truth — Eli Lilly

From 84% gross margin to 26% net margin — a 58pp gap
Research date 2026/11/09  ·  Price $1,411.95 (+4.28%)  ·  Market cap About USD 1.33 trillion  ·  PE(TTM) Approximately 48x

Core view

The drop from an 84.4% gross margin to a 26.4% net margin reflects selling expenses, R&D, PBM rebates and capex. The USD 4.2 billion acquisition cost sits in Non-GAAP — an honest approach, and one that leaves USD 35–36 as the true earnings power.

A 58-percentage-point gap

Eli Lilly's income statement carries a 58-percentage-point gap: gross margin 84.4%, net margin 26.4%. Where do the 58 points in between go? This is not a story about "high expenses" — it is a structure that needs to be taken apart. A rough breakdown: sales and administrative expenses at about 15%–18% of revenue, R&D around 15%, the two combined 30%–33%; of the remaining 25 percentage points, PBM rebates (a deduction from revenue, not an expense line), interest and tax account for the rest.

So an 84% gross margin does not mean 84% of the money lands in the pocket. What actually lands there is 26.4%. This also explains why Eli Lilly's cash flow runs at only 0.6–0.7x net profit — capex sits in between.

On the tax rate: Eli Lilly's effective tax rate has been in the 15%–18% range in recent years, helped by production in low-tax jurisdictions such as Ireland, below the US statutory 21%. That is a hidden support for the margin, and it is also a piece that could be shaved once the global minimum tax agreement (15%) takes effect — a limited impact, but a change that "cannot be reversed."

DuPont breakdown: how much of the 74.6% ROE is leverage-driven

A DuPont breakdown of the 74.6% ROE (2024): net margin 26%, asset turnover around 0.9, equity multiplier around 2.5. The least sustainable of the three is the equity multiplier — it is high because buybacks and dividends have thinned the equity base (equity of RMB 14.19 billion vs total assets of over RMB 100 billion). That is not a financial risk; it is an accounting structure.

What really matters is ROIC. Lilly's invested capital is rising sharply in 2026 (USD 27 billion of capacity + USD 4.2 billion of M&A). If profit does not rise in tandem in 2027–2028, ROIC will fall from above 30% today to below 20%. That would be the first hard evidence of deteriorating capital allocation, and it would surface earlier than a gross margin decline.

One more metric to watch: the growth rate of receivables and inventory. If inventory grows consistently faster than revenue, the channel is being stuffed; if receivables outpace revenue, payer payment terms are lengthening. These are the two earliest leading indicators of an earnings blow-up, one to two quarters ahead of gross margin. No anomalies at present, but check every quarter.

Should the RMB 4.2 billion IPR&D be added back?

Now the USD 4.2 billion IPR&D acquisition. That cash flowed through Non-GAAP and shaved USD 4.57 per share off EPS — this is Eli Lilly's most praiseworthy accounting treatment, and also its most misread one. Praiseworthy because the company did not strip the money out as "one-time"; misread because many see the "EPS guidance cut" and assume operations deteriorated, when in fact management chose to spend cash buying pipeline.

Should this money be added back? My approach is not to add it back. Eli Lilly has done deals three years running — Verve (about USD 1.3 billion) and Adverum (about USD 225 million) in 2025, and another USD 4.2 billion in Q3 2026. When something happens three years in a row, it is not "one-time" — it is "routine operating cost." Treat it as routine, and 2026's true earning power is USD 35–36, not USD 39.5–40.5.

Capex: trading cash flow for share

Finally, capex. USD 2.7 billion across four sites (three API, one injectable, all completed before 2030) plus USD 1.7 billion for the single Concord plant (completed 2027), with capex intensity above 20% of revenue. This is "trading today's cash flow for tomorrow's share" — right in direction, but the cost must be seen clearly: annual depreciation rises from 2027–2030, compressing gross margin by 2–4 percentage points.

Is cash flow sufficient? Yes. Operating cash flow matches net income, interest-bearing debt is under USD 30 billion, and interest coverage exceeds 50x. Eli Lilly can expand capacity, make acquisitions and pay dividends all at once, without borrowing. This is where it has more room than Novo—Novo must pay for both idle capacity and price cuts at the same time.

Conclusion

Financial takeaway: earnings quality scores 9. Revenue beat consensus by 7.9% and adjusted EPS by 19.5%, while gross margin rose 0.8pct against the trend — a clean sweep on all three, with no accounting concerns. Points are deducted only on capex certainty: the USD 27 billion of capacity must be absorbed by sales volumes over the next five years, and volume growth is not controllable.
