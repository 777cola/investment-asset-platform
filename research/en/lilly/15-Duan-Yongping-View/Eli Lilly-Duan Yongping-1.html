Core view

Ask six questions the way Duan Yongping would. Is this a good business? Yes. Will it still be here in ten years? Yes, though the molecules it sells will change. Do you understand it? About 70%. At what price do you buy? Below USD 1,200. What do you fear most? The payer, not the competitor. What if you already hold it? Hold, don't add. Put the six answers together and it comes down to one sentence: a good company, too expensive, wait.

1. Is this a good business?

Good. What makes it good? You do not need to be especially smart to understand how it makes money — make a drug others cannot make, sell it during the patent period, and patients keep taking it after they start. Gross margin 84.4%, net margin 26.4% — this is not squeezed out through management; it comes from "only it can make this."

I often say the business model matters most. Lilly's model is "one-time R&D investment plus long-term rent collection," and the best part is that repeat purchases need no selling: obese people do not keep taking the drug because of advertising; they keep taking it because stopping causes rebound. This demand comes from the body, not from marketing — that is a good business.

Another point I care about: this company does not need to live on borrowed money. Interest-bearing debt is under USD 30 billion, interest coverage exceeds 50x, and the cash it generates is enough to expand capacity, make acquisitions and pay dividends. The cost of mistakes at such a company is low, and management has room to do long-term things — such as spending USD 27 billion to build four plants, something only companies with strong cash flow dare to do.

II. Will it still be around in ten years?

Yes. But the question is not whether the company still exists; it is what it is still selling. Ten years from now tirzepatide's patent will have expired (May 2036 in the US), and by then Eli Lilly will be selling retatrutide, or some other molecule. So what you buy with Eli Lilly is not tirzepatide but the company's ability to keep producing blockbuster drugs.

Does it have that ability? I think so. It has walked the GLP-1 road for over a decade, from dulaglutide to tirzepatide to retatrutide, one generation after another — not luck. But having the ability and knowing what that ability is worth are two different things: the former is qualitative, the latter has to be calculated. When I can't calculate it, I use common sense: USD 1.33 trillion, earning 33 billion a year (rough Non-GAAP), takes 40 years to pay back — that math doesn't look worthwhile to me.

III. Can you make sense of it?

I understand 70% of it: mechanism, demand, channel, competition — all of that I get. What I don't understand is how much the pipeline is worth. Retatrutide's Phase III data are strong — 20.8% weight loss at 80 weeks — but how much tirzepatide share will it take once launched? Both drugs are our own. What do you call that? You call it cannibalising yourself. A good thing, but not something I can put a number on.

Then there's the oral drug. Foundayo sold only USD 184 million in the third quarter; oral Wegovy sold USD 811 million — a threefold gap in prescriptions. Some call it first-mover advantage, others a product-strength problem. I don't know which is right. If you don't know, you don't bet big; that's what "don't do what you don't understand" means. It doesn't mean you can't touch it — it means you can't assign a valuation to the part you don't understand.

IV. At what price would you buy?

I would start looking below USD 1,200 and buy below USD 1,050. Why? That price equates to about 25x 2027 earnings. For a company that can still grow 15%–20% with gross margins above 80%, 25x is fair. It now trades at 33.6x; the extra 8-odd turns is what you pay for the "story".

Some argue good companies never offer you a cheap price. That is half right — good companies are indeed rarely cheap, but the market goes mad once a year. Eli Lilly fell to USD 1,115.70 on September 11 this year, 21% below the current level; that was the opportunity, but no one dared buy at the time. So the price does come — the question is whether you can wait for it and whether you dare act.

V. What is the biggest fear?

My biggest fear is not Novo Nordisk but the payer. From 2026, Medicare will cover it at a net price of USD 245 per month, with a patient out-of-pocket cap of USD 50—and that price is set by the government, not by Eli Lilly. Overseas pricing is already -29%, versus only -7% in the US today; how long do you think that can hold?

My second fear is "thinking you understand." Tirzepatide has been too successful—so successful that everyone believes it will keep succeeding. That is the most dangerous moment—not because the company will turn bad, but because the price has already priced in "it won't turn bad." I have seen too many companies like this: results stay strong, yet the stock price just won't rise, because it was too expensive when bought.

Six: What should existing holders do?

Hold, don't add. The fundamentals are intact — revenue +38.6%, guidance raised for the third time, gross margin still rising. Selling here means paying tax and fees, and possibly not being able to buy back in. But don't add either: the only reason to add would be "it will keep going up," and that isn't investing.

If your cost basis is very low (bought two years ago, say), it's even simpler: do nothing. The hardest thing in investing isn't buying or selling — it's doing nothing. I often say that if you aren't willing to hold a stock for ten years, don't hold it for ten minutes — and conversely, if you plan to hold for ten years, this quarter's move has nothing to do with you.

Conclusion

A good company at an expensive price: wait. The third point matters most, not the first. Understanding a good company takes an afternoon; waiting for a good price takes a year, even three. Revisit below USD 1,200, buy below USD 1,050; until then, this belongs on the watchlist, not in the portfolio.
