Industry Chain — Eli Lilly

Eli Lilly (LLY) | 09 · Industry Chain
Research date 2026/11/09  ·  Price $1,411.95 (+4.28%)  ·  Market cap About USD 1.33 trillion  ·  PE(TTM) Approximately 48x
← Back to the hub

Core thesis

Value distribution across the innovative-drug supply chain is highly uneven: patent-drug companies like Lilly earn 84.4% gross margins and 52% operating margins; upstream peptide API and injection-pen suppliers earn 20%–40%; downstream PBMs and retail pharmacies run net margins of just 2%–5%, yet they hold the switch on "who gets into the formulary." Lilly's position in the chain is the top of the smile curve, but its rent comes from patents, not scale. Three shifts are now reshaping the chain: capacity is turning from shortage to surplus, pricing power is moving from drugmakers to payers, and oral drugs are moving the channel from hospitals to online.

Upstream: the real bottleneck lies in fermenters and purification lines

Tirzepatide is a peptide, not a small molecule. Peptide production relies on solid-phase synthesis plus purification, with low yields, long cycle times and difficult scale-up—a commercial line takes three to four years from design to start-up. That is why global GLP-1 supply stayed short through 2023–2024: not for lack of demand, but because the fill-finish capacity was not there.

Lilly's response was to bring this in-house: in February 2025 it announced at least USD 27 billion to build four US sites (three for API, one for injectables, completed by 2030), plus a USD 1.7 billion single plant in Concord due in 2027, taking cumulative US investment since 2020 above USD 50 billion. The upside of building your own capacity is independence; the downside is heavy assets, long timelines and hard exits. Upstream suppliers' bargaining power in this cycle is sliding from "shortage" toward "glut" as Lilly and Novo expand production.

Midstream: the 84% gross margin is a patent rent, not manufacturing capability

The profit Eli Lilly captures in the middle of the chain comes almost entirely from the word "exclusive." For the same tirzepatide, production cost is a low share of the selling price, and after deducting sales expenses, R&D and PBM rebates, the net margin is still 26.4%. This is not manufacturing profit; it is a legally granted rent. Once the patent expires, at the same plant and on the same line, gross margin will fall below 60%.

So assessing Eli Lilly's position in the value chain requires separating two layers: at the manufacturing layer it is a heavy-asset investor; at the pricing layer it is a rent collector. In 2026Q3, volume +53% and price -15% show that the rent-collection ratio is being compressed—using more volume at lower prices to sustain total rent growth. How long this model can run depends on how much room penetration has left (more than 100 million eligible people in the US, versus a few million actually on the drug).

Lilly's position: vertical integration, trading heavy assets for share

Eli Lilly's position in the chain has an easily underestimated advantage: vertical integration. Most pharma companies only do R&D and sales, outsourcing production to CDMOs; Eli Lilly is pulling API, formulation, fill-finish and even delivery devices in-house. Of the four sites behind that USD 27 billion in 2025, three make API and one makes injectables—a decisive advantage at a stage where capacity is share: the market Novo lost in 2023–2024 to insufficient capacity was captured by Eli Lilly on the back of self-built capacity.

The cost is a change in the shape of return on capital: from "asset-light, high ROE" to "asset-heavy, high ROE." ROE can still hold above 70% because profit is growing faster than net assets; once growth slows, heavy assets will quickly drag ROE back below 30%.This is the number to watch most closely after 2028.

Downstream: pricing power has shifted to the payers

This is the most important change across the entire chain, and the easiest to overlook. In November 2025, Eli Lilly, Novo Nordisk and the White House reached an MFN agreement: from 2026, Medicare will cover weight-loss GLP-1s at a net price of USD 245 per month, with patient out-of-pocket costs capped at USD 50, and the TrumpRx platform launching in January 2026. This is the first time US federal healthcare has paid for an obesity indication, and the first time the price has been pinned at USD 245.

In the language of the chain, this means "pricing power shifts from pharma to payers." What pharma gets in exchange is volume (Medicare coverage will bring in a large number of new patients); what it gives up is price. Q3 US volume +41%, price -7%; overseas volume +76%, price -29% — the current -7% in the US is temporary; today's -29% overseas is what the US will look like tomorrow. PBM and insurer net margins are only 3%, but they decide who gets into the formulary — the classic "low profit, high power" position.

Cycle position: the three curves are not at the same point

Demand is at an early stage (single-digit penetration), price is at a mid stage (already in one-way decline), and capacity is at a mid-to-late stage (concentrated ramp-up in 2027–2029). This mismatch is the biggest perception gap of this cycle. The market stares at penetration to size the opportunity; I stare at capacity and price to size profit.

Historical parallel: hepatitis C drugs (Sovaldi) in the 2010s followed the same path—price was halved within two years of launch, and after volumes peaked the entire category's valuation fell from 20x to 8x. GLP-1 is better positioned than hepatitis C (chronic disease, long-term use, a patient base ten times larger), but the two dynamics of "one-way price decline + concentrated capacity ramp-up" are identical. The steering wheel of the cycle is in the payer's hands, not Eli Lilly's.

Conclusion

Score the value chain 8: Eli Lilly occupies the most profitable position (84.4% gross margin, 52% operating margin), builds upstream capacity itself and uses LillyDirect downstream to bypass some PBMs — its chain control ranks among the strongest in pharma. But its rent comes from patents that expire in May 2036, and pricing power is already shifting to payers. These two facts mean it should not command 48x.
