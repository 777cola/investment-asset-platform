## Upstream: the real bottleneck is fermenter and purification capacity

Tirzepatide is a peptide, not a small molecule. Peptide production depends on solid-phase synthesis and purification, with low yields, long cycle times and difficult scale-up; a commercial line takes three to four years from design to start-up. That explains why global GLP-1 supply remained tight through 2023–2024: not for lack of demand, but because fill-finish capacity was not there.

Lilly's response was to bring production in-house: in February 2025 it announced at least USD 27 billion to build four US sites (three for API, one for injectables, completed by 2030), plus a USD 1.7 billion single plant in Concord due in 2027, taking cumulative US investment since 2020 above USD 50 billion. Building your own capacity buys independence; the price is heavy assets, long timelines and hard exits. As Lilly and Novo expand production, upstream suppliers' bargaining power is sliding from "shortage" toward "glut" this cycle.

## Midstream: the 84% gross margin reflects patent rent, not manufacturing capability

Eli Lilly's mid-chain profit comes almost entirely from the word "exclusive." For the same tirzepatide, production cost is a small share of the selling price, and after sales expenses, R&D and PBM rebates, net margin still reaches 26.4%. This is not manufacturing profit; it is a legally granted rent. Once the patent expires, the same plant and the same line will see gross margin fall below 60%.

Assessing Eli Lilly's position in the value chain therefore requires separating two layers: at the manufacturing layer it is a heavy-asset investor; at the pricing layer it is a rent collector. In 2026Q3, volume +53% and price -15% show the rent-collection ratio being compressed—more volume at lower prices to sustain total rent growth. How long this model can run depends on how much room penetration has left (more than 100 million eligible people in the US, versus a few million actually on the drug).

## Lilly's position: vertical integration, trading heavy assets for share

Eli Lilly's position in the chain carries an easily underestimated advantage: vertical integration. Most pharma companies confine themselves to R&D and sales and outsource production to CDMOs; Eli Lilly is bringing API, formulation, fill-finish and even delivery devices in-house. Of the four sites underpinning that USD 27 billion in 2025, three make API and one makes injectables—a decisive advantage at a stage where capacity is share: the market Novo lost in 2023–2024 to insufficient capacity was captured by Eli Lilly on the back of self-built capacity.

The cost is a change in the shape of return on capital: from "asset-light, high ROE" to "asset-heavy, high ROE." ROE can still hold above 70% because profit is growing faster than net assets; once growth slows, heavy assets will quickly drag ROE back below 30%.This is the number to watch most closely after 2028.

## Downstream: pricing power has shifted to the payers

This is the most consequential change across the entire chain, and the easiest to overlook. In November 2025 Eli Lilly, Novo Nordisk and the White House reached an MFN agreement: from 2026, Medicare will cover weight-loss GLP-1s at a net price of USD 245 per month, with patient out-of-pocket costs capped at USD 50, and the TrumpRx platform launching in January 2026. It marks the first time US federal healthcare has paid for an obesity indication, and the first time the price has been pinned at USD 245.

In the language of the chain, pricing power shifts from pharma to payers. Pharma gets volume in exchange — Medicare coverage will bring in a large number of new patients — and gives up price. Q3 US volume +41%, price -7%; overseas volume +76%, price -29% — the current -7% in the US is temporary; today's -29% overseas is what the US will look like tomorrow. PBM and insurer net margins are only 3%, but they decide who gets into the formulary — the classic "low profit, high power" position.

## Cycle position: the three curves are not at the same point

Demand sits at an early stage (single-digit penetration), price at a mid stage (already in one-way decline) and capacity at a mid-to-late stage (ramp-up concentrated in 2027–2029). That mismatch is the biggest perception gap of this cycle. The market sizes the opportunity off penetration; I size profit off capacity and price.

The historical parallel is hepatitis C (Sovaldi) in the 2010s, which followed the same path: price halved within two years of launch, and once volumes peaked the category de-rated from 20x to 8x. GLP-1 is better positioned than hepatitis C — chronic disease, long-term use, a patient base ten times larger — but the two dynamics of one-way price decline and concentrated capacity ramp-up are identical. The steering wheel of the cycle is in the payer's hands, not Eli Lilly's.
