Key points of the article

A publishable WeChat article: it opens with the November 9 episode in which the FDA added a warning and the stock rose 4.28% anyway, breaks down three highlights from the Q3 report (revenue +38.6%, tirzepatide at 16.075 billion in the quarter, gross margin 84.4%) and two details (the oral drug loses by three times, an overseas price cut of 29%), then closes with a three-scenario valuation laying out odds of 0.6 to 1 and the conclusion: "wait for USD 1,200."

Bad news rose 4.28%

On November 9, the US FDA required Eli Lilly to add a warning about a rare eye disease to the labels of Mounjaro and Zepbound. That is bad news. Yet Eli Lilly rose 4.28% that day to close at USD 1,411.95, just 0.4% below its all-time high. Novo Nordisk also rose 2.65%.

The world's two largest weight-loss drug companies rose together on the same piece of negative regulatory news. That scene says more about current market sentiment than any earnings figure: bad news is no longer being priced in.

I then went through Eli Lilly's October 29 third-quarter report. The results were so good there was almost nothing to fault. But after working through the valuation, I reached an unpopular conclusion: I will not buy a single share of this company.

First, the good news: these results are genuinely impressive

Start with the good news, because it is genuinely good.

First, revenue of USD 24.397 billion, up 39% YoY and 7.9% above the USD 22.6 billion consensus. Full-year guidance was raised from USD 85–87 billion to USD 91–92 billion — the third upgrade this year.

Second, a single drug now carries two-thirds of the company. Tirzepatide (Mounjaro, the diabetes version, at 10.723 billion, plus Zepbound, the weight-loss version, at 5.352 billion) generated USD 16.075 billion in the quarter, 65.9% of revenue. In the first three quarters this molecule sold USD 43.768 billion, still growing more than 50% YoY. A drug launched three years ago, annualising above USD 60 billion and still growing at 50% — there is no second example in pharmaceutical history.

Third, gross margin of 84.4%. USD 84 of gross profit on every USD 100 sold — achieved with selling prices down 15%, up 0.8 percentage points from a year earlier.

By now you probably think this is a perfect company. It is not. The report hides two unflattering details.

Orforglipron missed on efficacy, and by a factor of three

Detail one: the oral drug lost, and not by a small margin. Lilly's oral weight-loss drug Foundayo was approved in April this year, with the market initially expecting USD 3 billion of sales in 2026. Q3 sales came in at USD 184 million, of which USD 124 million was US, roughly USD 50 million below consensus. The full-year forecast has been cut repeatedly to USD 1.3 billion.

Compare Novo Nordisk: oral Wegovy launched in January this year and sold USD 811 million in Q3. Prescription volumes are more striking still—as of the end of October, oral Wegovy was running 183,000 prescriptions per week versus 63,000 for Foundayo, a threefold gap.

Why did it lose? Not because the drug is inferior. Foundayo is the first small-molecule GLP-1 that does not require fasting, theoretically the more convenient option. It lost on brand and payer access: oral Wegovy shares the "semaglutide" name, so doctors do not need to relearn it and insurers do not need to renegotiate; Foundayo is an entirely new molecule and in its early launch relied almost entirely on patient self-pay.

Why does this matter? Because tirzepatide's US patent expires in May 2036, and the oral drug is the bridge across that date. The bridge was not built well, so the growth story after 2028 will have to be rewritten.

Detail 2: The price war has already begun

Detail two: the price war has already begun — it just hasn't reached the US yet. In Q3, company-wide volumes rose 53% while realized prices fell 15%. Breaking it down: US volumes +41%, prices down only 7%; overseas volumes +76%, prices down 29%.

Overseas, prices have already been cut to 70% of list. Why did the US fall only 7%? Because its time hasn't come. In November 2025, Eli Lilly and Novo Nordisk reached an agreement with the White House: Medicare will reimburse weight-loss drugs at a net price of USD 245 per month starting in 2026, with patient out-of-pocket costs capped at USD 50, and the TrumpRx platform launched in January this year. The same drug carries a list price above USD 1,000, a Medicare net price of 245, and a cash-pay channel price of 149.

This is not a promotion, it is a system. Prices set by the system only move down, never up.

So where does the 84.4% gross margin come from? Capacity absorption. Volumes rose 53%, fixed factory costs were spread thinner, so gross margin still rose even with prices down 15%. But absorption happens only once, while price cuts happen every year. Lilly itself guides 2026 operating margin at 52%–52.5%; if gross margin could truly hold at 84% indefinitely, that number should be higher. Management's conservatism is usually closer to the truth than analysts' optimism.

Do the math: even if all goes well, it's still a 5% loss

Now run the numbers. At USD 1,411.95 and 941 million shares, market cap is USD 1.33 trillion. TTM EPS of USD 29.32 puts the stock on 48x. On 2026 guidance of USD 35–36, that is 40x; on the 2027 forecast of USD 42, 33.6x.

Three scenarios:

  • Bear: full-blown price war, oral fails, 2027 EPS USD 33, 25x multiple → USD 825, -42%
  • Base: tirzepatide holds share, oral ramps steadily, EPS USD 42, 32x multiple → USD 1,344, -5%
  • Bull: oral beats expectations plus new indication expansion, EPS USD 47, 38x multiple → USD 1,786, +26%

Look at the structure: even if everything goes right, you lose 5%. Upside of 26% against 42% downside — a 0.6-to-1 payoff, inverted.

Some will argue a good company should not be judged on P/E. I agree — so switch methods: 2026 Non-GAAP net income of roughly USD 33.4 billion against a USD 1.33 trillion market cap is a static return of 2.5%. To lift that to 8%, net income must rise to USD 100 billion, three times today's level. This company must triple its profit over nine and a half years (until patent expiry in 2036) just to justify today's price. It may get there, but you have already paid for it.

So my approach is to wait.

I am not bearish on this company. It clears the first four of my six gates almost perfectly: the business is understandable, gross margin is 84.4%, the moat has four legs, and management is honest — it raised revenue guidance while cutting EPS guidance on USD 4.2 billion of acquisition costs rather than forcing the numbers.

Only the fifth and sixth gates — margin of safety and discipline — fail.Buying at an all-time high can only be justified by "it is the leader", which is a judgment about identity, not about price.

So I wait. Either of two triggers is enough to revisit:

  • The stock falls below USD 1,200 (28.6x 2027 earnings; the payoff ratio flips to 1.4 to 1)
  • Foundayo sells more than USD 500 million in a single quarter, with two consecutive quarters of sequential growth (the oral franchise proves it can carry the baton; at that point even 35x is acceptable)

For reference: on September 11 this year the stock fell to USD 1,115.70, 21% below today. The market was worried about oral drugs and price cuts — the worries were right, yet the stock rose 26.6% in two months.The market goes crazy once a year; the question is whether you can wait it out and whether you dare act.

A good company bought too dear is still a bad investment. Easy to say, hard to do, because you watch it keep rising. But investing is not about who catches more winning stocks; it is about who survives longer.
