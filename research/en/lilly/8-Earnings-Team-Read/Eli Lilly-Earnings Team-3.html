Earnings Deep Dive (Munger) — Eli Lilly

Read it in reverse: identify the one claim that, if wrong, brings down the entire report
Research date 2026/11/09  ·  Price $1,411.95 (+4.28%)  ·  Market cap About USD 1.33 trillion  ·  P/E (TTM) Approximately 48x

Core view

Growth is entirely volume-driven, with pricing a drag. Accounting is clean and all the bad news is out in the open, so credibility is high. The risk is not the company — it is that "everyone agrees." When consensus is maxed out, any slight disappointment gets magnified.

One wrong sentence and everything is lost

Think in reverse: which single line in this earnings report, if wrong, would bring the entire report down? It is "volume +53%". If volume growth fell to 20%, revenue growth would drop from 39% to single digits, because price is -15%. In other words, this company's growth rests entirely on more patients starting the drug, not on each patient paying more.

That cuts both ways. The good news: more than 100 million people in the US are eligible, only a few million actually use the drug, penetration is in the single digits, and volume has ample room to run. The bad news: in a business that grows on volume, the ceiling is set by the payer — a Medicare net price of USD 245 per month and a patient out-of-pocket cap of USD 50. That price is negotiated by the government, not set by Eli Lilly.

Accounting checks and consensus risk

Check the accounting again. Is there any bad news being hidden? I looked at three places. First, the USD 4.2 billion acquisition charge, which went into Non-GAAP and was not hidden. Second, the USD 184 million oral drug, which is disclosed separately and was not hidden. Third, the -29% overseas selling price, which was volunteered on the call and was not hidden. All three are out in the open, and this report has high credibility.

So where is the risk? In the fact that "everyone agrees." Revenue above consensus, EPS above consensus, guidance raised for the third time, and even on the day the FDA added the eye-disease warning, the stock rose 4.28%. Consensus this strong means expectations are fully priced in, and any slight disappointment will be magnified. I am not worried the company is getting worse; I am worried it is "just not that good" — and that is already enough to take 48x down to 38x.

One more counter-question: if Eli Lilly's growth falls to 20% next year, what multiple will the market give it? Comparable blockbuster pharma companies growing 20% typically trade at 25–30x. That would imply USD 1,050–1,260. In other words, no bad news is needed — just "not as fast," and the stock has 25% downside.

Conclusion

I'd score the financials a 9, management a 9 and the price a 2. Add those three up and you don't get 20 — you get "don't buy." Investing isn't about scoring and ranking companies; it's about making trade-offs at a specific price.
