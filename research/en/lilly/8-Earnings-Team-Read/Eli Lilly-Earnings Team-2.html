Earnings Deep Dive (Buffett) — Eli Lilly

Profit quality, free cash flow, the durability of the moat
Research date 2026/11/09  ·  Price $1,411.95 (+4.28%)  ·  Market cap About USD 1.33 trillion  ·  P/E (TTM) Approximately 48x
← Back to the hub

Core view

The profit is real money, capex is a deliberate choice, and the financials need no defence. Price is the only question: at 48x TTM, the market is valuing cash flows beyond 2036 on the assumption that the company can keep collecting tolls.

Quality of profit

Start with earnings quality. Net profit was RMB 6.451 billion, up 16% YoY, 22.6 percentage points below revenue growth — but that is no bad thing: the gap comes mainly from USD 4.2 billion of acquired in-process R&D expense, a deliberate spend on the future rather than runaway costs. Adjusted EPS of USD 7.48 came in 19.5% above consensus, showing the operating level is far better than the market expected.

Next, capex. USD 27 billion to build four sites, with the single Concord plant at USD 1.7 billion due for completion in 2027; capex at over 20% of revenue has compressed free cash flow to 0.6–0.7x net profit. This is the line I care about most. A good company can post a few quarters of unimpressive profit, but it cannot fail to generate free cash flow over the long run. Lilly is still in the safe zone, but the safe zone is narrowing.

One more detail: EPS this quarter was USD 7.22, up 16% YoY, exactly in line with net profit growth, indicating no change in share count and no dilution from large preferred stock or convertible bonds. The reporting chain holds together — revenue, profit and EPS reconcile across all three lines, which in the filings I have read is not something to take for granted.

Moats have lease terms; valuations must be staged

Next, the moat's lease term. Tirzepatide's US patent expires in May 2036, nine and a half years out. Through then, Eli Lilly is a monopolist with pricing power; beyond it, the company must contend with generics, improved Chinese novel drugs and next-generation molecules. On a DCF, post-2036 cash flows must be valued separately and cannot simply be assigned perpetual growth.

The market currently pays 48x TTM, effectively capitalising post-2036 cash flows as if the company can keep levying its tax. That is the disagreement: I do not doubt the cash flows of these nine years; I doubt whether today's price holds after the ninth. So I would assign 25–30x, not 48x. That judgment does not change on the strength of one quarter's results.

Conclusion

I would happily own this company for ten years, but I would not initiate a position at today's price. Good business + good management + bad price = wait. Wait for the multiple to de-rate to 25–30x — the USD 900–1,050 range — and only then does it become "a good company at a fair price."
