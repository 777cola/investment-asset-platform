## The only substantive positive: the Q3 report on October 29

The stock rose 6.63% on earnings day and 3.19% the next day, up 10.0% over two sessions. This is not sentiment; it is numbers: revenue of USD 24.397 billion came in above the consensus of 7.9%, adjusted EPS of USD 7.48 came in above the consensus of 19.5%, and full-year revenue guidance was raised from 85–87 billion to 91–92 billion (midpoint +USD 5.5 billion). **All three beat, and this is the third guidance raise.**

The market is not buying this quarter's profit; it is buying the act of raising guidance itself — a company that raises guidance three times in a row has high visibility into demand. **But note: in the same report, EPS guidance was cut (35.50–36.50 → 35.00–36.00) due to USD 4.2 billion in acquisition costs. The market chose to look only at the revenue line.** That is evidence of sentiment, not of reason.

## The one thing most worth learning: good data can still fall

On September 29, Eli Lilly released Phase III TRIUMPH-2 data for retatrutide: the 12mg dose produced average weight loss of 20.8% (about 22.5 kg) at 80 weeks, with one-third of patients losing more than 25%; the company plans to file for approval in Q1 2027. **This is among the best data seen in the weight-loss drug space in the past decade.**

The stock fell 0.01% that day and 2.33% the next. Why? Because the market had already priced it in — the "next-generation pipeline" was part of the rationale for a 48x PE, and the data merely confirmed rather than surprised. **This is what fully loaded expectations look like: good news is no longer a reason to buy, only a reason not to sell.** Conversely, bad news gets treated as the last of the negatives — on November 9, the FDA added an eye-disease warning, which should have driven the stock down, yet it rose 4.28%, showing the market looks for a bullish interpretation even in bad news.

## Which are noise, and why

**The FDA warning on November 9 is pure noise.** NAION is a rare eye condition with an extremely low incidence rate; adding a warning label changes neither the indication, nor prescription volumes, nor reimbursement. The stock rose 4.28% because the shoe finally dropped — the market had feared harsher regulatory action, and all it got was a label update. **But trading a negative as a positive is itself a reading of overheating.**

**The three-day rally from October 14–20 is also noise.** No announcement, no data, no guidance, yet up 10.0% over five sessions. Such a move can only be attributed to flows (sector rotation, short covering, options gamma). **Its only informational content is this: the stock's short-term volatility is high, and the risk of chasing the rally is asymmetric.**

What really needs to be distinguished is the September slide — that was not noise. Oral Wegovy ramping (Q3 USD 811 million, 183,000 weekly prescriptions) and rising expectations of price cuts were both later confirmed by the earnings report (Foundayo 184 million, overseas pricing -29%). **The market gave the right answer in September; the October earnings report overturned it — what it overturned was sentiment, not facts.**

## What volatility tells us

Spread out the daily chart for these three months and a few numbers stand out: 63 trading days, average daily gain of 0.26%, daily volatility of 1.99% (about 31.6% annualized), largest single-day gain of +6.63% (the day after earnings), largest single-day drop of -3.76% (November 5, with no obvious announcement). **Annualized volatility of 31.6% means a USD 1.33 trillion market cap swinging USD 40 billion a day is the norm.**

For buyers this number carries a direct message: chasing a name with 30%+ volatility means knowingly taking on the odds of "buying at the peak of sentiment." Over the past three months, the stock moved more than 2.5% in a single day on 8 trading days, 5 of which had nothing to do with fundamentals. **Waiting for a -10% pullback is not hard; what is hard is having the nerve to buy when it arrives.**
