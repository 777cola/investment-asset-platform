Core view

Between August 11 and November 9, Eli Lilly's share price rose 16.2% from 1,215.02 to 1,411.95, and is up 26.6% from the September 11 interim low of 1,115.70. Only one thing genuinely moved the fundamentals over these three months: the Q3 report on October 29. The other six items—retatrutide data, the oral-drug war of words, the FDA eye-safety warning, the record highs—moved sentiment only. Separating the two is the precondition for judging whether USD 1,411.95 is a buy.

The only substantive positive: the Q3 report on October 29

The stock rose 6.63% on earnings day and a further 3.19% the next, up 10.0% over the two sessions. This is not sentiment; it is numbers: revenue of USD 24.397 billion beat consensus by 7.9%, adjusted EPS of USD 7.48 beat by 19.5%, and full-year revenue guidance was raised from 85–87 billion to 91–92 billion (midpoint +USD 5.5 billion). All three beat, and this is the third guidance raise.

The market is not buying this quarter's profit; it is buying the act of raising guidance itself — a company that raises guidance three times in a row has high visibility into demand. But note: in the same report, EPS guidance was cut (35.50–36.50 → 35.00–36.00) due to USD 4.2 billion in acquisition costs. The market chose to look only at the revenue line. That is evidence of sentiment, not of reason.

The one thing most worth learning: good data can still fall

On September 29, Eli Lilly released Phase III TRIUMPH-2 data for retatrutide: the 12mg dose delivered average weight loss of 20.8% (about 22.5 kg) at 80 weeks, with one-third of patients losing more than 25%; the company plans to file for approval in Q1 2027. This ranks among the best data seen in the weight-loss drug space in the past decade.

The stock fell 0.01% that day and 2.33% the next. Why? Because the market had already priced it in — the "next-generation pipeline" underpinned a 48x P/E, and the data merely confirmed rather than surprised. This is what fully loaded expectations look like: good news is no longer a reason to buy, only a reason not to sell. Bad news, by contrast, gets read as the last of the negatives — on November 9, the FDA added an eye-disease warning, which should have driven the stock down, yet it rose 4.28%, showing the market looks for a bullish interpretation even in bad news.

Which are noise, and why

The FDA warning on November 9 is pure noise. NAION is a rare eye condition with an extremely low incidence rate; a warning label changes neither the indication, nor prescription volumes, nor reimbursement. The stock rose 4.28% because the shoe finally dropped — the market had feared harsher regulatory action and got only a label update. But trading a negative as a positive is itself a sign of overheating.

The three-day rally from October 14–20 is also noise. No announcement, no data, no guidance, yet the stock gained 10.0% over five sessions — a move attributable only to flows (sector rotation, short covering, options gamma). Its only informational content: short-term volatility is high, and the risk of chasing the rally is asymmetric.

The September slide is the one that demands attention — that was not noise. The oral Wegovy ramp (Q3 USD 811 million, 183,000 weekly prescriptions) and rising expectations of price cuts were both later confirmed by the earnings report (Foundayo 184 million, overseas pricing -29%). The market gave the right answer in September; the October earnings report overturned it — and what it overturned was sentiment, not facts.

What volatility tells us

Lay out the daily chart for these three months and a few numbers stand out: 63 trading days, average daily gain of 0.26%, daily volatility of 1.99% (roughly 31.6% annualised), largest single-day gain of +6.63% (the day after earnings), largest single-day drop of -3.76% (November 5, with no obvious announcement). Annualised volatility of 31.6% means a USD 1.33 trillion market cap swinging USD 40 billion a day is the norm.

For buyers the message is direct: chasing a name with 30%+ volatility means knowingly taking on the odds of "buying at the peak of sentiment." Over the past three months the stock moved more than 2.5% in a single day on 8 trading days, 5 of which had nothing to do with fundamentals. Waiting for a -10% pullback is not hard; what is hard is having the nerve to buy when it arrives.

Conclusion

Eight events in three months; only the Q3 report changed fundamentals. The other seven swung sentiment back and forth, yet the stock still travelled an extra 20% on them. This is the signature of "good company plus high valuation": volatility comes from sentiment, trend from earnings. For buyers, that volatility is opportunity—wait for sentiment to ebb and the price returns to a level that matches earnings. The USD 1,115.70 of September 11 was the last such level, and it was only two months ago—same company, same fundamentals, a 26.6% price gap. That is the price of sentiment.
