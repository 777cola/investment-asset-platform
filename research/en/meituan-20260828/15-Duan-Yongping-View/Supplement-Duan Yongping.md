# Meituan（03690）— Duan Yongping Supplement

- Research date：2026/08/28
- Price：77.5 HKD
- Market cap：Approximately HKD 478.6 billion (6.175 billion shares)

## Notes

This Q&A-style analysis applies Duan Yongping's investment framework and voice; it is not a statement by him. Data sources are as before: Meituan's 2026 interim results announcement, 2025 annual results announcement and the August 28, 2026 earnings call. The valuation range is consistent with m04/m17.

## Q: Is this a good business?

Good — but it depends what you compare it with.

Against Moutai, it is not good. Moutai can raise prices without asking anyone; Meituan has to negotiate with merchants, users and regulators. Against JD.com, it is far better. JD.com's self-operated retail operating margin is 4.6%, while Meituan's core local commerce can reach a steady-state 20.9% — on the same yuan of revenue, Meituan earns four times what JD.com does.

So the precise answer is: this is a business that is very good in good years and very bad in bad years.It earned RMB 35.8 billion in 2024 and lost RMB 23.355 billion in 2025, with revenue differing by only 8 points. With a business like this, you have to accept its volatility.

## Q: Will it still be around in ten years?

Yes — and most likely still number one.

Demand will not disappear: people always need to eat, cities always need delivery, and once the 30-minute habit takes hold there is no going back. Supply is harder to replicate — 6 million riders, 8 million merchants and a dispatch network spanning 2,800 counties and districts represent time that new entrants cannot buy with money.

But "still there" does not equal "still profitable." In ten years it could be a utility-type company earning RMB 20 billion a year with a market cap of RMB 500 billion, or a consumer-infrastructure company earning RMB 50 billion a year with a market cap of RMB 1.5 trillion. The difference lies not in the company but in the competitive landscape. That is why I only buy a little: I can clearly see that it will still be there in ten years, but I cannot clearly see how much it will earn in ten years.

## Q: Can you understand it?

That much is understandable. Three revenue lines — delivery services RMB 26.78 billion, merchant services RMB 39.60 billion, product sales RMB 5.15 billion — against one cost line, riders and fulfilment, and two expense lines, marketing at RMB 24.72 billion and R&D at RMB 7.67 billion. Half an hour and it is explained.

Only one piece defies explanation: when the subsidies stop. That is not the company's problem but the industry's, and no one can forecast an industry problem — Wang Xing included. All he could say on the earnings call was that "industry subsidies remain higher than in 2024 and will need several quarters to normalize."

So "understandable" means precisely this: I know how it makes money, and I know what I am betting on. I am not betting that Meituan emerges stronger; I am betting that its rivals run the math and conclude the game does not pay. That is a bet I understand.

## Q: At what price to buy

HKD 77.5 is 15–18x 2027 normalized profit and 11–13x ex-net cash. Fair, but not cheap.

What counts as cheap? HKD 65 — about 12.8x, 9x ex-cash, a 1:3 risk-reward. That is where the company itself bought back stock in June, and it is also the 52-week low. What counts as expensive? Above HKD 110, over 20x — the price at which "the recovery has already succeeded."

My approach: buy a little at HKD 77.5 (2%), buy more at HKD 65 (4%–5%), sell half at HKD 110, sell the rest at HKD 150. Ignore tomorrow's moves; ask only whether the risk-reward at that price is worth it.

## Q: What is the biggest fear

The biggest fear is not losing money but this industry never returning to rationality.

If the three players keep fighting, each losing money and each refusing to exit, the end result is an industry that survives and companies that survive, but no profits. That state can persist for a long time—Chinese internet has precedents: video sites fought for ten years and three remained, none of them making big money.

The second fear is rider costs spiralling out of control. From occupational injury protection to pension insurance to the full five insurances and one housing fund, every step is irreversible. A RMB 1 rise in per-order cost within three years is RMB 25 billion a year—more than normalized profit in 2027. This is not a black swan but a visible grey rhino.

The third fear is overseas expansion. Brazil at USD 1 billion over 5 years—if that becomes USD 3 billion over 5 years, the nature of the investment changes. So I wrote "Brazil quarterly loss exceeding RMB 2 billion" as one of the falsification conditions.

## Q: What would you do if it fell 30%?

First, distinguish "the stock falling" from "the business changing."

If the business has changed — Q3 core local commerce operating profit drops below RMB 2 billion, Alibaba re-escalates, merchant services turn negative — admit the mistake, cut to 1% or exit entirely, regardless of cost basis.

If it is the stock falling — fundamentals unchanged, only market sentiment — then add. A 30% drop from 77.5 is HKD 54, roughly 10x 2027 normalized profit and 7x ex-cash. At that price, as long as the business hasn't changed, I will add up to 6%.

The key to this framework: decide what to do if it falls before you buy. Decide after buying, and emotion will carry you away.

## Q: Under what circumstances would you change your view?

Three scenarios.

First, Q3 core local commerce operating profit below RMB 2 billion. That would make Q2 a peak-season illusion, the inflection would not hold, and I would cut to below 1% and concede I misread the slope.

Second, Alibaba steps up subsidies again. That is not Meituan's fault; my assumption that "the competitor will do the math" would be what was wrong. I would wait rather than rush to add, because a restarted subsidy war could run four quarters.

Third, Meituan does something foolish itself — restarting a price war to grab share, say, or spending well above USD 1 billion in Brazil. The former would mean management has changed; the latter, that capital-allocation discipline is gone.

Share-price moves outside these three scenarios will not change my view. No adding on dips, no chasing rallies — I watch only these three. That is what "knowing why you bought" means: anyone who cannot explain why they bought cannot explain when to sell either.
