Core view

Management is the least of this report's worries and the most deserving of scrutiny. The people are not the issue: Wang Xing, 47, holds about 9.3% of shares but controls 45.3% of voting rights through A/B shares and has not sold a single share since 2010; Mu Rongjun runs technology and HR, Chen Shaohui runs finance and Wang Puzhong runs core local commerce—all four have been with the company for over a decade.Capital allocation is what needs watching: zero dividends since the 2018 IPO, a USD 2 billion buyback programme that has run for two years with only a fraction used, and the June 2026 round, which spent HKD 200 million over two days—a rounding error against a HKD 478.6 billion market cap.The good news is that management made the right trade-offs in the subsidy war; the bad news is that even at HKD 64 a share, it did not buy its own stock in size.

All four positions are held by veterans with more than ten years' tenure

Meituan's power structure is simple: Wang Xing sets direction, Mu Rongjun runs the organisation, Chen Shaohui runs the money and Wang Puzhong runs the battles. Three of the four are veterans from 2010–2015; there are no parachuted star executives and no visible succession race. That is rare among Chinese platform companies—most turn to "professional management" once they reach a USD 50 billion market cap. Meituan has not.

Wang Xing's control rests on a dual-class share structure: 515,869,783 Class A shares, representing 45.30% of Class A voting rights. Mu Rongjun is the other WVR beneficiary, and together the two hold an effective veto over shareholder meeting resolutions. Their economic interest is only about 9.3%, yet their control is close to half. The upside is that long-termism is protected—no one can force him to cut long-term investment for a single quarter's profit; the downside is that minority shareholders have no checks on him.

At the shareholder communication meeting on June 25, 2026, Wang Xing said he "has not sold a single share since the company was founded and has no plan to sell," while Chen Shaohui said "the company's value is currently severely undervalued." Said at a share price of HKD 64, such words are worth more than at HKD 200.

Money: right strategy, stingy buybacks, zero dividends

Start with what is right. The June 2023 acquisition of Light Year for RMB 2.065 billion brought in Wang Huiwen's team and compute at a price equivalent to a seed-round valuation; two years on, the LongCat model series and the two AI assistants "Xiaomei" and "Xiaotuan" are what that money bought. R&D in 2026Q2 was RMB 7.67 billion, 7.3% of revenue; at the height of the subsidy war the company neither cut R&D nor went off to build a "Token factory" — that restraint is correct.

Now what is not right. A USD 2 billion buyback was approved in June 2024, yet by November of that year only about USD 600 million had been executed; after reauthorisation in June 2026, the company bought 2.92 million shares for HKD 200 million over two days, 0.04% of market cap. Over the same period it held RMB 168.3 billion in cash and short-term investments. A net-cash company whose share price is -27% from its 52-week high, with management itself calling the stock "severely undervalued," spent only HKD 200 million on buybacks — not a capability problem, a willingness problem.

The dividend is zero; eight years listed and never paid one. For a growth company that is not wrong, but in a profit-recovery phase buybacks are the only capital-allocation tool that can grow per-share value. This item is why the management score falls from 9 to 7.

Integrity and governance: pushed to change three times

Meituan has been "pushed into change" three times in its history. None were voluntary, but each was carried through.

The first was the October 2021 antitrust penalty: a fine of RMB 3.442 billion, refund of RMB 1.289 billion in exclusive-cooperation deposits, and a finding that "choose one of two" restricted competition. The platform subsequently dropped merchant exclusivity requirements and made its commission structure transparent — sacrificing near-term merchant lock-in but defusing the "choose one of two" regulatory landmine over the long term.

The second was rider rights. Pilots for occupational injury protection began in 2022; 2025 brought nationwide coverage of rider pension subsidies (the industry's first scheme covering all rider types); and on July 1, 2026, "new occupational injury" insurance rolled out nationwide with premiums fully borne by the platform. This is real cost, and also a real compliance asset — after the State Administration for Market Regulation issued the national standard "Basic Requirements for Service Management of Food Delivery Platforms" in December 2025, whoever finishes first takes fewer penalties.

The third was the 2025 "anti-involution" campaign. Regulatory interviews, the national standard, and the Central Economic Work Conference's framing of "win-win between platform companies and workers" — Meituan publicly stated firm opposition to involution, and profit turned positive in 2026Q2. The common thread across all three: the company never moves first, but never digs in either. The implication for investors: policy risk is manageable, but do not expect it to act ahead of regulators.

Succession: no shortage of candidates, but no "next Wang Xing"

Wang Xing is 47, so succession talk is premature. The organizational structure, however, is already in place: the 2024 restructuring handed core local commerce to Wang Puzhong, freeing Wang Xing to focus on AI and overseas, with Mu Rongjun overseeing organization and HR and Chen Shaohui overseeing capital. This three-way split — someone running the business, someone managing the money, someone setting direction — is healthier than at many companies.

The real succession risk is not people but judgment. Every key decision at Meituan over the past decade — the 2015 merger with Dianping, the 2018 IPO, the 2021 retreat from community group buying, the 2025 anti-involution push — was Wang Xing's call. All share one trait: choosing to contract while everyone else was expanding. That counterintuitive judgment cannot be institutionalized, nor handed over.

Management therefore scores 7: 9 for the current lineup, 6 for capital allocation, 5 for transferability.

A detail in the shareholding structure

One easily overlooked detail in Meituan's A/B share structure: Wang Xing holds only about 9.3% of economic interest but 45.30% of voting power, while Mu Rongjun holds about 2.0% and is also a WVR beneficiary. Together they hold a de facto veto over ordinary resolutions at the shareholders' meeting.

The upside of this structure is strategic stability — no barbarians at the gate, no short-term performance pressure. The downside: minority shareholders have no say on dividends, buybacks or major capex (Brazil, for example).

The HKD 200 million buyback in June 2026 is a case in point: management called the stock "severely undervalued," yet the action amounted to just 0.04% of market cap. Minority shareholders cannot propose, cannot veto and cannot demand an explanation. This has to go into the governance discount — of the 7 points I give management, 1 is deducted here.

Conclusion

Management clears the bar, but only conditionally: the right person is in place, but the money isn't being spent aggressively enough. Wang Xing's trade-offs in the subsidy war, his restraint on AI and his catch-up on rider rights all point to a man running a three-year ledger, not a three-month one. But a net-cash company trading at a 30% discount, whose own CEO calls the stock undervalued, buying back only HKD 200 million — that needs explaining.

The metric to watch is simple: the size of the next buyback round. If buybacks in the second half of 2026 exceed HKD 2 billion (about 0.4% of market cap), intent has returned; if they stay at a token few hundred million, strip shareholder returns out of the valuation entirely.
