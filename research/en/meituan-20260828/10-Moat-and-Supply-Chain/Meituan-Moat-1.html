Core view

Meituan's moat is physical, not psychological. Network density 9, cost advantage 8, merchant ecosystem 7, brand 6, switching costs 5, licences 4 — it barely needs licences, and so has no licence protection. The implication: that mix keeps out new entrants but not the giants already in the field, because a giant can buy everything except density; the one thing it cannot buy is time. The most dangerous rival is not JD (share down to 7%) but Taobao Shangou, which has 800 million main-site users, Ele.me's delivery capacity as a base, and the ability to book food-delivery losses on the e-commerce P&L.

Position in the value chain: it must handle fulfilment itself

Meituan's position in the industry chain is unusual: it is both a platform (distributing demand, collecting commissions) and a fulfillment provider (employing its own riders, bearing the costs itself). Most platform companies choose an asset-light model—matching only, outsourcing fulfillment. Meituan did not, because it tried: outsourced delivery could not control timeliness, user experience collapsed, and the gateway was lost.

The upside of this choice is a harder moat: the fulfillment network is a physical asset that requires time, ground-level outreach, algorithms and rider relationships to accumulate simultaneously. JD.com and Alibaba spent two years and over RMB 100 billion, yet their combined order volume is still less than half—that is why.

The downside is that it will always be heavier than a pure platform: rider costs are rigid, social insurance compliance costs rise year by year, and insufficient density in any single county drags down overall unit economics. So Meituan's moat is a time barrier bought with heavy assets, not pricing power bought with brand.

Supply chain: supply-side control is weakening

Meituan's "supply chain" is not a goods supply chain but two chains: merchant supply + rider capacity.

Merchant side: roughly 8 million active merchants are an asset, yet merchant services revenue grew only 4.0% in Q2 2026, the weakest of all metrics. Two reasons. First, Douyin used low commissions to take chain brands and influencer shops. Second, merchants are themselves cutting costs, and advertising budgets are being cut harder than commissions. Control over this supply chain is declining, and the decline is structural.

Rider side: roughly 6 million riders with orders, one of the largest flexible labour pools in the world. Meituan did three things to stabilise it: nationwide coverage of pension insurance subsidies (the industry's first to cover all rider types), a nationwide rollout of occupational injury protection on July 1, 2026, and an algorithm addition of a "pop-up reminder to rest after 4 cumulative hours of deliveries." All three add cost, but what they buy is capacity stability, and capacity stability is the precondition for delivery timeliness.

Supply chain judgment: the rider chain is more stable than the merchant chain. Merchants can leave; riders will not—because Meituan offers the densest order density in the country, and riders cannot earn the same money elsewhere.

The most dangerous rival: Taobao Shangou, not JD

JD.com made the loudest entrance and suffered the worst outcome: its share once reached 31% and now sits at 7%, with new businesses losing nearly RMB 10 billion in a single quarter. It lost because it was wrong from the start—no rider network, no merchant base, yet it chose to fight on someone else's home turf. On July 17 it announced that 100% of its full-time riders would sign labour contracts with full social insurance and housing fund contributions—ahead on compliance, heavier on cost.

The real danger is Taobao Shangou. It is not really in food delivery; it is using food delivery as a traffic funnel: leveraging the 800 million users of the main site plus subsidies, it took order volume from 0 to over 40% in under two years. Worse, the math works for it—food delivery losses can be covered by e-commerce advertising.

Two turning signals emerged in 2026, however: Alibaba's HKD 80 billion placement in August went entirely to AI infrastructure rather than instant retail, and according to 36Kr, Alibaba is halving Taobao Shangou investment in the new fiscal year. If both hold true, Alibaba has done the math: food delivery is not worth unlimited investment. This is the most important external condition for Meituan's margin recovery.

The third rival, Douyin, is not on the food delivery battlefield—it is in in-store. In-store is Meituan's fattest profit pool (gross margin far above food delivery), and Douyin's in-store GTV grew nearly 60% in 2025 to over RMB 800 billion. Food delivery can be defended; in-store cannot—this is Meituan's most real battlefield over the next three years.

The Three-Kilometer Rule: The Spatial Boundary of a Moat

Every Meituan advantage is bounded by a single physical constraint: three kilometres. The rider's delivery radius, the user's willingness to wait and the merchant's catchment area all sit at this scale.

The implication is that Meituan's moat is not "one national network" but "2,800 independent networks." The economics of each network hinge on order density within that trade area — per-order cost in Beijing's Guomao may be half that of a county town.

"Scale effect" therefore warrants a discount when applied to Meituan: it has scale effects, but they are segmented, local and cannot be reallocated across regions. That explains why it loses to small local platforms in lower-tier cities, and why it must fund ground promotion county by county.

Investors readily mistake "largest nationally" for "strongest everywhere." In truth it is strongest in tier-1 and tier-2 cities; in county towns it merely "exists" — it is not "strong."

What if the competitor changes tack

Today's competition is a subsidy war. Were it to shift to either of the other two playbooks, Meituan's position would look entirely different.

The first is a quality war: rivals compete not on price but on food safety, on-time delivery and after-sales service. That hurts Meituan—quality wars demand investment, and Meituan's current margin cannot support the extra spending—but it is not fatal, since Meituan's fulfilment timeliness is already the strongest.

The second is an ecosystem war: rivals bundle food delivery with e-commerce, payments and video memberships, attacking through cross-subsidies. This is what Taobao Shangou is doing, and it is the most dangerous—because Meituan's ecosystem is only "eating," while rivals' ecosystems cover "food, clothing and daily necessities."

The third is a regulatory war: if regulators bar platforms from using subsidies to squeeze out competitors, the subsidy war would be forcibly terminated. That is good for Meituan, but it equally limits Meituan's ability to fight back.Of the three playbooks, the second is the most likely to happen and the hardest to defend against.

Conclusion

Moat conclusion: physical barriers (network, cost) score 8–9; non-physical barriers (brand, switching cost, licence) score 4–6. That mix makes the company unkillable, but it also means it cannot raise prices.

Investment implication: the moat supports "won't lose", not "high multiple". A 15–20x valuation is therefore reasonable — it pays for the certainty of "steady-state number one", not the imagination of "monopoly pricing power".
