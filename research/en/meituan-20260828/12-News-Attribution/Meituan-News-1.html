Core thesis

Only two events genuinely moved the share price between late May and late August: the June 1 Q1 report, which showed a loss of RMB 6.47 billion against a consensus loss of RMB 9.01 billion and lifted the Hong Kong shares 5% the next day, and the August 28 Q2 report, where operating profit turned positive at RMB 2.69 billion, far ahead of expectations. The other four items—the buyback mandate, the HKD 200 million buyback, the nationwide rollout of new work-injury coverage for riders, and JD's full-time rider social insurance and housing fund contributions—were directional signals with almost no impact on the share price over the period. Noise included Wang Xing's statement that he had "not sold a single share" and assorted disputes over who ranks first by market share. Over these three months the stock recovered from its 52-week low of HKD 64.25 to HKD 77.50, a gain driven by expectations of subsidy retreat rather than by earnings.

Signal one: Q1 results "lost less than expected"

The June 1 quarterly report showed revenue of RMB 91.04 billion, +5.6%, an operating loss of RMB 6.47 billion and an adjusted loss of RMB 4.97 billion. Against consensus of a RMB 9.01 billion loss, the actual loss came in at just 70% of expectations—the first "better than expected" print of the year.

Hong Kong stocks rose about 5% the next day, showing the market understood: losses are narrowing, and narrowing on revenue growth of just 5.6%, driven by expense control. This is the first numerical validation of "anti-involution."

But note one detail: the Q1 operating loss of RMB 6.47 billion narrowed 59.8% from RMB 16.07 billion in Q4 2025. The narrowing was substantial, but the absolute figure remains a negative RMB 6-plus billion. What the market priced in at the time was "no longer deteriorating," not "starting to improve."

Signal two: the "RMB 2.69 billion" in the Q2 report

Q2 results, released after the close on August 28: revenue RMB 104.64 billion, +14.4%; operating profit RMB 2.69 billion, against consensus for a loss of RMB 846 million. An expected loss of RMB 850 million versus an actual profit of RMB 2.69 billion — a RMB 3.5 billion swing, the largest consensus surprise since 2025.

The stock nonetheless closed flat (HKD 77.50, 0% change), as the release came after the close and the session had not yet priced it in. These results can only be priced on the next trading day — which is why the current level (HKD 77.5) still does not fully reflect the information.

Core local commerce RMB 5.67 billion, +52.3%, margin 7.9%; new initiatives lost RMB 1.74 billion, loss ratio 5.3% — three metrics improved simultaneously, and that, rather than the RMB 2.69 billion itself, is what the market will re-rate.

Noise: three things that were over-interpreted

First, Wang Xing "didn't sell a single share." That is a fact and a statement, but it changes no cash flow. The founder holds 9.3% and 45.3% of voting rights, so there was never any motive to sell — selling one share would send the market a signal. Treating this line as a reason to buy mistakes governance structure for earnings.

Second, the HKD 200 million buyback: 2.9248 million shares at HKD 66.5–69, or 0.04% of market cap. The scale looks more like a "statement buyback" — it proves the company is buying, not that the company thinks the stock is cheap. The real signal is the second half: if cumulative buybacks exceed HKD 2 billion, that is conviction.

Third, the dispute over share metrics. "Meituan 51%," "Meituan 60%," "Taobao Shangou 45.7%" — three numbers from three bases (orders, GTV, revenue), which the media often mix. When you see a share news item, the first question is "which basis," the second is "who calculated it."

Attribution: what drove the gains over these three months

52-week range HKD 64.25–106.30; at HKD 77.50 the stock sits 27.1% below its high but roughly 20% above the late-June low.

Earnings explain little of that 20%: the Q1 report showed only a narrower loss, and the Q2 report has yet to be priced in after hours. The rally is really a bet on a "subsidy retreat" — reports that Alibaba halved its investment, that JD.com is pivoting to a compliance narrative, and that regulators are framing "anti-involution". In other words, the market is buying "the war is over", not "Meituan is getting stronger".

The distinction matters. If "the war is over" is the driver, a restart of the war gives back the entire gain; if "Meituan is getting stronger" is the driver, the valuation centre shifts up. For now only the former can be confirmed, not the latter — which is why we are buying only 2%.

One piece of information that has not been priced in

The August 28 results landed after the close, with the stock finishing the session at HKD 77.50, up 0.0%. The market has therefore not yet priced in operating profit of RMB 2.69 billion, above the consensus of RMB 3.5 billion.

Contrast the Q1 report on June 1: a loss of RMB 6.47 billion against a consensus loss of RMB 9.01 billion, and the stock rose 5% the next day. That was merely a smaller loss than expected; this is a bigger profit than expected — a different order of magnitude.

The current level therefore offers an information-gap window. But an information gap is worth one day, not one month. If the stock opens up more than 8% on the next trading day, the window closes; if it opens flat or lower, the market is worried about Q3 guidance (management explicitly flagged rising costs), which is instead an opportunity.

Three-month share price structure

Splitting the share-price move from end-May to end-August into three phases shows what the market was trading.

Phase one (early June): a rebound off the lows as the Q1 loss narrowed above consensus. What rallied here was "no longer deteriorating".

Phase two (late June–July): the stock moved sideways in the HKD 65–70 range while the company bought back shares at HKD 66.5–69. This was "bottoming", with volume shrinking, indicating selling pressure was exhausted.

Phase three (August): the stock rose on Alibaba's earnings and reports of fading industry subsidies, reaching HKD 77.50 on August 28. What rallied here was the expectation that "the war is over".

The three phases together produced a gain of about 20%, but none of them was earnings-driven — the real pricing of earnings is still to come after August 28.

Conclusion

Six events in three months: two are signals (two earnings reports), four carry directional information and three are noise. Conclusion: the 20% share-price rebound rests on expectations, not results; genuine pricing of results must wait for trading days after August 28.

Trading implication: do not chase the rally on the first day after earnings. If it opens up more than 10%, wait for a pullback; if it opens flat or lower, that is a chance to add. The real test comes with the Q3 report at the end of October.
