VII. Risk Checklist: Five Things That Would Kill This Investment — Meituan

Every item gives a trigger signal, so you don't have to wait for earnings to find out

Core view

Five risks, ranked by probability × impact: a further step-up in rider costs (40% probability, RMB 10–25 billion impact); a renewed subsidy war (30%, RMB 15 billion profit impact); in-store share loss to Douyin (35%, a third of the profit pool); Keeta's overseas expansion running out of control (30%, RMB 5–10 billion a year); and a continued slide in share (20%, revenue growth falling to single digits).Three of the five can be tracked through public signals ahead of the quarterly report.

Risk 1: Rider costs are the gray rhino

The trajectory is clear: occupational injury protection pilot (2022) → nationwide pension insurance subsidies (2025) → nationwide rollout of new occupational injury protection (July 1, 2026, premiums fully borne by platforms) → five social insurances and one housing fund (JD.com already covers all full-time riders). Each step converts "flexible employment" into "standard employment."

Under the most aggressive assumption—all full-time riders receive five social insurances and one housing fund within three years—per-order costs rise by RMB 0.5–1. At 70 million orders per day, that annualises to RMB 10–25 billion. Yet normalized profit in 2027 is only RMB 25 billion—this single risk could consume all of it.

What makes this risk unusual is its irreversibility: once policy lands, it will not be withdrawn; once costs rise, they will not fall. This is not "bad news that might happen"—it is "a cost that will definitely happen."

Risk 2: Subsidy war restarts

Three triggers: Alibaba's quarterly instant-retail loss widening again, Taobao Shangou no longer halving its investment, or JD.com announcing a fresh round of subsidies.

Why only 30%? Because the rivals have done the math too. Alibaba lost about RMB 100 billion over five quarters against incremental revenue of RMB 33–35 billion; JD.com lost about RMB 40 billion over two years, with share falling from 31% to 7%. Neither has reason to double down.

But "no reason" does not mean "won't do it." Chinese internet history is full of rational companies making irrational decisions—especially when "share" is treated as a strategic metric. The observation window for this risk is Alibaba's November 2026 quarterly report.

Risk 3: In-store business eroded by Douyin

This is the most underappreciated point: the market fixates on food delivery share and ignores in-store, yet in-store is Meituan's fattest profit pool.

Douyin's in-store GTV grew nearly 60% in 2025 to over RMB 800 billion, as low commissions plus content traffic won it chain brands and viral stores. Merchant services revenue grew only 4.0% in 2026Q2, a direct reflection of that pressure.

If in-store operating margin falls from above 30% to below 25%, Meituan's profit pool shrinks by 30% — a bigger hit than food delivery share sliding from 51% to 45%, because in-store carries almost no fulfillment cost.

Risk 4 and 5: Overseas expansion and market share

Overseas expansion: Brazil has signed a 5-year USD 1 billion agreement, pitting Meituan against Didi's 99Food. Saudi Arabia reaching profitability in 22 months is strong evidence, but Brazil combines low average order value, fierce subsidies and a complex tax regime. If Brazil's quarterly loss exceeds RMB 2 billion, the RMB 25 billion profit in the base case must be cut by 20%.

Share: 51% of order volume is the floor. A fall below 45% would signal that high average order value is also being eroded — that is the real danger. There is no evidence of this yet, but Goldman Sachs' estimates should be checked every six months.

Neither scenario is highly probable (30%, 20%), but both carry significant impact. Taken together, three of the five risks are "industry-wide" (riders, subsidies, in-store) and two are "company-specific" (overseas expansion, share) — the industry-wide ones are more lethal, because you cannot avoid them by switching companies.

The underestimated sixth risk: organization

All five risks sit at the business level; one more sits at the organizational level: Meituan is a company that runs on "strong execution," and strong execution depends on high-intensity KPIs and a ground-push culture.

That culture has been worn down over the past two years of subsidy war. Ground teams must simultaneously defend share, control costs, push new businesses (Xiaoxiang, Kuailehou) and comply with regulations (rider social insurance, transparent kitchens). Under multiple mandates, execution quality declines.

Evidence is hard to find, but there is one indirect indicator: merchant services revenue grew only 4.0% in Q2 2026. This could stem from competition, or from a decline in the service quality of ground teams. If the latter, the problem is messier than subsidies—because once organizational capability loosens, it takes years to tighten back.

This risk cannot be quantified and should not go into the model, but it deserves a place on the checklist: when looking at a company, beyond how much it earns, look at whether its troops are still willing to charge.

Conclusion

Risk conclusion: This company will not be killed, but it may be beaten down to the point where its margin never returns to 15%. Of the five risks, rider cost is the only one that is "almost certain to happen"; the other four are probabilistic events.

The response is not to avoid buying, but to buy small and set clear lines: Q3 core local commerce operating profit of RMB 2 billion, whether Alibaba restarts subsidies, and whether merchant services growth turns negative—hold these three lines and the risk is manageable.
