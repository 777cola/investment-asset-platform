V. Industry and Competition: A Business Where All Three Lose Money Together — Meituan

The subsidy math does not add up, so it will stop

Core view

The most counterintuitive feature of this industry: three giants are losing money in the same market at the same time, and all of them know the losses are meaningless. Alibaba burned roughly RMB 100 billion in instant retail over five quarters to buy RMB 33–35 billion of incremental revenue; JD's new businesses lost about RMB 9.85 billion in a single quarter and were left with just 7% share; Meituan lost RMB 17 billion in 2025. None of the three maths works, so all three are pulling back — the most important industry change of 2026.

Alibaba: the most dangerous rival, and the first to get the math right

Taobao Shangou won over 40% order-volume share within a year on three things: traffic from 800 million main-site users, Ele.me's delivery capacity base, and e-commerce profits that can subsidise food-delivery losses.

The numbers, though, look ugly: roughly RMB 100 billion of losses over five quarters, and after stripping out existing Ele.me and Hema revenue, incremental revenue of only RMB 33–35 billion.In other words, it spends RMB 3 to buy RMB 1 of revenue.That ratio does not hold up in any industry, unless you believe "food-delivery users will convert into e-commerce users"—and three years have produced no evidence of that.

So in 2026 it made two moves: an August placement of HKD 80 billion, directed entirely to AI infrastructure; and, per 36Kr, Taobao Shangou investment halved in the new fiscal year.This is not surrender but prioritisation—AI returns are clearly higher than food delivery.

JD.com: Misapplying Its Strengths

JD.com's playbook was "quality food delivery plus compliant full-time riders." Both calls were right; the execution was the worst of both worlds: share fell from a peak of 31% to 7%, and new businesses lost about RMB 9.85 billion in a single quarter.

Where did it go wrong? It treated food delivery as a logistics business. Food delivery is a merchant-density business — users open the app for "this store is available," not for "fast delivery." JD.com had capacity but no merchants; Meituan had merchants and built up its own capacity. The sequencing of these two things decided the outcome.

It got one thing right, though: on July 17 it announced that 100% of full-time riders would sign labour contracts and receive the five social insurances and one housing fund. That is leading-edge on compliance and suicidal on cost — it will force the entire industry to follow, Meituan included. On that reading, JD.com is a driver of industry cost inflation, not a beneficiary.

Douyin: absent from food delivery, present in in-store services

Douyin never made a serious push into food delivery—it tried, then pulled back. Its business is in-store: group-buy vouchers, store-exploration videos, redemption. In 2025, in-store GTV grew nearly 60%, surpassing RMB 800 billion.

This poses a bigger threat to Meituan than food delivery, because in-store is Meituan's fattest profit pool: no fulfillment, no riders, high gross margin. Douyin is carving into it with low commissions plus content traffic—a direct attack on Meituan's profit pool.

Merchant services revenue grew just 4.0% in 2026Q2, a reflection of that pressure. Food delivery can be defended; in-store cannot—this is Meituan's real battlefield over the next three years, and the risk the market is most prone to underestimate.

Endgame: 6:3:1, not winner-takes-all

Many investors value Meituan on a winner-takes-all assumption. I think that is wrong. Fulfillment density has a physical ceiling: order volume in a single trade area cannot support three players all earning a profit at once, but it can support two—because users need to compare prices.

I therefore assume a steady-state share of 6:3:1 by GTV: Meituan 60%, Taobao Shangou 30%, JD.com 10%. On that structure, industry average operating margin is 10%–15%, not the 20.9% of 2024.

That assumption drives the valuation: under winner-takes-all (80% share, 20% margin), Meituan is worth HKD 200; under 6:3:1 (60% share, 12%–13% margin), HKD 110. I assume the latter, hence my HKD 110.

Three evidence chains for the subsidy pullback

"The subsidy retreat" is the core assumption behind this investment and needs evidence, not intuition. Three chains of evidence:

First, funding. Alibaba raised HKD 80 billion in an August 2026 placement, all of it directed to AI infrastructure; according to 36Kr, Taobao Shangou investment will be halved in the new fiscal year. Where the money goes is where the priority lies.

Second, costs. JD.com announced on July 17 that 100% of its full-time riders would sign labour contracts and receive the five social insurances and one housing fund; Meituan extended new occupational injury insurance nationwide on July 1. Both are adding costs to themselves—companies adding costs do not simultaneously increase subsidies.

Third, the financials. Meituan's Q2 core local commerce operating profit was RMB 5.67 billion, +52.3%, driven by "reduced user incentive spending." Had the industry still been raising subsidies, that number could not have appeared.

All three point in the same direction, but note: they prove "subsidies are retreating," not "subsidies have fully retreated." Management itself says current subsidy levels remain above 2024.

Conclusion

Industry conclusion: Subsidies will retreat, not because anyone won, but because none of the three can make the math work; how far they retreat determines whether Meituan is worth HKD 60 or HKD 110. Our base case is a steady state of 6:3:1, implying HKD 110.
