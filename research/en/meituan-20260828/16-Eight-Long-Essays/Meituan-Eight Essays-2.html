II. The Nature of the Business: What Is It Actually Selling? — Meituan

Three kilometers, thirty minutes — how much is that sentence worth?

Core view

Meituan does not sell meals; it sells "certainty" — you order at ten at night, and it guarantees delivery in thirty minutes. That certainty rests on three things: a capacity pool of 6 million riders, a dispatch network spanning 2,800 counties and districts, and supply density from 8 million merchants. The three reinforce one another, forming a virtuous cycle. But that cycle has a physical boundary: three kilometers. Beyond three kilometers, everything resets to zero.

Unit economics: a few cents of profit per delivery order

A RMB 30 delivery order breaks down like this: the user pays RMB 3–5 in delivery fees, the merchant pays a 15%–20% commission (RMB 4.5–6), and the platform collects a bit of advertising revenue; on the cost side are the rider delivery fee (RMB 6–8), payment channel fees, and allocated customer service and systems costs.

A rough calculation puts gross profit per order at RMB 1–2. That is why core local commerce revenue of RMB 71.53 billion in 2026Q2 produced only RMB 5.67 billion in profit—per-order profit is paper-thin, and the model relies on massive order volume to spread fixed costs.

This structure dictates two things: first, order volume must keep growing, or fixed costs cannot be spread thin enough; second, any rise in per-order cost (rider social insurance, fuel prices, algorithm compliance) directly eats into profit, because there is no buffer.

Density: the only scale effect

Delivery scale effects are not national; they are trade-area level. A rider working within three kilometers of the Guomao bridge can deliver 6–8 orders an hour; in a county town, only 3–4. The difference is not effort but order density—the denser the orders, the less time riders spend waiting and deadheading.

This explains a counterintuitive phenomenon: Meituan cannot beat local small platforms in lower-tier cities, not because it is weak, but because density is insufficient—order volumes in lower-tier cities cannot support its dispatch system.

Conversely, this also explains why JD.com and Alibaba cannot buy their way in: they can buy subsidies, users and riders, but they cannot buy "a large volume of orders in the same trade area at the same time." Density can only be cultivated through time and order volume. This is Meituan's hardest moat and its only true scale effect.

Three-sided network: merchants, riders, users

Meituan's network has three sides, and the value of each side depends on the other two: more merchants leads to more user choice, which leads to more orders, which leads to higher rider income, which leads to more riders, which leads to faster delivery, which leads to a better user experience, which leads to more users.

Starting this loop is extremely costly, but maintaining it is not cheap either. The battle in 2025 was a live test of that "maintenance cost": marketing expenses rose sharply, and group operating profit swung from +RMB 45.1 billion to -RMB 17 billion, a gap of RMB 62.1 billion. In other words, the annual "insurance premium" for maintaining this network runs to tens of billions of RMB.

For comparison: WeChat's network requires almost no maintenance cost (users come on their own), Taobao's network has a moderate maintenance cost (relying on the merchant ecosystem), and Meituan's network has the highest maintenance cost (relying on subsidies and rider costs). This is the inherent flaw in its business model, and the fundamental reason its valuation can only command 15-20x.

Ceiling: food delivery peaks, instant retail takes over

The ceiling for food delivery is now visible: high penetration in tier-1 cities, low average order value in county-level markets, and growth in the overall restaurant market down to around 5%. This business has shifted from "growth engine" to "cash cow."

Instant retail (Shanggou) is taking over. In 2026Q2, merchandise sales rose 78.9%, order frequency among existing Shanggou users increased, and Xiaoxiang Supermarket covered 68 cities.Shanggou's unit economics are better than food delivery's — higher average order value, broader categories, and consolidated fulfillment (multiple items per order).

But Shanggou is also an asset-heavy business: Xiaoxiang Supermarket must build warehouses, Kuailehou must open stores, and fresh-produce spoilage rates are high.The endgame for this business is not a "high-margin platform" but a "high-efficiency retailer" — it should be valued as retail, not as a platform.The market has not fully priced this in.

Why it must handle delivery itself

This question deserves separate discussion, because it is the key to understanding Meituan. Around 2015, Meituan tried outsourcing delivery to third-party labor companies, with poor results: uncontrollable timeliness, high rider attrition, and volatile user experience.

The reason is simple: delivery timeliness depends on "dispatch control." If riders are not under your management, you cannot temporarily raise prices on stormy days, cannot prioritize orders during peak hours, and cannot guarantee the 30-minute promise. Outsourcing means handing user experience to someone else.

So Meituan chose the heaviest path: building its own capacity pool, writing its own dispatch algorithms, and bearing riders' social insurance costs itself. This choice turned it into a "heavy company" and also let it build a moat others cannot replicate.

The cost: it can never have the 50% gross margin of a pure platform, and it must always face rising labor costs. This is the inherent contradiction of the business model — it traded "heaviness" for a moat, and "heaviness" also caps its profit margin.

Conclusion

Conclusion on the nature of the business: It sells certainty, earns money from density, and pays an insurance premium in the form of maintenance costs. The ceiling of this business is capped by "maintenance costs," and the floor is supported by "density barriers." Only by knowing these two boundaries can one know what it should be worth.
