II. Business Model: What Is It Actually Selling? — Meituan

Three kilometres, thirty minutes — what is that sentence worth?

Core view

Meituan does not sell meals; it sells certainty — order at ten at night and it guarantees delivery in thirty minutes. That certainty rests on three things: a pool of 6 million riders, a dispatch network spanning 2,800 counties and districts, and supply density from 8 million merchants. Each reinforces the others, forming a virtuous cycle. But the cycle has a physical boundary: three kilometers. Beyond three kilometers, everything resets to zero.

Unit economics: a few cents of profit per delivery order

A RMB 30 delivery order breaks down as follows: the user pays RMB 3–5 in delivery fees, the merchant pays a 15%–20% commission (RMB 4.5–6), and the platform collects a small amount of advertising revenue. On the cost side sit the rider delivery fee (RMB 6–8), payment channel fees, and allocated customer service and systems costs.

On a rough calculation, gross profit per order is RMB 1–2. That is why core local commerce revenue of RMB 71.53 billion in 2026Q2 produced only RMB 5.67 billion in profit: per-order profit is paper-thin, and the model depends on massive order volume to spread fixed costs.

This structure dictates two things. First, order volume must keep growing, or fixed costs cannot be spread thinly enough. Second, any increase in per-order cost—rider social insurance, fuel prices, algorithm compliance—flows straight through to profit, because there is no buffer.

Density: the only scale effect

Delivery scale effects are not national; they operate at trade-area level. A rider working within three kilometres of Guomao bridge can deliver 6–8 orders an hour; in a county town, only 3–4. The difference is not effort but order density—the denser the orders, the less time riders spend waiting and deadheading.

This explains a counterintuitive phenomenon: Meituan cannot beat local small platforms in lower-tier cities, not because it is weak, but because density is insufficient—order volumes in lower-tier cities cannot support its dispatch system.

Conversely, it also explains why JD.com and Alibaba cannot buy their way in: they can buy subsidies, users and riders, but they cannot buy "a large volume of orders in the same trade area at the same time." Density can only be cultivated through time and order volume. This is Meituan's hardest moat and its only true scale effect.

Three-sided network: merchants, riders, users

Meituan's network has three sides, and each side's value depends on the other two: more merchants widen user choice, which drives more orders, which lifts rider income, which attracts more riders, which shortens delivery times, which improves the user experience, which brings in more users.

Getting that loop started is extremely costly, but keeping it running is not cheap either. 2025 was a live test of that maintenance cost: marketing expenses rose sharply and group operating profit swung from +RMB 45.1 billion to -RMB 17 billion, a gap of RMB 62.1 billion. In other words, the annual "insurance premium" on this network runs to tens of billions of RMB.

By comparison, WeChat's network carries almost no maintenance cost (users come on their own), Taobao's carries a moderate one (relying on the merchant ecosystem), and Meituan's carries the highest (relying on subsidies and rider costs). That is the inherent flaw in its business model, and the fundamental reason its valuation can only command 15-20x.

Ceiling: food delivery peaks, instant retail takes over

Food delivery's ceiling is now in sight: tier-1 cities are highly penetrated, average order value in county-level markets is low, and growth in the wider restaurant market has slowed to around 5%. The business has gone from growth engine to cash cow.

Instant retail (Shanggou) is taking over. In 2026Q2 merchandise sales rose 78.9%, order frequency among existing Shanggou users increased, and Xiaoxiang Supermarket reached 68 cities.Shanggou's unit economics beat food delivery's: higher average order value, broader categories and consolidated fulfilment (multiple items per order).

But Shanggou is also asset-heavy. Xiaoxiang Supermarket has to build warehouses, Kuailehou has to open stores, and fresh-produce spoilage rates are high.The endgame here is not a high-margin platform but a high-efficiency retailer — it should be valued as retail, not as a platform.The market has not fully priced this in.

Why it must handle delivery itself

This question deserves separate discussion, because it is the key to understanding Meituan. Around 2015, Meituan tried outsourcing delivery to third-party labour companies, with poor results: uncontrollable timeliness, high rider attrition and a volatile user experience.

The reason is simple: delivery timeliness depends on dispatch control. If riders are not under your management, you cannot surge prices on stormy days, prioritise orders at peak hours or guarantee the 30-minute promise. Outsourcing means handing user experience to someone else.

So Meituan chose the heaviest path: building its own capacity pool, writing its own dispatch algorithms and bearing riders' social insurance costs itself. That choice turned it into a "heavy company", but it also let it build a moat others cannot replicate.

The cost: it can never earn the 50% gross margin of a pure platform, and it must always face rising labour costs. This is the inherent contradiction of the business model — it traded "heaviness" for a moat, and "heaviness" also caps its profit margin.

Conclusion

What this business really is: it sells certainty, monetises density, and pays an insurance premium in the form of maintenance costs. Maintenance costs cap the upside; density barriers underpin the downside. Only by understanding these two boundaries can you judge what it should be worth.
