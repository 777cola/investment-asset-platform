III. Moat Anatomy: Six Scores and Their Limits — Meituan

Why it keeps out new players but not incumbent giants

Core view

Break the moat into six scores: network density 9, cost advantage 8, merchant ecosystem 7, brand 6, switching cost 5, licences 4. The first two are physical barriers; the last four are social barriers. Physical barriers take a decade and hundreds of billions of RMB to replicate; social barriers can be bypassed with a single marketing campaign. That is why JD.com spent two years and over RMB 100 billion yet holds only 7% share, while Taobao Shangou took more than 40% in one year—the former was attacking a physical barrier, the latter was bypassing it.

Physical barriers: density and cost

Network density: 9 points. 6 million riders with orders, 2,800 counties and districts, minute-level dispatch — these three took time to build. JD and Alibaba can spend money to recruit riders, but riders recruited in low-density commercial districts cannot make money and will churn; they can spend money on subsidies, but users leave once subsidies stop. Density is the one thing money cannot buy directly.

Cost advantage: 8 points. Q2 2026 delivery service revenue RMB 26.78 billion, +13.1%, driven by a decline in per-order delivery cost, not by price increases. Lower per-order cost → higher per-order pay for riders → riders more willing to stay → more reliable delivery times → denser orders. This is a self-reinforcing loop; to break it, competitors must first lose more on cost.

Where points are deducted: both moats have geographic boundaries. Beyond three kilometers, density drops to zero; beyond China, rider costs and the regulatory environment are entirely different (Saudi Arabia is profitable, Brazil may not be).

Social barriers: merchants, brands, switching, licenses

Merchant ecosystem: 7 points. 8 million active merchants are an asset, but merchant services revenue grew only 4.0% in Q2 2026—bargaining power is eroding. Douyin is poaching chain brands with low commissions, and merchants themselves are cutting costs and slashing advertising. This moat still exists, but it is thinning.

Brand: 6 points. "Order takeout on Meituan" still holds, but only in high-ticket scenarios. Below RMB 30, subsidies have trained users to go with whoever is cheaper. Among price-sensitive users, the brand is worth almost nothing.

Switching costs: 5 points. Users need only download an app to switch platforms, and merchants listing on multiple platforms is the norm. This industry has no "lock-in," only "habit." Habit can be changed by RMB 1 billion in subsidies.

Licenses: 4 points. Takeout requires no license—an advantage (no entry restrictions) and a disadvantage (no entry protection). Regulation only adds costs, not moats.

Why Taobao Shangou can bypass it

Taobao Shangou's approach is not "build a better delivery network" but "flood orders in using main-app traffic." It does not attack density; it buys density — using the traffic of 800 million users to forcibly manufacture order volume within a trade area.

This is the only way to bypass physical barriers, and the only dangerous way: it must subsidize continuously; once subsidies stop, order volume falls, density returns to zero, and riders churn.

Its sustainability therefore depends on two things: how long Alibaba's cash flow can hold out, and whether e-commerce profits are willing to keep subsidizing food delivery. In August 2026, Alibaba's HKD 80 billion placement was directed entirely to AI infrastructure, and Taobao Shangou investment was halved for the new fiscal year — these two moves answer the question.

The Boundaries of the Moat: Three Kilometers and One Hundred Days

Meituan's moat has two boundaries, and both must be remembered.

The spatial boundary is three kilometers. Within each commercial district it is a standalone fortress, and the country's 2,800 counties and districts are 2,800 separate wars. This is both its strength (no one can take all of them at once) and its weakness (every fortress must be provisioned separately).

The temporal boundary is one hundred days. If subsidies persist for a hundred days, user habits shift; if they persist for a year, riders shift too. The 2025 war ran four quarters, and Meituan lost 14 percentage points and RMB 23.4 billion — that is the measured value of the temporal boundary.

The flip side of a moat: what it cannot protect

It is easy to describe a moat only in terms of "what it blocks"; more important is "what it cannot block."

It cannot block subsidies. In 2025, Taobao Shangou captured more than 40% of order volume in one year, proving that share can be bought as long as one is willing to lose money. A moat can prolong a war; it cannot end one.

It cannot block regulation. Rider social insurance, algorithm compliance, and standardized merchant fees are each a cost, and an irreversible one. A moat cannot stop policy.

It cannot block substitution. If consumer habits change (for example, more home cooking, wider adoption of prepared dishes), or technology changes (drone delivery maturing to the point where cost falls below labor), the physical basis of the moat will be rewritten. Meituan itself is investing in drones and autonomous vehicles—buying insurance for this moat.

So the real function of a moat is this: it guarantees it wins in "normal competition," but does not guarantee it thrives in "abnormal competition." That is why the score is 8, not 10.

Conclusion

Moat conclusion: total score of 39/60 across six factors — a "strong but not wide" moat. It ensures Meituan survives and is not displaced by new entrants, but it does not ensure it can raise prices, nor that it can hold off giants willing to lose money. That is the dividing line between 15–20x and 30x.
