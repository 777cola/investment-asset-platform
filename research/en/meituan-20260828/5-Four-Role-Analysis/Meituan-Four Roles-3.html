Industry Research (Munger's Lens) — Meituan

In an industry where all three lose money together, whoever figures it out first exits first

Core view

Instant retail is one of the few businesses in China that combines high frequency, hard demand and a high fulfillment barrier, and one of the few in which three giants lose money at the same time. In 2025 Meituan posted an operating loss of RMB 17 billion, Alibaba's flash-shopping business lost about RMB 100 billion cumulatively over five quarters, and JD.com's new businesses lost nearly RMB 10 billion in a single quarter. This is not an industry that fails to make money; all three are using profit to buy the same thing — users' ordering habits.

The Subsidy Ledger: Who Pays, and What the Subsidy Buys

Alibaba's numbers make the case most clearly. According to media estimates, Taobao Shangou racked up cumulative losses of about RMB 100 billion over five quarters, while incremental revenue over those five quarters, after stripping out existing Ele.me and Hema revenue, was only about RMB 33–35 billion. Paying RMB 100 billion for RMB 35 billion of incremental revenue is a trade that fails in any financial model—unless you treat it as marketing spend to acquire new users, and the annual retention value of a food-delivery user falls far short of the 100 billion/35 billion ratio.

This is where Munger would approve: irrational behavior cannot last forever, because someone always does the math. Reports in June 2026 already said Alibaba would halve Taobao Shangou investment in its new fiscal year; JD.com announced on July 17 that 100% of its full-time riders would be signed to labor contracts with full social insurance and housing fund contributions, permanently raising its cost structure and forcing it to pull back. The subsidy retreat is not a matter of anyone's conscience awakening—it is that no one can make the math work.

Three steps of cost

Industry costs are climbing three steps, and each one is irreversible.

Step one: rider protections. On 1 July 2026, "new occupational injury" insurance achieves nationwide coverage, with platforms bearing the full premium; JD.com announced that full-time riders receive the five social insurances and one housing fund. On a rough estimate of 70 million orders per day and RMB 0.1 per order, Meituan's annualized cost rises by about RMB 2.5 billion.

Step two: compliance. In December 2025, the State Administration for Market Regulation's national standard "Basic Requirements for Service Management of Food Delivery Platforms" took effect, regulating delivery time limits, algorithms and merchant fees — the costs saved in the past by using algorithms to compress time must now be given back.

Step three: the merchant ecosystem. Merchant service revenue grew only 4.0% in Q2 2026, close to stagnation. Commission rates cannot be raised, meaning platforms cannot pass costs on to merchants and must absorb them themselves.

Stack the three steps together, and the industry's medium-term margin ceiling is pushed lower. This explains why the normalized margin assumption for 2027 can only be 12%–13%, rather than 2024's 20.9%.

Endgame: One Dominant Player or Three-Way Split

Munger would ask: how many players will this industry have in ten years? My answer is three, but share will settle back toward roughly 6:3:1.

The reason is that fulfillment density has a physical ceiling: with three players subsidizing simultaneously in one trade area, riders' marginal returns diminish; whoever has higher density has lower unit cost, and the market eventually returns to steady state.Meituan's current 51% order volume and over 60% GTV are the floor of that steady state.Taobao Shangou can hold 30%–35% on main-site traffic, and JD.com can hold 5%–10% in quality food delivery.

But "three players splitting the market" means industry-average margins, not excess margins.This is the core valuation assumption: a 20x multiple for Meituan reflects its position as steady-state number one, not an ability to raise prices as a monopolist.

In-store: the truly underappreciated battleground

I spent a lot of space on food delivery in the industry analysis, but Meituan's fattest profit pool is actually in-store. In-store needs no fulfillment and no riders, and its gross margin is far higher than delivery's—it is the main source of the 20.9% operating margin at Core Local Commerce in 2024.

This pool is being eroded by Douyin. Douyin's in-store GTV grew nearly 60% in 2025 to more than RMB 800 billion, using low commissions plus content traffic to take chain brands and influencer shops.Merchant services revenue grew only 4.0% in Q2 2026—the first financial evidence of this erosion.

Munger would say:Delivery is the bone, in-store is the meat. The bone held, but the meat is being lost—that is more worrying than losing share.If the in-store operating margin falls from above 30% to 25%, the profit pool shrinks by 30%, and the 2027 assumption of RMB 25 billion would have to be cut to below RMB 20 billion.

Conclusion

Industry view: The subsidy retreat is structural (none of the three can make the numbers work), but the cost step-up is also structural (rider protections + compliance). The result is a lower mid-point for industry margins, falling from 20% in 2024 to 12%–15%, rather than a return to previous levels.
