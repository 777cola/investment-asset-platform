Core view

Buffett's habit when reading financial reports is to look at cash first, then profit. This interim report's cash looks impressive: operating cash flow of RMB 37.3 billion, 3x net profit. But the source is stretched accounts payable, not higher sales.

Cash: earned, but from the wrong source

Operating cash flow of RMB 37.3 billion against net profit of RMB 12.33 billion — a ratio of 3x. On the surface, earnings of exceptionally high quality, but the company itself explains why: a decrease in cash paid for goods purchased. In other words, it pushed back payments owed to suppliers.

Buffett would immediately ask: is this sustainable? No. Payable days have a ceiling, and suppliers have a floor. As the world's largest buyer, you can do this once, twice, but not year after year. And this kind of cash flow masks real operational deterioration — a company with sales volume down 15.7% whose cash flow improves is itself a signal.

Capex: this is why he and BYD parted ways

Over the past five years BYD has ploughed essentially all of its earnings back into production lines: more than ten domestic bases, four overseas vehicle plants, eight ro-ro ships. Cumulative free cash flow over the five years was negative. Shareholders have therefore received nothing from the company over that period other than share-price appreciation.

What Buffett values most is "owner earnings" — net profit minus the capex required to maintain competitiveness. On that measure, BYD's owner earnings may be negative. He trimmed his stake in 2022, publicly citing valuation, but behind the valuation lay this: for a company that must keep investing to defend its position, true profitability was overstated.

That does not make it a bad company. TSMC and Toyota are the same. It does, however, dictate the valuation method: P/E only, not DCF; a manufacturing multiple, not a consumer-stock multiple.

FX: a new, structural hole

Financial expenses swung from a net gain of RMB 3.2 billion a year ago to a net expense of RMB 5.1 billion — an RMB 8.3 billion reversal. This is not higher interest; it is FX loss. Overseas revenue accounts for 47.1% of the auto segment and 52.6% at group level, and with overseas assets and overseas receivables both rising, currency swings are starting to hit the income statement.

Buffett's view on FX losses is simple: "It's a variable I can't predict and can't control, so it has to be priced into the margin of safety." RMB 8.3 billion equals 67% of first-half net profit — large enough to strip the "profit improvement" story back to reality.

Conclusion

Buffett-style conclusion: the cash is real, the profit is real, the capex is real. Put the three together and you have a company that must grow just to stand still. He would start getting interested below 20x; at 27x he would not give it a second look.
