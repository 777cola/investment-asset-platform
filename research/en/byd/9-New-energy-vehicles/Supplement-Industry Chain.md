## Upstream, BYD can squeeze materials suppliers, but not miners or chipmakers

Batteries are the single largest cost block at 30%–40% of vehicle cost. BYD's strategy is to make in-house whatever it can: partial self-supply of cathodes and anodes, plus equity stakes in electrolyte and lithium mining.That buffers the company when material prices rise, but it also saddles it with heavy assets.

Two areas resist pressure. First, mines: when lithium prices ran from RMB 50,000/tonne to RMB 600,000/tonne in 2022, BYD could only accept it — its equity stakes in mines are too small to move pricing. Second, intelligent-driving chips: Nvidia and Horizon Robotics earn 50%–70% gross margins, and BYD is the buyer with no bargaining power.As the focus of competition in 2026 shifts from batteries to intelligent driving, this vulnerability will only become more pronounced.

## Midstream: in-house cell supply underpins every cost advantage

FinDreams Battery's self-supply rate exceeds 80% — the most fundamental difference between BYD and every other automaker. Peers must buy cells from CATL, surrendering 20%–24% of gross margin to the upstream supplier; BYD makes its own, keeping that profit in-house. 2026H1 automotive gross margin reached 23.0% in Q2, above the 18.85% group gross margin, precisely because cell profit is internalised.

The cost is capex. A battery plant requires RMB 10 billion-level investment, and when utilisation is insufficient, depreciation eats into profit. 2026H1 sales volume fell 15.7%, utilisation declined, and this pressure will surface first in gross margin, only partly masked by the overseas mix dividend.

## Downstream: price wars at home versus premium pricing overseas — an uneven pair of legs

Downstream splits into two markets with entirely different logic. China is a share fight in a stagnant pool: the industry grew 7.3%, BYD fell 15.7%, and the price war has cut net profit per vehicle to just RMB 0.72 million (and that is the improved figure). Selling one car in China earns RMB 7,200, among the lowest of any automaker globally.

Overseas is the growth market: exports of 79.2 million vehicles, up 67.8% YoY; overseas automotive revenue of RMB 129.5 billion, up 55.8% YoY, with markedly higher gross margins. The same product, sold somewhere else, earns a good deal more — that is the value of distribution and scarce supply.

But this premium has a shelf life. Once plants in Thailand, Brazil, Hungary and Uzbekistan come online, local supply will rise fast, and the price war will follow the industry abroad. BYD is itself the one adding that supply, and it knows this full well.

## Cycle position: the penetration-rate dividend is over, but the overseas expansion window remains open

The 2026 picture can be summed up in one sentence: China is clearing out, overseas is expanding, and the two timelines are three years apart.

China: penetration has passed 50%, industry growth has slowed to 7.3%, capacity is oversupplied, and the price war that began in 2023 has not let up. Following the path the home appliance industry took from 2005 to 2015, consolidation needs another 3–5 years, during which leaders' margins will be compressed to 3%–5%.

Overseas: Chinese automakers have roughly 3–4 years of overseas expansion window left. After that, local plants come online, local competitors wake up, and tariffs and compliance thresholds rise, narrowing the premium. BYD's four plants are racing to seize time within this window—win the race and they become long-term assets; lose it and they become sunk costs.

Investment implication: profit growth over the next three years will come mainly from overseas, and in China, stopping the decline is victory. So the value of the signal "monthly domestic sales turn positive" lies not in how much profit it brings, but in proving that "the bleeding has stopped."

## Its position in the chain: comprehensive, but asset-heavy

Within the chain, BYD is the only player involved in everything "from mine to car": lithium mine stakes, in-house materials, in-house cells, in-house chips, in-house vehicles, its own fleet and its own charging network.That vertical depth is a moat when supply chains tighten and a burden when demand falls short.

One metric captures it: asset turnover must stay above 1.0 for this system to pay off. Once sales fall and utilisation drops, heavy assets turn into depreciation pressure. 2026H1 sales -15.7%: that risk has already surfaced, offset only by the overseas mix and R&D cuts.

Its valuation should therefore reflect a "heavy-asset manufacturer", not a "platform company" — precisely the divide between 20–25x and 35x.
