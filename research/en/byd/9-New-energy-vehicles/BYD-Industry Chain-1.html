Core view: it holds the most complete position, and is therefore the heaviest

The profit distribution across the new energy vehicle supply chain resembles an olive: both ends (resources, autonomous-driving chips, brands) earn fat margins, while the middle (materials, battery cells, vehicle manufacturing) earns on scale. BYD is the only company spanning the entire olive—from minority stakes in lithium mines to batteries, motors, electronic controls and chips, and on to complete vehicles and overseas plants.

The advantage of this position is cost control and supply security; the drawback is that cyclical swings at every link flow straight into its financials. 2026H1 is a case in point: losses from the domestic vehicle price war were offset by overseas markets; lithium price volatility was absorbed by self-supply, but FX losses then consumed RMB 8.3 billion of profit.

Upstream: it can squeeze materials suppliers, but not mines or chipmakers

Batteries account for 30%–40% of vehicle cost, the single largest block. BYD's strategy is "make in-house whatever we can": partial self-supply of cathodes, partial self-supply of anodes, equity stakes in electrolyte and lithium mining.This gives it a buffer when material prices rise, but also saddles it with heavy assets.

Two areas resist pressure. First, mines — when lithium prices rose from RMB 50,000/tonne to RMB 600,000/tonne in 2022, BYD could only accept it; its equity stakes in mines are too small to change pricing. Second, intelligent-driving chips — Nvidia and Horizon Robotics earn 50%–70% gross margins, and BYD is the buyer with no bargaining power.The focus of competition in 2026 is shifting from batteries to intelligent driving, and this vulnerability will only become more pronounced.

Midstream: in-house cell supply is the source of all its cost advantages

FinDreams Battery's self-supply rate exceeds 80%, and this is the most fundamental difference between BYD and every other automaker. Other automakers must buy batteries from CATL, and the gross margin is skimmed 20%–24% by the upstream supplier; BYD makes its own, so that profit stays in-house. 2026H1 automotive gross margin was 23.0% (Q2), higher than the 18.85% group gross margin you see, precisely because cell profit is internalized.

The cost is capex. A battery plant requires RMB 10 billion-level investment, and when capacity utilization is insufficient, depreciation eats into profit. 2026H1 sales volume fell 15.7%, capacity utilization declined, and this pressure will show up first in gross margin, only masked by the overseas mix dividend.

Downstream: price wars at home, premium pricing overseas — the two legs are uneven

The downstream splits into two markets with completely different logic. China is a fight for share in a stagnant pool: the industry grew 7.3%, BYD fell 15.7%, and the price war has cut net profit per vehicle to just RMB 0.72 million (and that is the improved figure). Selling one car in China earns RMB 7,200, among the lowest of any automaker globally.

Overseas is a growth market: exports of 79.2 million vehicles, up 67.8% year on year; overseas automotive revenue of RMB 129.5 billion, up 55.8% year on year, with markedly higher gross margins. The same product, sold somewhere else, earns a good deal more — that is the value of distribution and scarce supply.

But this premium has a shelf life. Once plants in Thailand, Brazil, Hungary and Uzbekistan come online, local supply will rise fast, and the price war will follow the industry abroad. BYD is itself the one adding that supply, and it knows this full well.

Cycle position: the penetration-rate dividend is over; the overseas expansion window remains open

The 2026 position can be summed up in one sentence: China is clearing out, overseas is expanding, and the two timelines are three years apart.

China: penetration has passed 50%, industry growth has fallen to 7.3%, capacity is oversupplied, and the price war that began in 2023 has not stopped. Following the path of the home appliance industry from 2005 to 2015, consolidation needs another 3–5 years, during which leaders' margins will be compressed to 3%–5%.

Overseas: Chinese automakers have roughly 3–4 years of overseas expansion window left. After that, local plants come online, local competitors wake up, and tariffs and compliance thresholds rise, narrowing the premium. BYD's four plants are racing to seize time within this window—win the race and they become long-term assets; lose it and they become sunk costs.

Investment implication: profit growth over the next three years will come mainly from overseas, and in China, stopping the decline is victory. So the value of the signal "monthly domestic sales turn positive" lies not in how much profit it brings, but in proving that "the bleeding has stopped."

Its position in the chain: comprehensive, but asset-heavy

Placed in the chain, BYD is the only company involved in everything "from mine to car": stakes in lithium mines, in-house materials, in-house cells, in-house chips, in-house vehicles, its own fleet, its own charging network.That vertical depth is a moat when supply chains tighten and a burden when demand is insufficient.

One metric captures it: its asset turnover must stay above 1.0 for this system to pay off. Once sales fall and capacity utilization drops, heavy assets become depreciation pressure. 2026H1 sales -15.7%: that risk has already surfaced, only offset by the overseas mix and R&D cuts.

So its valuation should not be that of a "platform company" but of a "heavy-asset manufacturer" — precisely the divide between 20–25x and 35x.

Conclusion

Industry-chain conclusion: BYD sits on the longest segment of the entire chain — the source of its cost advantage and also its cyclical exposure. It cannot squeeze miners or chipmakers upstream; in-house cell supply midstream is a genuine moat; downstream it is bleeding in China and earning a premium overseas. The cycle sits mid-way through "domestic shakeout, overseas expansion," with the two timelines three years apart. Investment implication: the return comes from "overseas mix + domestic stabilization," not from "industry-chain profit redistribution."
