Industry Research (Munger's Lens) — BYD

Once penetration passes 50%, what does this business become?

Core view

China's new-energy vehicle industry has passed its most comfortable phase. After penetration exceeded 50%, growth fell from double digits to 7.3%, and the industry shifted from "competing for incremental volume" to "competing for existing share," while the intensity of the price war rose instead. This is the common script for every durable consumer good once it completes its penetration cycle.

What penetration above 50% means: from "replacing fuel vehicles" to "replacing each other"

From 2020 to 2024, NEV makers did not need to steal customers from rivals; they only had to wait for ICE vehicle owners to switch—this was the "penetration dividend," with industry growth above 30%, and everyone could survive. By 2026, penetration exceeds 50% and the dividend ends: every incremental NEV sale must be taken from another NEV maker.

That is why the industry grew 7.3% while BYD fell 15.7%. The industry is not failing; the nature of competition has changed. Munger would call this "the flip side of economies of scale"—once everyone has built out capacity, scale is no longer a moat but a fixed-cost burden.

The economics of price wars: why they can't stop

The price war will not stop, because whoever stops first immediately loses share. Marginal cost in the vehicle industry sits far below average cost—the incremental cost of building one more car is only materials and labor, while production-line depreciation is already sunk. So as long as capacity utilization is below 100%, every player has an incentive to cut prices and fill capacity.

In 2026H1, BYD's response was "protect profit, not share": gross margin rose 0.8 points, volume fell 15.7%. That is a rational choice, but its cost is share, and share is the only long-term moat in this industry. Munger would ask: if you have to choose once a year between "protect profit" and "protect share," what makes you confident it gets the choice right every time?

Reference: the same path China's home-appliance industry once walked

China's home appliance industry from 2005 to 2015 ran almost the same script as China's new energy vehicles today: penetration crossed 50%, price wars erupted, several hundred brands consolidated to a dozen or so, and the leaders survived on scale and overseas expansion. What was the outcome? Gree and Midea held net margins at 8%–10% over the long run, and their market cap growth came from profit growth, not valuation expansion; meanwhile, the "growth stock" valuations of the day were cut from 40x all the way down to 10x.

BYD will most likely become the "Midea of autos": first in share, mid-range margins, growth sustained by overseas expansion, and a lower valuation center. That is not a bad outcome — Midea's annualized return over the past fifteen years was hardly poor — but it means the entry price must be low enough to make money from profit growth rather than from another round of valuation reflation.

Going global is the only source of incremental growth and the only area still commanding a premium

The domestic battle has run out of steam; growth is overseas. In 2026H1, exports reached 792,000 vehicles, up 67.8% YoY, and overseas automotive revenue hit RMB 129.5 billion, up 55.8% YoY, with gross margins clearly higher than domestic. This is not "selling a few more cars" — it is "selling the same cars somewhere else and earning a bigger slice."

But the overseas window will not stay open forever. Thailand already hosts Chinese automakers' factories, Brazil and Indonesia are queuing up, and Europe is setting limits through tariffs and localisation thresholds. BYD's response is to build plants — 150,000 units in Thailand, 150,000 in Brazil (300,000 planned), 150,000 in Hungary, 300,000 in Uzbekistan. The path is right, but it takes time and capital, and it is tight on both.

Cycle positioning: the industry is in the middle phase of "penetration dividend over, overcapacity beginning, overseas window still open." Leaders can survive this phase, but valuations will be systematically marked down — from growth stock to cyclical manufacturer.

Conclusion

Sector rating: C+. The business remains; the dividend is gone. Once penetration passes 50%, the sector enters competition for existing demand. Price wars are structural, and overseas expansion is the only source of growth, with a limited window. BYD is the strongest competitor in this landscape, but "strongest competitor" does not mean "most profitable" — and that is what confines it to a manufacturing valuation.
