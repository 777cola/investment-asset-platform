Business analysis (Duan Yongping's perspective) — BYD

Who pays, why they pay, and whether the paying lasts

Core view

In one sentence: BYD runs the entire chain from lithium, cathode materials, cells, motors, electronic controls and chips through to the finished vehicle, building its own cars from its own parts; vertical integration pushes per-vehicle cost to the lowest in the world, and overseas plants then sell those cars where they command a premium. It earns manufacturing money: unit economics can be calculated, and the ceiling is visible.

Who pays: price-sensitive households at home; overseas markets willing to pay a premium

Customers fall into two groups, and they pay for entirely different reasons. Domestic buyers are purchasing "the highest spec at this price point": at RMB 80,000 to 200,000, BYD offers the Blade Battery, DM-i hybrid and L2 driver assistance, and value for money is the only reason to buy — there is no brand loyalty. Overseas buyers are purchasing "the Chinese EV you can actually get": in many markets BYD is the only brand with cars in stock, after-sales service and a local plant, so the premium reflects scarce supply rather than brand strength.

The durability of these two customer bases differs sharply. Domestic customers will walk next door to Geely or Leapmotor at any time, and 2026H1 is the proof: total sales -15.7% against an industry +7.3%. Overseas customers currently have no alternative, but that lack of choice will disappear as competitors build plants. Thailand already hosts Changan, Great Wall and Neta factories, and more are queuing up in Brazil.

Why pay: cost advantage is quantifiable, brand premium is not

The cost advantage is quantifiable: more than 80% of battery supply is in-house, IGBT and SiC are self-supplied, tooling is produced internally, and per-vehicle cost runs 10%–15% below rivals. In 2026H1 revenue fell 7.1% while gross margin rose 0.8pp — the direct monetisation of that cost advantage. Second-quarter volume recovered from 70 to 111 (in 10,000 units) and per-vehicle net profit jumped from 0.58 to 0.72 (in 10,000 yuan): the scale effect, too, can be calculated.

What cannot be calculated is the brand. Denza plus Yangwang account for only 12.8% of volume, and the above-30 (in 10,000 yuan) segment is one it has never held. That gap condemns it to fight forever in the most crowded price band, re-proving each year that "I am cheaper than the guy next door and better than him too." Duan Yongping's test is "will users pay more for this brand" — BYD's answer is no.

How long it pays: passing one and a half of three criteria

First, does demand disappear? No. Electrification is a certainty and global penetration is still climbing — full marks here. Second, are customers taken away? Yes. They are already being taken domestically and will be overseas in two to three years — this one fails. Third, can prices be raised? No. Raising prices means losing volume — this one fails too.

One and a half out of three — a business with scale but no pricing power. The fair valuation for this kind of business sits above the manufacturing midpoint, at 20–25x, not the 30x-plus of a growth stock. RMB 87.4 corresponds to 27x TTM — too high.

Will this business still exist in ten years? Yes, but it will not look the way it does today

A decade from now China will most likely still be the world's largest auto market, and BYD will most likely still be in the top five. The question is not whether it stays in the top five, but what form it takes. Three possibilities. First, it becomes "the Toyota of autos", with a 10%–15% share and an 8% net margin — a good company whose market cap and profit grow in tandem. Second, it becomes "the Foxconn of autos", contract-manufacturing for others and earning only manufacturing-cost returns, in which case the valuation should de-rate to 10x. Third, it falls behind in intelligence and retreats to the sub-RMB 100,000 market, which is a worse business.

Duan Yongping would not try to guess which one it is; he would ask "which has the highest probability, and whether the price reflects it". The current 27x TTM reflects the first (successful Toyota-ization), while the 2026H1 data look more like the second. That is why he is not buying — not because he is bearish, but because the price has already priced in the best outcome.

Conclusion

Business rating: B. Understandable, calculable, cost leader—but no pricing power, heavy capex, losing share. Duan Yongping would say, "I respect this company, but I won't buy at this price." Entry conditions: domestic volume turns positive + valuation back below 23x. Both required.
