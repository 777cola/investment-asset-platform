Financial analysis (Buffett's perspective) — BYD

Does profit convert to cash, must all earnings be reinvested, and does ROE depend on leverage?

Core view

The answers to the three questions: profit is cash (operating cash flow is 3x net profit), but all the money earned must be reinvested (five-year free cash flow is negative), and ROE does not rely on leverage (low interest-bearing debt ratio). This is a highly efficient machine, and also a machine that devours cash.

Is profit cash: yes, but check where the cash comes from

Operating cash flow was RMB 37.3 billion, 3x net profit — a healthy ratio for a manufacturer. But the company's own explanation is "lower cash paid for goods purchased" — in other words, payment terms to upstream suppliers are stretching. This is the other side of vertical integration: as the largest buyer, you can extend payment cycles and shift working-capital pressure onto suppliers.

That erodes the quality of this cash flow. It is not sustainable — payable days have a limit, and so does supplier patience. Truly healthy cash flow should come from selling more and collecting faster, not from paying slower. 2026H1 volumes were -15.7%, so little of this cash flow came from selling well.

Should all earnings be reinvested? Yes — and that is the biggest question

Over the past five years BYD has ploughed essentially all its earnings back into production lines: more than a dozen domestic vehicle assembly bases, capacity expansion at FinDreams Battery, four overseas plants in Thailand, Brazil, Hungary and Uzbekistan, and eight ro-ro ships. Cumulative free cash flow over five years was negative, meaning shareholders received nothing and effectively subsidised the capital.

That is not a management failing but the structure of the industry. Autos is a scale business: without capacity expansion you lose share, losing share erodes cost advantage, and losing cost advantage puts you out of the game. So its capex is not a "choice" but a "survival" imperative. This is exactly the kind of business Buffett dislikes most — he prefers companies like See's Candies that "grow without putting money in."

The implication for valuation is direct: you cannot value it on discounted free cash flow, because free cash flow is negative over the long term; you can only use P/E, and P/E presupposes that earnings are sustainable. That loops the question back to "can it defend its share."

ROE is not leverage-driven, but nor is it margin-driven

A ten-year average ROE of 13%–15% breaks down into a net margin of 3.58% (low), asset turnover of around 1.0x (high) and an equity multiplier of around 3x (mid). It earns money through turnover, not margin, and not leverage. That is a healthy structure in manufacturing: it shows assets are used intensively, not that scale is propped up by borrowing.

Turnover's enemy, however, is overcapacity. Once industry capacity utilisation slides, turnover falls and ROE falls with it — faster than net margin does. In 2026H1 net margin dropped from 4.18% to 3.58%, and ROE has most likely already fallen from double digits to single digits. That is the next number to watch.

Conclusion

Financial rating: B+. The cash is real, the efficiency is real, and so is the cash burn. Two of the three tests pass: earnings are cash-backed rather than leverage-driven. The third fails — every dollar earned must be reinvested simply to hold position. A business like this can only be valued on P/E, and 27x is too rich for a company with a 3.58% net margin and negative free cash flow.
