## Wang Chuanfu: 9 out of 10 on technology judgment, 6 out of 10 on capital discipline

His judgment is well documented. In 2003, when everyone thought a battery maker building cars was madness, he bought Qinchuan Automobile. In 2020, the Blade Battery turned safety into a selling point using lithium iron phosphate, sidestepping the cobalt and nickel costs of NCM chemistry. In 2021, DM-i broke through the RMB 100,000 price band with "electric cheaper than oil." In 2024, flash charging cut charging time to single-digit minutes. Four calls, all four on industry inflection points — that is not luck.

Capital discipline is weaker. Five-year capex far exceeds operating cash flow, free cash flow is negative, and four overseas plants plus eight ro-ro ships are under construction simultaneously. The direction is right, but the pace is aggressive: with domestic sales down 15.7% in 2026H1, capacity is still expanding outward, and fixed-cost pressure will persist for two years.

Most alarming is the move in 2026H1: R&D expense cut 21.2%, RMB 6.3 billion less spent, while net profit fell only 20.5%. Trading a quarter of R&D for a year of reported earnings is a CFO's logic, not a founder's. Management is under pressure on near-term profit, and this time "protect profit" has overridden "protect the future."

## Shareholding structure: founder-controlled, though not an absolute majority

Wang Chuanfu holds about 18%, Lu Xiangyang about 5.5% and Xia Zuoquan about 3% — together less than 27%. With a dual A+H listing and a substantial H-share float, the institutional base is dispersed. The upside of this structure is that no single dominant shareholder can force through reckless decisions; the downside is that no external force can challenge management.

The past decade brought no control battle and no related-party transaction scandal. The lithium mine dealings between Rongjie Group and BYD drew scrutiny, but the amounts were small relative to revenue and disclosure was full. Audit opinions have been standard unqualified every year. Judged by whether shareholder money has been stolen, BYD is clean.

## Integrity and governance: two blemishes, neither fatal

In May 2023, Great Wall Motor publicly alleged that two of BYD's plug-in hybrid models used unpressurised fuel tanks and were suspected of failing emissions standards. The substance was a competitor using regulatory means to attack a rival, but it also exposed how BYD handles compliance boundaries while pushing cost to the extreme. No penalty followed, but the brand was damaged.

In late 2024, during construction of the Brazil plant, local prosecutors identified labour-condition problems on the part of a contractor; the company terminated cooperation with that contractor and rectified the issues. This is a textbook case of management reach failing to keep pace with expansion speed in the overseas push. A single instance can be blamed on the contractor; repeated instances point to a systemic problem.

Neither matter involves financial authenticity or appropriation of controlling-shareholder funds. The rating deducts 1–2 points; it does not constitute a veto.

## Employee stock ownership aligns management with the share price, but it also ties them to short-term profit

In 2022 the company ran an A-share buyback to fund an employee stock ownership plan for core technical and management staff, with performance metrics typically tied to net profit or sales volume. The design aligns interests on the upside; on the downside, it encourages "manufacturing the profit."

In 2026H1 R&D expense fell 21.2% while net profit fell only 20.5% — hard to argue this is entirely unrelated to assessment pressure. A company with a genuine long-term mindset would raise R&D when share is slipping, not cut it, because that is the only way to win share back. BYD chose another path: protect profit, protect the stock price, protect the assessment.

This is not a moral issue; the incentive structure determines it. If the metric is annual net profit, cutting R&D is "correctly completing the KPI"; if the metric is three-year share, the actions would look entirely different. How a company spends money is more reliable than what it says — in the first half of 2026, it chose to save.

## Succession: the biggest gap

Wang Chuanfu was born in 1966 and turns 60 in 2026, with no publicly disclosed succession plan. There is no obvious "number two" in management: Li Ke runs overseas, and the Fudi units (battery, powertrain, technology) each have their own heads, but strategic decisions are highly concentrated in the founder alone. Product cadence, technology roadmap and capacity-expansion decisions all rest on one person's judgment.

Global automakers such as Toyota and Volkswagen rely on systems, not individuals. BYD has not yet reached that stage. This is not a reason to sell today, but it is one reason the stock cannot command too high a valuation — a company dependent on a single decision-maker has weaker error tolerance than a systematised company.
