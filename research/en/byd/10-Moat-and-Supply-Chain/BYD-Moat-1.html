Core call: it can defend costs, but not customers

Scoring the moat across six components gives a weighted 6.5/10. Vertical integration ranks highest at 9 and scale at 8; switching costs are lowest at 3 — consumers pay nothing to switch cars, a weakness shared by every automaker, and BYD is no exception.

2026H1 bears this out: gross margin rose 0.84 points against the trend, showing the cost moat is working, while total volume fell 15.7% against an industry up 7.3%, showing the customer moat is not. The most dangerous rival is not Tesla but Geely, which competes in the same price band with an upgraded version of the same playbook.

Position in the value chain: it performs the supplier's work and absorbs the supplier's risk

A conventional automaker's supply chain runs on "I design, you build": batteries from CATL, electronic controls from Bosch, chips from Infineon. BYD has internalised that relationship — FinDreams Battery, FinDreams Powertrain and BYD Semiconductor are all in-house. The payoff is material costs 10%–15% below peers and a higher grade of supply security.

The cost is that BYD bears capacity and inventory risk at every link itself. With sales down 15.7% in 2026H1, other automakers can slash battery orders; BYD cannot — its battery plants are its own, and cutting output means depreciation it cannot absorb. Vertical integration is an accelerator when demand is strong and a drag chute when demand is weak.

Supply-chain risk: three links where it calls no shots

The first is lithium. Its equity stakes in mines give it only limited volume, so when lithium prices surge it has little choice but to accept them — the lesson of 2022 still stands. It is nonetheless better off than its rivals: at least part of its supply is self-sourced, giving it a flatter cost curve than automakers that buy purely on the market.

The second is smart-driving chips. Nvidia's Orin/Thor and Horizon Robotics' Journey are all bought externally, with gross margins of 50%–70%, and BYD is a pure buyer. As competition shifts to smart driving in 2026, this vulnerability will widen.

The third is shipping. It owns eight ro-ro vessels, better than automakers that charter ships, but when global ro-ro capacity is tight, berth slots still queue. Of the 792,000 vehicles exported, how many were lost sales because capacity could not keep up, the company has not said.

Moat by item: the two that run deep are precisely not barriers

Vertical integration scores 9 and scale scores 8 — real strengths, but they defend against a cost war, not a share war. A cost advantage lets BYD outlast rivals in a price war; it cannot stop customers from walking into the shop next door. H1 2026 is a case in point: gross margin rose while volume fell 15.7%.

Brand scores 5 and intelligent driving scores 5 — the two factors that determine share, and BYD is strong in neither. Denza plus Yangwang account for only 12.8% of volume, showing the company cannot crack the above-RMB 300,000 market. Its intelligent-driving data pool is ample (3.33 million vehicles, 210 million km added daily), yet the experience is not top-tier, pointing to gaps in algorithms and engineering.

Switching cost scores 3 — a common failing of the auto industry, not BYD's alone. Consumers who switch cars lose only residual value, and products at the same price reach parity within six months. The industry has never had customer lock-in, only "this generation is better" — which is why automakers must launch new models every year. No launch, no share.

The moat's boundaries: strongest below RMB 150,000, weakest above RMB 200,000

Split the market by price band and the moat's width changes completely. Below RMB 100,000: strongest — the cost advantage translates into a RMB 5,000 price gap, and buyers at this price point are the most price-sensitive, so rivals cannot follow. RMB 100,000–200,000: strong but caught up — Geely's Galaxy and Leapmotor's C series both field comparable products; the contest here is pace and channels, not cost. Above RMB 200,000: weakest — here the contest is smart driving, cabin and brand, and Denza plus Yangwang account for only 12.8% of sales.

This structure explains why it must move up-brand, and why moving up is so hard. Below RMB 100,000 it sets the rules; above RMB 200,000 it is just one option among many. And the most profitable segment is exactly the one where it is weakest.

The most dangerous rival: Geely, not Tesla

Most people's first reaction is Tesla or Xiaomi. Wrong. Tesla sits above RMB 200,000 and Xiaomi at RMB 200,000–300,000; both are fighting for the market BYD wants to move up into, not BYD's core base.

Geely is the one. Three reasons: first, the same price band (RMB 80,000–200,000); second, an upgraded version of the same playbook (multi-brand + group procurement + rapid iteration); third, in 2026H1, with the industry at +7.3% and BYD at -15.7%, Geely's NEV sales still grew 10%. Share is flowing from BYD to Geely, not to Tesla.

Geely also has an advantage BYD lacks: it does not carry a battery plant's depreciation. It can buy from CATL, from its own battery plant, or from SVOLT — whichever is cheaper. In a year of falling demand, that flexibility is worth more than vertical integration.

The second threat is BYD itself: cutting R&D spend 21.2% to buy profit. If the product cycle fails to keep up in 2027, the price will be paid double — this is not external competition, it is internal overdrawing.

Conclusion

Moat conclusion: cost moat 9 points, customer moat 3 points, weighted 6.5/10. That defends "we won't lose our shirt," not "customers won't leave." The most dangerous rival is Geely — same price band, same playbook, still growing. What really decides BYD's fate is not Tesla's technology but the price war in the RMB 150,000 band that no one can stop. Investment implication: the moat supports "won't collapse," not "deserves a premium."
