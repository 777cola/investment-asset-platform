# BYD（002594）— Duan Yongping Supplement

- Research date：2026/09/04
- Price：87.4 CNY
- Market cap：RMB 796.8 billion market cap (91.17 billion total shares, A+H)

## Notes

This report re-examines BYD through the lens of Duan Yongping's public investment framework: business essence, circle of competence, benfen and the stop-doing list. The Q&A below reflects our own reasoning, not his stated views. Data: 2026 interim report (revenue RMB 344.82 billion, net profit RMB 12.33 billion, gross margin 18.85%, R&D expense RMB 23.3 billion, net financial expense RMB 5.1 billion, overseas auto revenue RMB 129.5 billion); 2025 net profit RMB 32.619 billion; valuation based on 2027 neutral net profit of RMB 34 billion and 9.117 billion shares.

## Q1: Is this a good business?

Not good. I have always found the car business a grind: asset-heavy, strongly cyclical, new production lines every year, and retooling for every model change.The world's best carmakers run net margins of just 8%; Toyota, the global leader, is no different. This is not the Moutai business, and it is not the Apple business.

But BYD has taken this difficult business to its limit, and I give it that. It makes its own batteries, motors, electronic controls, even its own chips, so its costs run well below rivals'.Revenue fell 7% in the first half of 2026 while gross margin rose 0.8 points — that is real capability, and it cannot be faked.

But you have to separate a "good company" from a "good business".A good company runs a difficult business better than others; a good business makes money while standing still. BYD is the former. The former's valuation ceiling is always lower than the latter's.

## Q2: Will it still be around in ten years?

BYD will still be there. China will remain the world's largest auto market, and BYD will most likely still rank in the top five. That is not the real concern.

But being there is not the same as being good. In ten years the company could look like one of three things: first, the Toyota of autos, with a 10%–15% share and an 8% net margin — a good company; second, the Foxconn of autos, contract manufacturing for others and earning hard-won money, in which case the valuation should de-rate to 10x; third, falling behind on intelligence and retreating to the sub-RMB 100,000 market.

I would not claim to know which. Where I cannot see clearly, I generally will not pay a high price. The current 27x assumes the first outcome is inevitable, and that assumption looks too generous to me.

## Question three: Do you understand it?

Half. Making cars, making batteries, selling cars — these I understand. Cost structure, gross margin, per-vehicle profit — I can work those out.

What I don't understand is competition in the RMB 150,000 price band. There is no moat there, only cost and pace: today your car has 50 km more range, tomorrow a rival catches up; today you cut RMB 20,000, tomorrow a rival cuts RMB 25,000. In that kind of business I cannot see "who wins in ten years," only "who is cheaper next year."

Where I don't understand, I don't bet big. That is not a question of ability, it is a question of discipline. Many people lose money not because they don't understand the business, but because they bet big where they don't.

## Question 4: At what price would you buy?

Just above RMB 70. The logic is simple: I use RMB 34 billion of net profit for 2027; at 23x that implies a market cap of RMB 780 billion, which divided by 9.117 billion shares gives roughly RMB 85; a 25% margin of safety brings that to RMB 65–70.

At the current RMB 87.4 and 27x, the base case offers only 3% upside. Buying here means paying for all the good news while still carrying the bad. I like to buy at a discount, not at a premium.

Some argue a 21% drop already makes it cheap. A 21% drop only moves it from "very expensive" to "expensive", not "cheap". And profit is falling too, while the valuation has barely changed — the most painful kind of decline, because your margin of safety has not increased.

What if domestic sales turn positive? Then I will redo the math. Positive sales growth means RMB 34 billion could become RMB 40 billion, and RMB 90 could become RMB 105, which would make 27x reasonable. So I am not just waiting for a price; I am waiting for a reason I can calculate clearly.

## Question five: What scares you most?

What I fear most is not a price war, nor tariffs. What I fear most is a company that does not know it is on the decline.

In the first half of 2026, R&D was cut 21%, saving RMB 6.3 billion. That cut was clever — profit immediately looks better, the statements immediately look pretty. But R&D is the source of all this company's advantages; cutting R&D is selling blood for cash. If management believes this is the right thing to do, that is the real trouble.

Another one: FX. In the first half, financial expenses swung from a gain of RMB 3.2 billion to a loss of RMB 5.1 billion — RMB 8.3 billion round trip. The more it sells overseas, the bigger this hole grows. No matter how much money is made this way, exchange rates can swallow it in one bite.

As for tariffs, I am actually less afraid. It has built plants in Thailand, Brazil, Hungary and Uzbekistan — the right way to handle tariffs. What I fear is that after the plants are built, locals also fight a price war in the same price band — then it no longer has the "overseas premium" card to play.

## Question 6: If you could only buy one Chinese automaker

That remains the one. Not because it is cheap, but because it is the only company standing firm on all four legs: profitability, scale, overseas expansion and in-house technology. Geely costs less, Li Auto has no overseas business, Leapmotor's gross margin is too thin, and Xiaomi's car unit is still loss-making.

But "only one company can be bought" is an assumption; in reality I can buy none of them. The money is in my own hands, and not buying is also a choice — often the better one.

So my answer is: this company deserves a place on the list and a monthly look at sales, but it does not deserve to be bought at RMB 87. I will reconsider when it falls to RMB 70, or when its domestic sales turn positive. If neither happens, so be it — there are plenty of good companies.
