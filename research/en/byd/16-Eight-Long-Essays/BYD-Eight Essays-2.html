② The business essence: one battery, four wheels, and an extremely long cost chain — BYD

Where the money comes from, how costs are calculated, whether unit economics hold up

Core view

BYD's business can be summed up in one sentence: use its own parts, build its own cars, sell them to the people most sensitive to price, then go overseas and sell them to people less sensitive to price. The first half is the cost advantage; the second half is the source of profit.

Cost structure: why it can be 10%–15% cheaper than others

In an EV's cost structure, the battery accounts for 30%–40%, the motor and electronic controls for 10%–15%, and the rest is body, interior and electronics. BYD makes the biggest piece itself — FinDreams Battery self-supply exceeds 80%; motors and electronic controls in-house — FinDreams Powertrain; power semiconductors in-house — BYD Semiconductor (IGBT/SiC); even tooling is made in-house.

This means the gross profit others pay to suppliers stays inside the company. CATL's cell gross margin is 20%–24%; BYD's self-supply effectively captures that 20%. This is the source of the "10%–15% lower per-vehicle cost."

But this advantage has a precondition: capacity utilization must stay high. Vertical integration means a high fixed-cost share; when output falls, unit cost rises. With 2026H1 sales -15.7%, this advantage is being eroded — merely masked by the overseas mix.

Source of profit: break-even at home, profit overseas

Splitting the two markets apart makes the picture clear. Domestic: revenue RMB 145.6 billion, down 33.6% YoY; the price war has cut net profit per vehicle to just a few thousand yuan, leaving the business essentially at breakeven. Overseas: revenue RMB 129.5 billion, up 55.8% YoY, with visibly higher gross margin — the real source of profit.

So the answer to "what is BYD actually earning" is: it earns the spread between "Chinese manufacturing cost + overseas market pricing." Not brand premium, not technology monopoly — a cost gap. The shelf life of that spread depends on when overseas becomes a second China market.

Given the ramp schedule of four plants (Thailand already in production, Brazil in July 2025, Hungary in 2026, Uzbekistan), overseas supply will be released in concentrated fashion in 2027–2028, and the price war will most likely follow exports abroad. That leaves the spread at most 3–4 more years.

The three numbers of this business

If you remember only three numbers, remember these: net margin 3.58% (thin, an industry trait), net profit per vehicle RMB 0.72 million (improving, but a broker estimate), capex above 10% of revenue (heavy; the money earned must be reinvested).

Together the three numbers say this: it is a business that "earns on scale, survives on turnover, and lifts margins by going overseas." Scale is the lifeline — if sales fall, all three numbers deteriorate at once. In 2026H1 sales fell 15.7%, and the lifeline took a hit.

Look one level deeper: who the customers are, and where repeat purchases come from

Autos are a low-frequency purchase: a car lasts 6–8 years, so repeat buying is not the main source of this business—the size of the first-time buyer cohort is. In 2026, China's first-time buyer cohort is shrinking (demographics plus penetration above 50%), while the trade-up cohort is expanding, and trade-up buyers care more about brand and smart driving—precisely BYD's two weakest attributes.

Overseas the logic differs: many markets in Southeast Asia, Latin America and the Middle East are electrifying for the first time, so the first-time buyer cohort is expanding and is insensitive to brand but sensitive to price.That is why it cannot gain traction at home but can abroad—the product has not changed; the customer stage has. Grasp this and you grasp the underlying reason behind "the overseas window still has 3–4 years."

Put those three numbers into one sentence:it earns RMB 7,200 per vehicle sold, roughly half from overseas and half from China. If the overseas share keeps rising, per-vehicle profit can rise further; if China keeps falling, per-vehicle profit will be dragged back down.

In other words,what it sells is not cars but lower unit cost at the same specification—the car is merely the carrier.

Conclusion

Business essence conclusion: A cost-driven manufacturer earning the spread between "manufacturing cost + overseas pricing," with a net margin of 3.58%, heavy capex, and scale as its lifeline. The reasonable valuation for this kind of business is 20–25x, and the reasonable expectation is "growing with profit," not "valuation doubling."
