Core thesis

We put six companies in the AI compute chain into a single comparison table: Nvidia, AMD, Broadcom, TSMC, Marvell and Intel. The rough screen applies three hard metrics — growth ≥30%, gross margin ≥45%, ROE ≥15%. Nvidia, TSMC and Broadcom pass; AMD fails on ROE, Marvell on gross margin, and Intel on all three.

The final pick comes down to Nvidia versus TSMC. Nvidia wins on ecosystem and systems; TSMC wins on irreplaceability and valuation. AMD is eliminated for one reason only: its share price has already discounted "becoming Nvidia", and it cannot become Nvidia. That is not a problem with the company; it is a problem with the price.

Initial screen: three hard metrics cut the list by half

Screen one, growth ≥30%: Nvidia +106%, AMD +50%, Broadcom +70%, TSMC +35% and Marvell +40% pass; Intel's single-digit growth fails. Screen two, gross margin ≥45%: Intel at 35% fails; all others pass. Screen three, ROE ≥15%: AMD at 10%, Marvell at 10% and Intel negative — three fail.

Three screens leave Nvidia, TSMC and Broadcom. No surprise here — profit in the AI compute chain concentrates in these three: one defines the ecosystem, one defines manufacturing, one defines custom silicon.

AMD falls down on ROE because its 2022 Xilinx acquisition was paid in stock, inflating net assets to RMB 63 billion while profit has only just recovered. Not fatal, but its role in the screen is to remind you that capital efficiency has yet to reach top-tier.

Final pick: Nvidia vs TSMC, and why AMD is not the third option

NVIDIA's strength is an "irreplaceable ecosystem plus system-level delivery". Its weaknesses are price — 28x TTM is not expensive, but a USD 5 trillion market cap demands it beat expectations every year — and off-balance-sheet commitments of USD 530.5 billion. TSMC's strength is "irreplaceable manufacturing plus pricing power"; its weaknesses are geopolitics (all capacity in Taiwan) and capex intensity.

Why does AMD fail to make the final cut? Three reasons. First, its growth depends on a rival's capacity, which it does not control. Second, its gross-margin ceiling is capped near 60%, because it sells components, not systems. Third, and most important — the current price already discounts 70% of the "doubling by 2027" scenario; buying it means buying "above consensus", and the probability of exceeding consensus is no more than 30%.

Forced to pick one "challenger" between AMD and Broadcom, this report picks Broadcom: its custom ASIC business carries a 75% gross margin, its customers are Google, Meta and ByteDance, order visibility extends three years, and it trades at 18x FY27. AMD's story is sexier; Broadcom's business is sturdier.

Why it matters: the report's actual conclusion

Strictly speaking, this is not a "recommendation on AMD" but an "assessment of AMD." The conclusion: it is the most elastic name in this chain, and also the most expensive.

If the investor's goal is "exposure to AI compute," the optimal solution is not AMD but TSMC (manufacturing monopoly + 28x) or Nvidia (ecosystem monopoly + 28x).AMD suits investors who "believe the second supplier's share will rise from 9% to 20%" — a judgment that must be verified each quarter against earnings.

So the funnel's conclusion: AMD stays on the watchlist, not the buy list. Wait for its price to fall to a level where "even if share only reaches 15%, you don't lose" — below USD 350.

Marvell deserves a separate mention. It is the second supplier of custom ASICs (after Broadcom), with Amazon and Microsoft as customers, 40% growth and 60% gross margin — the business quality is not bad. It was eliminated because ROE is only 10% — again, acquisitions inflated equity.This is the same disease as AMD: good business + poor capital structure.The difference between the two is valuation: Marvell at 35x, AMD at 125x.

Why can't TSMC be left out? Because it is the only foundry that can achieve 70% yield on N2, and both AMD and Nvidia have to queue up. Buying TSMC is buying "the water seller's water seller," at a valuation of 28x — more than four times cheaper than AMD.Same industry trend, two completely different risk-reward structures.

Intel is the cautionary tale in this table. It has three things AMD lacks: its own fabs, the original x86 license, and the backing of a US government stake. But its gross margin is only 35%, because the foundry business is losing money and 18A yields have not ramped.Owning manufacturing is not necessarily an advantage — when the process lags, it is a burden.AMD sold off manufacturing fifteen years ago; today that looks right.

Conclusion

Funnel conclusion: six names screened to three; final pick TSMC and Nvidia, AMD out on price. This is not a rejection of the company — AMD's execution ranks second among the six — it is a rejection of paying 125x for a second supplier. For the same money, TSMC buys you a monopoly at 28x; AMD buys you a challenger at 125x.
