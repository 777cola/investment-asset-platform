Core view

AMD's business can be summed up in one sentence: sell chips made by TSMC to customers who need a second option besides Nvidia. Every word in that sentence matters — TSMC sets the ceiling on capacity, customers set the ceiling on share, and "second option" sets the ceiling on gross margin.

Unit economics: how a single card makes money

Take MI450 as an example. On a rough per-card economics basis, the selling price is around USD 30,000 against a bill of materials of about USD 16,000–18,000 — HBM4 432GB at USD 6,000–8,000, the GPU die at about USD 5,000, packaging and substrate at USD 3,000 and the remainder at USD 2,000–3,000 — for gross profit of about USD 12,000–14,000 and a gross margin of 40%–45%. That is below the company-wide 56%, which is lifted by high-margin server CPUs.

The server CPU economics are better still: EPYC sells for USD 8,000–15,000 per unit on a bill of materials of USD 3,000–4,000, a 60%–70% gross margin. This is AMD's real cash cow. It does not need to compete with Nvidia, only with Intel, and Intel's Xeon is already a generation behind on energy efficiency.

AMD's profit structure therefore stacks up as follows: CPUs supply stable, high-margin cash flow, GPUs supply growth at lower margins, and FPGAs supply steady industrial orders. Of the three legs, the most profitable grows the slowest, and the fastest-growing is the least profitable.

Why clients buy: three reasons, one common thread

Twenty percent cheaper, half the lead time, a second-source policy. What these three arguments share is that none of them has anything to do with AMD's product itself — all three hinge on "NVIDIA's situation." NVIDIA is expensive, so AMD's cheapness matters; NVIDIA's lead times are long, so AMD's speed matters; NVIDIA is dominant, so a second source is necessary.

AMD's demand is therefore derived demand — set by the degree of NVIDIA's supply tightness, not by its own technology.If NVIDIA's capacity is tight in 2026, AMD thrives; if Rubin capacity is filled in 2027, AMD fades. This is not speculation — it is the story of MI300 in 2024: that year NVIDIA's lead time shrank from 40 weeks to 20 weeks, and AMD's data center growth immediately fell from 80% to 30%.
The ceiling of the business: three caps

Share ceiling: cloud providers' second-source policy caps share at 20%–30%, model companies at 40%, for a weighted 15%–25%.

Gross margin ceiling: 56% selling chips, 60% selling racks, 75% selling ecosystem. AMD sits in the first tier today; the move to the second hinges on Helios.

Valuation ceiling: a second source warrants 20–25x, a standard-setter 30–40x. AMD is currently priced as a third-tier player despite being positioned in the first tier. Of the three ceilings, valuation is the easiest to clear.

The order book shows whether demand is real. Oracle committed in October 2025 to buy 50,000 MI450 chips for OCI superclusters; the first 1GW of OpenAI's 6GW agreement delivers in H2 2026; Anthropic's 2GW splits into two phases; Microsoft Azure is moving from MI300X to Helios testing. Together these four cover more than 60% of the 2027 data center revenue target. The orders are real; the question is whether AMD can deliver on time.

Revenue recognition timing also needs to be clear. Racks are not booked on sale: delivery, racking, acceptance and benchmark validation take 3–6 months end to end. "MI450 revenue" in Q3 2026 therefore corresponds to orders placed in Q1 2026, and Q4 2026 revenue to Q2 orders. Reading the order book gives two quarters of revenue visibility — the only information edge AMD investors have.

The business model differs from Nvidia's in what is sold. Nvidia sells the full rack — GPU + NVLink switch + Spectrum networking + liquid cooling + software stack + three-year service — with an NVL72 quoted above USD 3 million. AMD sells GPUs and reference designs; OEMs assemble the racks, and software is free. For the same compute, AMD quotes 20%–30% less because it sells less networking, software and service — and that missing portion is exactly where the gross margin gap comes from.

The cash flow timing gap deserves separate mention. Revenue is recognized 3–6 months after rack delivery, while materials must be paid for 6–9 months in advance (both HBM and CoWoS are prepaid). The faster the growth, the tighter the cash flow. AMD's operating cash flow currently covers net income 1.3x because the CPU business collects cash quickly, not because the GPU business is healthy. If GPUs reach 70% of the mix in 2027, that coverage ratio falls below 0.9.

Conclusion

Business essence: derivative demand, second-supplier status and three ceilings. The business will neither disappear nor become highly profitable. Its best case is NVIDIA capacity staying tight while its own yields hold on target; its worst is NVIDIA cutting prices as cloud providers design chips in-house. Neither outcome is AMD's to control.
