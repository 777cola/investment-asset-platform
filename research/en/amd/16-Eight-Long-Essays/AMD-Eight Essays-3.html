Core view

A moat is not a phrase but five lines of defense: licenses, cost, technology, ecosystem, and customer relationships. AMD has genuine barriers in licenses and cost, is weak in ecosystem and brand, and its customer relationships are project-level rather than company-level. We dissect each one.

The two real ones: licences and costs

The x86 license is the hardest barrier in the semiconductor industry, bar none. When IBM's PC chose the 8086 in 1982, Intel licensed x86 to AMD as a second source, an arrangement that has held for forty years. Any company trying to enter this market today must either obtain a license (impossible) or wait for RISC-V to mature (a decade away).

Chiplet is a barrier AMD built itself. When Zen first shipped in 2017, AMD lacked the money for large dies on advanced nodes and could only split the CPU into four small dies stitched together on 14nm. It turned out this architecture cost 30% less than Intel's large dual-core dies, with far higher yields. A forced innovation became a long-term advantage—the finest move in AMD's history.

Together, these two explain how AMD went from 1% to over 30% in server CPUs: the license guarantees it can compete, and cost guarantees it can win.

The three false ones: ecosystem, brand, customer relationships

ROCm's problem is that it "works, but not well." Moving a model from CUDA to ROCm requires rewriting kernels, tuning, and re-validating accuracy—2–6 person-months of work. Worth it for large clusters, not for small customers. This dictates that AMD can only win big customers, not the long tail.

The brand problem is the phrase "cost-effective." It means "cheap," and it also means "a tier below." A procurement manager choosing AMD has to write a justification; choosing Nvidia, they don't—a layer of organizational friction AMD has spent a decade failing to eliminate.

The customer-relationship problem is that it operates "at the project level." AMD's joint development with Microsoft and OpenAI is real, but the relationship fades once the project ends. Nvidia's relationships with customers are "organizational"—resident CUDA teams, developer conferences, a certification system. Project-level relationships win orders; organizational-level relationships win pricing power.

The history of the x86 license is worth noting. In 1982, IBM chose the 8086 as its PC processor and required Intel to have a second source; Intel licensed it to AMD. That arrangement kept AMD alive for forty years and made x86 the world's only "duopoly instruction set." In 2009 the two reached a new cross-licensing agreement under which AMD pays an annual license fee in exchange for perpetual usage rights. This moat wasn't built by AMD—it was built by IBM—but it is a moat all the same.

The chiplet cost advantage can be explained with yield math. On a wafer, the larger the die area, the lower the yield (defects are distributed by area). Split a 600mm² die into four 150mm² dies: single-die yield rises from 60% to 90%, and the effective yield of the four combined is 0.9⁴ ≈ 66%, still above 60%, and the smaller dies can use mature processes and cheaper capacity. This is why AMD can pair an N6 IO die with an N3 compute die at a cost 30% lower than Intel's monolithic approach.

The ROCm gap can be quantified. CUDA's kernel libraries (cuBLAS, cuDNN, NCCL, TensorRT) cover 99% of mainstream models; ROCm covers about 85%, and the missing 15% is the latest attention variants and quantization kernels. The toolchain is trickier still: Nsight's performance analysis, Triton's compiler backend—ROCm has substitutes for all of them, but two generations behind in maturity. Between "runs" and "runs well" lie 15% of the kernels plus two generations of tooling.

"Weak customer switching capability" needs to be made concrete. Migrating a 1,000-GPU inference cluster from CUDA to ROCm requires rewriting about 5% of the kernels, re-tuning the communication libraries, and re-validating accuracy—roughly 20 person-years of work. For a Microsoft or Meta with AI infrastructure teams of several hundred people, that is bearable; for a 50-person AI startup, it is not. So ROCm can only win big customers, not the long tail—and the long tail is CUDA's real moat.

Conclusion

Anatomy conclusion: five lines of defense, two real and three fake. The two real ones (licenses, cost) keep AMD alive on the CPU side over the long run; the three fake ones (ecosystem, brand, customer relationships) mean it cannot command a premium on the GPU side. Investment implication: buying AMD means buying "CPU certainty," not "GPU imagination."
