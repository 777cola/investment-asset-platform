05 Industry and competition: second tier of the most profitable value chain — AMD

Core view

AI compute is, bar none, the most profitable business in the market today. Profit pools along the chain are, however, highly uneven: the standard-setter earns a 75% gross margin, the manufacturing monopolist 58%, the second supplier 56% and the assembler 6%. AMD sits in that second row, and the position defines its ceiling.

Three-tier structure of competition

The first layer is training, where Nvidia holds a monopoly. AMD has essentially no share: training places the heaviest demands on the ecosystem, and CUDA's operator libraries, NCCL's communication libraries and the Megatron parallel framework are all optimised around Nvidia. For AMD to enter this layer, ROCm would have to rebuild all of it from scratch.

The second layer is inference, a three-way battle. It is less demanding on the ecosystem but more demanding on cost per token. AMD's price-performance is competitive here, cloud providers' in-house ASICs cost even less, and Nvidia is cutting prices at scale. This is the only battlefield AMD can win.

The third layer is sovereign AI and HPC, where AMD has a unique advantage. It requires "no lock-in to US vendors" plus "can run scientific computing," and AMD's CPU + GPU combination (EPYC + Instinct) is one of the few solutions that can be delivered as a whole. Europe's JUPITER and the successor to Japan's Fugaku follow this logic. This layer is small in volume but high in gross margin and strong in stickiness.

2027 share projection

Base case: the AI accelerator market grows from USD 300 billion in 2026 to USD 420 billion in 2027 (+40%), AMD's share rises from 9% to 22%, and data center revenue reaches about USD 92 billion — the revenue base for EPS of USD 19, half again above the "double" figure (about USD 60 billion).

Bull case: the market grows 60% to USD 480 billion, AMD's share reaches 24%, revenue hits USD 115 billion and EPS is USD 24. This requires persistent capacity constraints at Nvidia plus Helios winning a 20% share of racks.

Bear case: the market grows 15% to USD 345 billion, AMD's share stalls at 16%, revenue is USD 55 billion and EPS is USD 12. This scenario does not require AMD to fail — only for the industry to normalize.

Who is most at risk

The near-term driver is Nvidia's price cuts; the long-term driver is cloud providers' in-house chips. Price cuts are about "grabbing profit," in-house chips about "grabbing share." The latter is more lethal: it is structural and will not reverse with Nvidia's capacity cycle.

Cloud in-house chips account for 22% in 2026 and possibly 30% in 2027. Of these 8 points, half comes from Nvidia (which can hold) and half from AMD (which cannot). AMD's share story is essentially competing with cloud providers' in-house teams for the same increment.

A rough sizing of the market: the global AI accelerator market is about USD 300 billion in 2026 (Nvidia 180 billion + AMD 27 billion + in-house 66 billion + others 27 billion) and, at 40% growth, 420 billion in 2027. AMD's share goes from 9% to 22%, corresponding to 92 billion—the revenue base for EPS of USD 19, 50% above the "annualized doubling" basis (about 60 billion). The key assumption here is a 13-point share gain, more aggressive than "doubling"—and this is where we have doubts about the company's target.

Mix across the three layers: training about 55%, inference about 40%, HPC and sovereign about 5%. AMD has almost no share in training (below 3%), about 12% in inference and about 25% in HPC. Its strength lies in the smallest layer, which is why its gross margin is high but total volume is small.

TSMC's CoWoS allocation is a hard constraint. 2026 CoWoS monthly capacity will be about 120,000 wafers, with Nvidia taking 70,000, AMD 25,000 and Broadcom and others 25,000. One CoWoS wafer yields about 4–6 AI accelerators, so AMD's 25,000 wafers correspond to monthly output of 100,000–150,000 units—its delivery ceiling and the physical boundary of "doubling."

The sovereign AI segment deserves elaboration. Europe's JUPITER (Jülich Research Centre) uses a combination of EPYC + Instinct, and Japan's Fugaku successor is also evaluating AMD. Such customers share three traits: multi-year budgets, no pursuit of the latest generation, and a requirement that the supply chain not be locked in by a single vendor. AMD's share in this layer is about 25%, the only sub-market in AI accelerators where it leads Nvidia. But this layer accounts for only 5% of the market and cannot change the big picture.

"Share" needs thorough explanation. Share does not rise linearly; it follows the "customers' open-source cost-cutting cycle": when Nvidia's capacity is tight, cloud providers raise the second-supplier share from 5% to 15%; when capacity is loose, they push it back to 8%. 2026 is exactly a tight period, so AMD's share appears to be rising, but in fact it is an "institutional quota" being temporarily enlarged. Treating the share at a cycle peak as a steady state is where such companies are most easily overvalued.

The cost account on the inference side can be quantified. Running the same 70-billion-parameter model, the MI450 rack's cost per million tokens is 20%–25% lower than Nvidia's solution, mainly from two points: a purchase price 20% lower, and larger per-card HBM capacity (432GB) that can fit longer contexts and require less inter-card communication. This advantage is amplified in long-context inference and offset by CUDA's scheduling efficiency in short requests. So AMD's sweet spot is "long context, high concurrency, cost-sensitive" inference scenarios—exactly the fastest-growing segment in 2026.

Conclusion

Industry view: within this three-layer structure AMD can win only the second and third layers; the first belongs to Nvidia, while the fourth (in-house silicon) is encroaching on its second. A share gain from 9% to around 20% is a reasonable projection (base case 22%); reaching 27% would require a rival misstep.
