06 Management: Lisa Su's twelve years — AMD

Core view

In October 2014, Su Lisa took over AMD: a USD 3 stock price, USD 2.3 billion in cash, three consecutive years of losses, and all seven product lines bleeding money. Twelve years later, revenue is annualized at USD 46 billion, data center revenue is USD 6.7 billion in a single quarter, and market cap is USD 802.4 billion. This record deserves its own piece.

Three key decisions

The first time, she cut the product line. In 2014–2015, she shut down mobile phone chips, tablet chips and low-end APUs entirely, keeping only three lines: CPU, GPU and semi-custom. Internal opposition was fierce, because mobile chips were a "growth market." Her reasoning: AMD lacked the resources to fight on four fronts at once.

The second time, she sold manufacturing. In 2016 she sold the packaging and testing plant to Tongfu Microelectronics, and in 2017 handed all wafer production to GlobalFoundries and TSMC. At the time this was read as "abandoning core competitiveness"; a decade later it looks like "shedding dead weight" — AMD did not have to pay for a USD 20 billion fab and could put all its money into R&D.

The third time, she bet on chiplets. When Zen debuted in 2017, it assembled server CPUs from four small dies, sidestepping the 14nm process disadvantage. What these three steps share: when resources are scarce, choose to do less but do it thoroughly.

Capital allocation: five acquisitions, four correct

Xilinx (RMB 49 billion, 2022): gained FPGA, adaptive computing, and military and communications customers. The deal shifted AMD's revenue mix from "highly cyclical" to "three-legged," and rightly so.

Pensando (RMB 1.9 billion, 2022): DPU and SmartNIC, filling out data-center networking. Small money, fills a gap, right call.

Silo AI (RMB 665 million, 2024): Finnish model team, adds software-adaptation capability. Small amount, large symbolic value, neutral.

ZT Systems (RMB 4.9 billion, 2025): rack-level system integration, the precondition for delivering Helios. This one is contentious — the scarcity of system integration is declining; Dell and Hon Hai can both do it. But for AMD, going from 0 to 1 requires it.

Four right, one neutral; not one of these deals was about "growing revenue" — all were about "adding capability."That is a high mark for capital allocation.
Governance and succession

Governance has two blemishes: the chairman and CEO roles are held by one person (since 2022), concentrating power; and the OpenAI warrant cap of 160 million shares (about 9.6% dilution) transfers part of shareholders' future returns to a single customer. Neither is fatal, but neither looks like "first-class governance."

Succession is the biggest single-point risk. Su is 57, the CTO is 66, and there is no public succession pipeline. The market's valuation of AMD embeds a "Su premium" that no financial statement supports—it exists only in investors' trust. If she retires, the valuation drops 20% first, and fundamentals get discussed later.

Su's résumé deserves laying out: PhD in electrical engineering from MIT; after graduation, device and process work at TI and IBM; at IBM she led the Cell processor (the PS3 chip); then CTO of Freescale; joined AMD as SVP in 2012; became CEO in October 2014; added the chairman role in 2022. She is a rare CEO in semiconductors who is both technically trained and skilled at capital allocation.

The details of what she took over in 2014: a $3 stock price, $2 billion market cap, $2.3 billion cash, $2 billion debt, three consecutive years of losses, 14,000 employees. Her first move was to cut 7% of staff and exit mobile chips; her second was to concentrate resources on Zen. Some on the board wanted to "sell the company"; she refused on the grounds that "the architecture still has value." That judgment was vindicated three years later.

Compensation and ownership: her pay is mostly equity, with total compensation for fiscal 2025 on the order of $30 million (including RSUs and options), and she holds about 4 million shares (including unvested). This structure aligns with shareholder interests—the stock rises, she gains; the stock falls, she loses. But note that her stake is only 0.24% of total shares outstanding; in absolute terms the incentive is not large against an $800 billion market cap.

One organizational detail: AMD's engineering teams are "architecture-led" rather than "product-led"—each Zen generation has its specs set by the architecture team, with the product team responsible for execution. This structure let it compensate for lagging process nodes with architecture (chiplet was the architecture team's idea), but it also makes it slow to respond in new markets—the GPU software stack needs product-team leadership, and AMD only elevated the head of ROCm to SVP level in 2024. Organizational change moves slower than the technology catch-up.

One more thing worth noting: AMD's engineering culture is strong, but its marketing capability is weak. It holds one product launch a year, and its developer conference is one-tenth the size of Nvidia's GTC. This is not fatal, but it means AMD's good products take longer for customers to discover—MI450's specs are not weak, yet customers' first reaction is still "let's test it first," not "let's place an order."

Conclusion

Management verdict: capability 9, governance 7, succession 5. Lisa Su is one of the best CEOs in semiconductors this decade, and there is no arguing that. But reliance on a single person is itself a risk, and that risk is not priced into a 125x valuation.
