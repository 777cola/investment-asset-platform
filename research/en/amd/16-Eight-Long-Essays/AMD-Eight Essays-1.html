Core view

Three misconceptions dog the AMD story: that it is a "cheap version of Nvidia"; that doubling its data center business means it is challenging Nvidia; and that its high growth justifies a high valuation. Stack the three together and you get a 125x P/E.This piece sets out to take them apart.

Misconception 1: AMD is a "mini Nvidia"

No. AMD is a CPU company; its GPU business has simply grown quickly over the past two years. Of the RMB 11.536 billion in 2026Q2 revenue, Data Center contributed RMB 6.718 billion (mostly GPUs), Client RMB 3.062 billion (CPUs), Embedded RMB 977 million (FPGA and adaptive computing) and Gaming RMB 779 million. CPU-related revenue (client plus server CPUs) still accounts for more than 40%.

The distinction matters because the two companies rest on different foundations. Nvidia's root is CUDA, a developer ecosystem built over twenty years; AMD's root is the x86 licence, four decades of instruction-set compatibility. The former's moat is software; the latter's moat is a licence. A software moat weakens as the computing paradigm shifts; a licence moat does not.

The right analogy, then, is not "AMD is a mini-Nvidia" but "AMD is the only company that holds both an x86 licence and GPU capability." That identity is strong on the CPU side and weak on the GPU side.

Misconception 2: Doubling means challenging Nvidia

Q2 2026 data center revenue of RMB 6.718 billion, up 107% YoY, sounds like a challenge to Nvidia. But Nvidia's data center revenue for the same period (FY2027Q1) was RMB 75.2 billion—AMD is one-eleventh of that. Even after doubling, AMD stands at RMB 135 billion against Nvidia's RMB 250 billion (extrapolated from its growth rate); the gap narrows from 11x to 2x—still a catch-up, not a challenge.

Share tells the story better. On 1H26 revenue, Nvidia holds about 60% of AI accelerators, cloud providers' in-house chips 22% and AMD 9%. AMD needs three years to go from 9% to 15%, and needs Nvidia to stumble to reach 25%. "Challenge" is premature.

Misconception 3: High growth deserves a high valuation

Revenue +50%, data center +107% — that growth rate justifies 30–40x, not 125x. The gap comes down to durability of growth: if revenue doubles in 2027 and still grows 50% in 2028, 125x can be digested; if it doubles in 2027 and grows only 20% in 2028, 125x is the ceiling.

Our base case is the latter. Cloud capex is in year three of the cycle, and 2028 growth will most likely fall back to 10%–15%. AMD's growth curve will track the industry, not decouple from it.

Strip out the three misconceptions and AMD's shape becomes clear: a strong CPU, a GPU that is catching up, system integration just getting started, a 9-out-of-10 CEO, and a 125x price tag. The point of the cognitive reset is to stop pricing the stock as "the Nvidia substitute" and start pricing it as "the second source."

Lay out the timeline and AMD has changed identity five times in fifty-seven years: a follower in memory and logic chips in 1969; the x86 second source via the IBM PC in 1982; a CPU+GPU dual line after the USD 5.4 billion ATI acquisition in 2006; fabless after the 2009 fab spin-off; and back at the table on Zen in 2017. Every identity shift was forced, not chosen. Understand this and you will not treat "MI450 succeeds" as inevitable.

In 2023 the narrative was "AI second source," in 2025 "data center doubles," and in 2026 "system solutions provider (Helios)." The three versions carried multiples of 30x, 60x and 125x. Each narrative upgrade doubles the multiple, while revenue has less than doubled. That is the problem the cognitive reset must solve: separate how much of the move reflects business improvement and how much reflects a bigger story.

Nvidia was founded in 1993 and has spent thirty-three years on accelerated computing; AMD has made five transformations in fifty-seven years. The difference in focus ultimately shows up in ecosystem depth — twenty years of accumulation behind CUDA versus only seven for ROCm, interrupted twice along the way. This is not a capability problem but a time problem, and time cannot be compressed.

One more observation: at USD 483.36, the stock sits just 16.8% below its 52-week high of USD 580.91 but is up 153% from its low of USD 190.95 a year ago. The same price is "more than doubled" to someone who bought last year and "down 17%" to someone who bought at the end of June. That divergence is itself a feature of high-multiple stocks — the price is set by when you bought, not by company value.

Conclusion

Conclusion: AMD is not a "mini Nvidia" — its identity is a three-in-one of x86, GPU and systems integration. Identity determines positioning, and positioning determines the valuation ceiling. Of the three misconceptions, pricing AMD as Nvidia is the most expensive.
