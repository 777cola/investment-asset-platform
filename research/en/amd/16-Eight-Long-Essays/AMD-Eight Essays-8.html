Core thesis

The previous seven pieces covered "what this is"; this one covers "how to do it." The framework boils down to three lines: buy uncertainty with price, buy certainty with evidence, sell with valuation.

Two paths to Buy

First, price. USD 350 equates to 18x 2027 neutral EPS and embeds the bear case of "share only reaching 15%, industry growth of 15%". At that level you don't lose money even if the story only half materialises. This is the Graham-style approach: protect yourself with price.

Second, evidence. If Q4 results show MI450 at USD 3 billion for the quarter with a 55% gross margin, then USD 450 is also a buy — because the biggest uncertainty (whether it can scale) has been eliminated. This is the Fisher-style approach: trade price for certainty.

What both paths share: neither requires forecasting. Prices are public, financials are public, and all you have to do is wait.

Three lines of selling

Falsification line: gross margin below 52%, or data center revenue down QoQ. Trigger means liquidate the position, regardless of price.

Valuation line: above 29x 2027 EPS (about USD 700). At that level the market is already pricing 2028, yet visibility into 2028 is zero — halve the position.

Opportunity line: a higher-conviction name appears (TSMC at 20x, for example) — switch. This is not "sell because you've made enough" — it is "sell because there is a better choice."

Three rules for position sizing

First, no single stock above 5%. AMD has high beta and large geopolitical exposure; 5% is already the cap.

Second, do not stack it with holdings of the same factor. If you already hold Nvidia or TSMC, AMD's position should be compressed accordingly—the three names have a correlation of 0.7–0.9, so they are essentially one position.

Third, no leverage. July 28–29 saw -13.2% in two days; leverage turns volatility into permanent loss. For high-beta stocks, the first rule of position management is "live long enough."

Opportunity cost must be counted. For the same money, TSMC is 28x TTM, a monopoly position, with diversifiable geopolitical risk; Nvidia is 28x, ecosystem monopoly, heavy off-balance-sheet commitments; AMD is 125x, second-supplier position, single-customer dependence. Of the three, AMD has the worst risk-reward—not the worst company, the worst price.

Time cost must also be counted. Waiting for USD 350 may take six months, or it may take two years; chasing the high may earn 20% immediately, or it may trap you for three years. In expected-value terms, the cost of waiting is "missing a rally"; the cost of chasing is "permanent capital loss." The two are asymmetric, so waiting is right.

Five checklist items—run through them every time before buying: ① Is gross margin ≥55%? ② Is data center revenue positive quarter on quarter? ③ Is MI450 ≥USD 3 billion in a single quarter? ④ Are cloud capex forecasts still being raised? ⑤ Is valuation ≤25x 2027 EPS? Only act when three of the five are "yes."

Also be clear about "who should buy AMD." Three types of investors: ① Industrial investors who believe the second supplier's share will go from 9% to 20%—they should buy below USD 350 and wait three years; ② Portfolio managers allocating to the AI theme—they should buy Nvidia or TSMC, with AMD only a beta supplement at no more than 2%; ③ Short-term traders—they should exit before earnings, because "good news doesn't lift the stock" has already happened twice. None of the three should hold a heavy position at USD 483.

Four dates to put on the calendar: October 2026 (Q3 earnings—watch MI450's first revenue and gross margin), November (Nvidia FY27Q3—watch the Rubin production ramp), January 2027 (Q4 earnings—watch whether MI450 reaches USD 3 billion in a single quarter), April (Q1 earnings—watch whether data center revenue grows 80% YoY). Any "miss" at any of the four dates is enough to cut the rating by one notch.

Finally, three common mistakes. First, treating "second supplier" as "catching up"—the second supplier's ceiling is share, not technology. Second, treating "doubling" as "acceleration"—after doubling comes decelerating growth, not another doubling. Third, treating "excellent CEO" as "excellent company"—the person is a 9, the position is a 6, the price is a 0. All three mistakes lead to the same outcome: buying a good company at the wrong price, then waiting three years for it to come back.

Conclusion

Framework summary: the only two entry points are USD 350 or MI450 at USD 3 billion; the only exit signal (the falsification point) is 52% gross margin; the only position cap is 5%. None of the three requires forecasting the future—only disciplined execution.
