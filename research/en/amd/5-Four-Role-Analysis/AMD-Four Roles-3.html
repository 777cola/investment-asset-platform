Industry Research (Munger's Lens) — AMD

AMD (AMD) | Position in the semiconductor chain and in the cycle

Core view

AI accelerators are the most profitable and most crowded segment of the semiconductor industry. Of roughly USD 800 billion in global AI capex in 2026, 60% goes to Nvidia, 9% to AMD, and the remainder to in-house ASICs and memory.AMD occupies the position of "highest value share, lowest pricing power."

Cycle position: capex at a peak, not a trough

The four North American cloud providers will spend roughly USD 800 billion of capex combined in 2026, up 60% YoY, and 2027 guidance is still climbing. This is the third year of the cycle. Historically, semiconductor capex cycles rarely rise for four consecutive years — 2000, 2008, 2018 and 2021 all peaked in the third year.

That does not mean 2027 must decline, but it does leave the "double" assumption with very little margin for error. If cloud capex grows only 10% in 2027, AI accelerator market growth would fall from 60% to 20%, AMD's share gains would be offset by market contraction, and data center revenue might grow only 30% — far short of "double".

Munger would say: in the third year of a cycle, using the first year's growth rate to value a company is the most common mistake.

Supply adds another bottleneck: CoWoS. TSMC's 2026 CoWoS capacity was fully booked in 2025, with Nvidia taking about 60%, AMD 20%, and the rest going to Broadcom and others. A substantial part of AMD's RMB 808 million in capex is prepayment for that capacity. How much capacity AMD secures directly determines how many MI450 units it can deliver — this is the first constraint on whether 2027 guidance can be met, and it is harder than demand.

Structure: value concentrates toward standard-setters

Profit distribution along the chain is highly uneven: EDA 80% gross margin, TSMC 58%, Nvidia 75%, AMD 56%, ODM 6%, cloud providers 30% operating margin. The closer a player sits to "defining the standard," the higher the gross margin.

AMD sits in the middle. It owns the x86 standard on the CPU side but not the AI compute standard on the GPU side, so its gross margin falls between "standard setter" and "assembler" — and 56% reflects exactly that position.

There is only one path up: make ROCm the standard, or make Helios the de facto rack standard. The former takes five years or more; the latter requires Nvidia to stumble. Neither is within visible range by 2027.

Export controls are another variable. In 2026, US restrictions on AI chips to China are tiered by compute density; MI450 is not currently in the strictest tier and can be exported in a downgraded configuration. But the rule is reviewed every six months, and any tightening would affect 15%–20% of revenue. This is policy risk, not operating risk, and cannot be mitigated through company effort.

Conclusion

Industry view: good industry, bad position, late cycle. AI accelerators will be one of the best businesses of the next decade, but AMD's position in the industry means it cannot capture the best slice of the profit pool — and the current share price assumes it can.
