Core view

AMD is not selling chips; it is selling "the second option besides Nvidia." Three types of buyers want that option: cloud providers looking to push down prices, model companies seeking supply security, and sovereign customers determined not to be locked in. All three buyer types are real, but their payment logic differs entirely—one pays to save money, one pays for delivery timelines, one pays for politics.

Why buy AMD: three real reasons

First, cost savings. At the same FP8 inference throughput, an MI450 rack costs roughly 70%–80% of a same-generation Nvidia solution. Cloud providers calculate cost per token, not cost per FLOPS, and AMD has an edge on that metric: its larger HBM capacity (432GB) fits a bigger KV cache, enabling higher per-card concurrency.

Second, lead time. Nvidia's rack lead time remains above 30 weeks in the first half of 2026, versus 12–16 weeks for AMD. For a lab racing to hit a model launch window, going live three months earlier is worth a lot.

Third, the second-supplier policy. Microsoft, Meta and Oracle all have explicit rules: no critical component may have only one supplier. This rule has nothing to do with whether AMD's product is good or bad — it is procurement policy.This is AMD's most secure demand — not because it wins, but because someone must be there.

On specific orders: Oracle announced in October 2025 that it would purchase 50,000 MI450 chips for OCI superclusters; Microsoft Azure was the earliest deployer of MI300X and in 2026 moved to testing Helios racks; Meta is testing both Nvidia and AMD solutions at roughly a 3:1 ratio. None of these orders is large, but each one tells the market: the second supplier is not a spare tire, it is an institution.

There is another layer from a commercial perspective: customers do not "buy once," they "buy every year." Cloud providers procure under three-year framework agreements with annual purchase orders, which means AMD is not winning an order but a position.Once a position is secured, it will not be replaced unless delivery runs into major problems — that is the most valuable part of the second-supplier business.

Why it won't buy more: where is the ceiling

The ceiling differs by buyer type. Cloud providers typically allocate 20%–30% of share to a second supplier and no more, given the incumbent's clear ecosystem and performance advantages; model companies would go to 40%, but their own fundraising capacity dictates purchase volumes; sovereign customers allocate generously, though total volume is small.

Weighted together, AMD's steady-state share of AI accelerators is most likely 15%–25%. At 9% in 2026 (USD 6.7 billion vs Nvidia's USD 75.2 billion), it has room to double; at 20% in 2027, data center revenue would reach roughly USD 150 billion — the optimistic scenario, and the source of the company's "double" target.

In other words, AMD's growth story does not require it to win, only to be needed. The logic holds, but it confers no pricing power: share has a ceiling, prices are benchmarked, and gross margin is capped. Duan Yongping would say: this is a good business, but not a good business that can set its own prices.

That is also why AMD does not engage in price wars. Its quotes already sit 20%–30% below Nvidia's; going lower would push gross margin below 45%, and 45% cannot support USD 2.4 billion in annual R&D. AMD's competitive strategy is "equal performance, 20% cheaper, half the lead time," not "cheaper." Price is its entry ticket, not its weapon.

Conclusion

Business judgement: real demand, a clear ceiling, no pricing power. Three buyer groups give AMD a visible growth curve (share 9%→20%) and, equally, a clear valuation cap. The money is in share gains, not pricing power.
