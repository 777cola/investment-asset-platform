## The takeover hand: RMB 2.3 billion in cash, share price at USD 3

In October 2014, AMD stood on the brink of collapse. Revenue was USD 5.5 billion, the company had posted three consecutive years of losses, cash of USD 2.3 billion sat against USD 2 billion of debt, Intel had squeezed its x86 share below 10%, and its server share was in the single digits. The board cycled through three CEOs before finally choosing an internal technologist — Lisa Su, then COO.

Her first move was not to launch new products but to cut product lines: mobile chips, tablet chips and low-end APUs were all discontinued, with resources concentrated on the Zen architecture and servers. The second was to sell off manufacturing — in 2016 the packaging and testing plant went to Tongfu Microelectronics, and all wafer production was handed to GlobalFoundries and TSMC. The third was the 2017 Zen launch, which used chiplets to sidestep process-node disadvantages.

The results are quantifiable: server CPU share rose from 1% in 2017 to more than 30% in 2026, while data center revenue went from USD 1.5 billion in 2019 to a 2026 annualized USD 27 billion. This is not luck — it is three correct strategic trade-offs.

## Capital allocation track record: only additions, never subtractions

Only five major moves in twelve years, each with a clear rationale:

- 2016: Sold the packaging and testing plant to Tongfu Microelectronics in exchange for a long-term capacity agreement — the first step toward an asset-light model.

- February 2022: All-stock acquisition of Xilinx for USD 49 billion. Gained FPGA, adaptive computing and a stable revenue stream from defense and communications, while expanding share count from 1.2 billion shares to 1.6 billion shares.

- 2022: Acquired Pensando for USD 1.9 billion, gaining DPU and SmartNIC to complete the data center networking stack.

- 2024: Acquired Silo AI (a Finnish model team) for USD 665 million to bolster software and model adaptation capabilities.

- 2025: Acquired ZT Systems for USD 4.9 billion, gaining rack-level system integration capability, then sold the manufacturing portion to Sanmina — retaining only design and integration. This deal is the precondition for on-time delivery of Helios racks.

Dividends: zero. Buybacks: yes, but only large enough to offset equity incentive dilution, not a value-return tool. This is not in the same league as Nvidia (USD 26 billion returned in a single quarter of 2026). For a company still in expansion, this is reasonable; for a company at 125x P/E, it means shareholder returns depend entirely on the share price.

Two deals warrant a question mark. ZT Systems' USD 4.9 billion bought "system integration capability," but the scarcity of that capability is declining — Dell, Hon Hai and Wistron can all build racks. Silo AI's USD 665 million bought a team, not a product. Neither deal is wrong, but neither was a must-buy.

## Integrity and governance: no scandals, but structural issues

No accounting blemishes: no restatement, no SEC investigation, no substantive allegations from short sellers. Related-party transactions are clean, and the share of executive pay tied to performance is reasonable. That is not a given in semis—several companies ran into revenue recognition problems over the same period.

Two structural issues stand out. First, the chairman and CEO roles sit with one person. Lisa Su has also served as chair since 2022; the lead independent director can convene independent director meetings, but for a company with a market cap of USD 800 billion that is a weak check. Second, the OpenAI warrants—a cap of 160 million shares, roughly 9.6% potential dilution, with exercise conditions tied to purchase volume. When the deal was announced in October 2025, it was read as "locking in a major customer," but it also cedes part of shareholders' future returns to a single customer.

Board composition is reasonably healthy: 9 of 11 directors are independent, the audit committee is fully independent, and 3 directors have semiconductor operations backgrounds. The governance risk is not "acting recklessly" but "relying too much on one person."

## Succession: the biggest single-point risk

Lisa Su was born in 1969 and turns 57 in 2026. Semiconductor CEOs typically retire around 65, which in theory leaves her eight years. AMD, however, has no public succession pipeline: CTO Mark Papermaster is already 66, CFO Jean Hu comes from a finance background, and data center head Forrest Norrod is the closest candidate but has no track record of running the whole company on his own.

The valuation impact is real. Much of AMD's excess return over the past twelve years is the "Lisa Su premium" — the market trusts her judgment. That premium appears in no financial statement, yet it sits inside the 125x P/E.If she announces retirement one day and the successor lacks equal credibility, the stock de-rates 20% first and the fundamentals get discussed afterwards.

The metrics to watch are simple: whether the next 8-K discloses a COO or President-level appointment, and who presents the 2028 roadmap at analyst day.
