Title and lead-in

Title: AMD earned USD 11.5 billion—so why did the stock still fall 7%?

Lead: On August 4, AMD delivered the best earnings report in its history. The next day, the stock fell 7%. This is not the market failing to understand—it is the market calculating too clearly.

Part One: How good is this earnings report, really?

Start with the numbers. Q2 revenue was USD 11.536 billion, up 50% year on year, a record high. Data center revenue was USD 6.718 billion, up 107% year on year, accounting for 58% of total revenue. Gross margin was 56%, up 12.8 percentage points from a year earlier. Net income was USD 2.297 billion, up one and a half times.

More important is the guidance. The company said it can do USD 13 billion in Q3, versus consensus of USD 12.5 billion. Capex was USD 808 million, 2.7 times consensus — for a chip company that does not build its own fabs, suddenly spending this much has only one explanation: locking in capacity for next year's orders.

Lisa Su gave two targets on the call: double data center revenue by 2027, and EPS above USD 20. On those two targets, the USD 483 share price is just 24 times earnings — not expensive.

In one sentence, this earnings report: the money was genuinely earned, and earned at high quality — gross margin rose 12.8 percentage points, showing it was not low-price volume chasing. AMD this quarter deserves the word "excellent".

Part Two: So why is it still falling?

Because the market is not buying this quarter's USD 11.5 billion; it is buying the doubling in 2027. And that doubling will only show up in the financials in Q4 this year and Q1 next year.

The more direct reason is "expectations." The day before the earnings release, the stock had already risen 7% as some funds bet ahead of the print. The next day it fell 7%, giving back the entire gain. Between the rise and the fall, nothing changed at the company; what changed was "who holds the stock at what price."

One detail worth remembering: on the day Nvidia reported in May, AMD rose 8.1%; in August, when its own results came in above the consensus, it fell 7% instead. The same good news produces opposite reactions at different valuations. In May, AMD traded at 90x earnings; in August, at 125x.

Another comparison: AMD's data center revenue was USD 6.7 billion, versus Nvidia's USD 75.2 billion in the same period. In other words, AMD doubled and is still one-eleventh of Nvidia. Doubling deserves applause, but it is still far from a "challenge."

Part 3: What does 125x buy?

A 125x P/E means you pay USD 125 for every USD 1 the company earns in a year. That price is justified in only one case: profits grow at a high rate for several years running.

AMD's problem is that its growth is not up to AMD. It sells "the second option after Nvidia," and customers buy for three reasons — 20% cheaper, half the lead time, and a second-source policy — none of which is "because it is better."

Nvidia's gross margin is 74.9%; AMD's is 56%. The 19-point gap is not manufacturing efficiency but pricing power. Whoever holds pricing power holds the profit.

One harder fact: the company says "data center revenue doubles" and also says "EPS above USD 20." The two targets do not add up — doubling supports only USD 14–15 of EPS; to reach USD 20, data center revenue must exceed USD 100 billion, more than triple this year. Targets that conflict show management itself is talking in its most optimistic terms.

Part Four: What price is worth waiting for

We ran three scenarios. Bear case: share stays in the single digits, 2027 EPS of USD 12, 20x multiple, USD 240. Base case: the doubling materializes but the multiple compresses, EPS of USD 19, 25x, USD 475. Bull case: upside surprise, EPS of USD 24, 30x, USD 720.

Weighted at 35%, 45% and 20%, the expected price is USD 442, 8.6% below the current price. The upside requires a 49% gain and needs the company to do better than its own targets; the downside requires a 50% drop and only needs the industry to normalize. At those odds, this is not a position to size up.

So our conclusion is watch, not avoid. The execution deserves a place on the watchlist: Lisa Su's twelve years, selling manufacturing, acquiring Xilinx, betting on chiplet, winning OpenAI's 6GW and Anthropic's 2GW — every step right. What it lacks is not capability but position.

Position can only change with time and a product generation. Act when one of two signals appears: the stock falls below USD 350, or MI450 quarterly revenue exceeds USD 3 billion with gross margin holding at 55%.

One risk not to overlook: customer concentration. The top five customers account for more than half of revenue; a single cutback by one of them would force a guidance reset next quarter. This is not speculation — it happened once in 2024, when one cloud provider delayed purchases and AMD's data center revenue fell 8% quarter on quarter.

Conclusion

Final word: AMD made money this quarter, but has not yet earned its position. At 125x, you are paying for "the position will change" — and a change in position cannot be verified by any earnings report.

Wait for USD 350, or wait for MI450 revenue. Until then, the best move is to do nothing.
