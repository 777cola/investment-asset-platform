## Q1: Is this a good business?

Good. Why good? Because customers have to pay every year, and they pay willingly—no, not willingly, they don't dare not to pay. A company with 50,000 employees has its email, meetings, documents and permissions all on Microsoft. Tell it to switch, and the IT director objects first, because he bears the blame when something goes wrong.

Gross margin 67%, net margin 39.7%, USD 133.7 billion earned in a year. What does that mean? **You can count on one hand the companies worldwide that earn USD 100 billion a year.** And it does not do so by selling resources; it does so by selling habits—habits are worth more than resources, because resources have a price and habits do not.

There is one drawback: the business is getting heavier. It used to sell licenses, a matter of one disc; now it must build data centers, buy GPUs and pay electricity bills. Heavy-asset businesses are inherently valued lower than light-asset ones—that is common sense.

## Q2: Will it still be around in ten years?

Yes. I don't need long to think about this one.

Will people still use Office in ten years? Yes. The form may change, but companies will still need a place to write documents, hold meetings and store files. Will companies still use the cloud in ten years? Yes, and more of it. As long as these two things hold, Microsoft stays.

What could really change is "who collects the money." If AI makes software unnecessary for writing documents — you just talk to an assistant and it handles everything — then Office's form will change. But it is the form that changes, not the need.**Microsoft has been beaten many times in its history (phones, search, browsers), but each time it held on to the pot that feeds it.**

## Question three: Can you understand it?

What I do understand: subscriptions, price increases, renewals, and why customers cannot leave. These I understand; it is the same logic as when I ran BBK — channels, brand, user habits.

What I do not understand: with USD 175 billion invested a year, what the return is. That I cannot say for sure. GPUs become obsolete in six years, while data centers are built to last twenty; if AI demand falls in between, these assets turn into a pile of iron.

My principle is: **what I do not understand does not affect my judgment of whether it is a good business, but it affects the price I pay.** A good business plus capex I do not understand means I bid a little below the market. That is not conservatism; it is my duty — I do not make money I do not understand.

## Question 4: At what price to buy

USD 464, 25.8x P/E. What to say about this price—not expensive, but not cheap either.

Let me do a rough calculation: it earns RMB 133.7 billion a year; at 25x that's RMB 3.3 trillion, roughly in line with the current market cap. In other words, **buying at the current price, what you earn is the money from its future profit growth, not from valuation re-rating.** How much can profit grow? Azure up 43%, total revenue up 18%, profit up around 20%. So your annualized return is roughly 15%–20%, provided the valuation holds.

15%–20% annualized, for a company with this much certainty, I think is acceptable. But if it falls to 430, the annualized return becomes 22%–25%, which would be very comfortable. **So my approach is: buy a little now, buy more below 430.**

## Question 5: What do you fear most?

The biggest fear is not Google, nor antitrust. The biggest fear is that "AI ultimately isn't that big a deal."

Everyone now assumes AI will transform everything the way the internet did, so they are willing to pay a premium for compute. If three years from now it turns out AI is just a better search engine plus a better writing assistant, and enterprises are willing to pay only USD 10 per user per month extra rather than USD 30, then Azure's growth will fall from 43% to 15%, and the RMB 175 billion investment will have been wasted.

How big is this risk? I don't know. But I know one thing: **when everyone believes the same thing, the price of that thing has already priced in the good news.** So I won't go all in.

## Question 6: What should those who already hold it do?

Hold it. Don't sell just because it's up 19%, and don't add just because it's down 10%.

The precondition for holding is that you thought clearly about "why buy" when you bought it. If the reason was "Azure is growing fast," then check Azure's growth every quarter; if the reason was "customers can't run away," that reason won't change within five years, and you don't need to read the quarterly reports at all.

The worst kind is the person who says he was right when it rises and says the market is wrong when it falls. **The market is never wrong; you just haven't thought it through.** If you have thought it through, rises and falls are both opportunities; if you haven't, rises and falls are both torment.
