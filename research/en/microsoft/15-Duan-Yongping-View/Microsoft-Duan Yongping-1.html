Core call: I understand the business, but not the RMB 175 billion it is spending

Ask about Microsoft the way Duan Yongping would and the answer is simpler than you'd think: a good business, customers who can't leave, and one that will most likely still be here in ten years. The only thing I can't figure out is this — USD 175 billion a year on data centers, and how long it takes to earn that money back.

The part I understand is worth 25x; the part I don't understand gets no money. So I'm buying, but only a little, and I'll add more if it falls.

Q1: Is this a good business?

Good. Why good? Because customers pay every year, and they pay willingly—or rather, they don't dare not to. A company with 50,000 employees runs its email, meetings, documents and permissions on Microsoft. Tell it to switch and the IT director objects first, because he carries the blame when something goes wrong.

Gross margin 67%, net margin 39.7%, USD 133.7 billion earned in a year. What does that mean? You can count on one hand the companies worldwide that earn USD 100 billion a year. And it does not do so by selling resources; it does so by selling habits—habits are worth more than resources, because resources have a price and habits do not.

One drawback: the business is getting heavier. It used to sell licences, a matter of one disc; now it must build data centres, buy GPUs and pay electricity bills. Heavy-asset businesses are inherently valued lower than light-asset ones—that is common sense.

Q2: Will it still be around in ten years?

Yes — this one doesn't take long to think through.

Will people still use Office in ten years? Yes. The form may change, but companies will still need somewhere to write documents, hold meetings and store files. Will companies still use the cloud in ten years? Yes, and more of it. As long as both hold, Microsoft stays.

What could genuinely change is who collects the money. If AI makes software unnecessary for writing documents — you simply talk to an assistant and it handles everything — then Office's form will change. But it is the form that changes, not the need.Microsoft has been beaten many times in its history (phones, search, browsers), yet each time it held on to the pot that feeds it.

Question three: Can you understand it?

What I do understand: subscriptions, price increases, renewals and why customers cannot leave. That is the same logic I ran BBK on — channels, brand, user habits.

What I do not understand: with USD 175 billion invested a year, what the return is. That I cannot say for sure. GPUs become obsolete in six years, while data centers are built to last twenty; if AI demand falls in between, these assets turn into a pile of iron.

My principle is: what I do not understand does not affect my judgment of whether it is a good business, but it affects the price I pay. A good business plus capex I do not understand means I bid a little below the market. That is not conservatism; it is my duty — I do not make money I do not understand.

Question 4: At what price to buy

USD 464, 25.8x P/E. Not expensive, but not cheap either.

A rough calculation: it earns RMB 133.7 billion a year; at 25x that's RMB 3.3 trillion, roughly in line with the current market cap. In other words, at the current price you are being paid for future profit growth, not for a valuation re-rating. How much can profit grow? Azure up 43%, total revenue up 18%, profit up around 20%. That implies an annualised return of roughly 15%–20%, provided the valuation holds.

15%–20% annualised, for a company with this much certainty, is acceptable in my view. But if it falls to 430, the annualised return becomes 22%–25%, which would be very comfortable. So my approach is: buy a little now, buy more below 430.

Question 5: What do you fear most?

The biggest fear isn't Google, and it isn't antitrust. It's that "AI ultimately isn't that big a deal."

Everyone now assumes AI will transform everything the way the internet did, and on that basis is willing to pay a premium for compute. If, three years from now, AI turns out to be just a better search engine plus a better writing assistant, and enterprises are willing to pay only USD 10 per user per month extra rather than USD 30, then Azure's growth falls from 43% to 15% and the RMB 175 billion investment has been wasted.

How big is this risk? I don't know. But one thing I do know: when everyone believes the same thing, the price of that thing has already priced in the good news. So I won't go all in.

Question 6: What should those who already hold it do?

Hold. Don't sell just because it's up 19%, and don't add just because it's down 10%.

The precondition for holding is that you were clear on the "why buy" when you bought. If the reason was "Azure is growing fast," check Azure's growth every quarter; if it was "customers can't run away," that reason won't change within five years and you needn't read the quarterly reports at all.

The worst kind is the investor who claims he was right when it rises and that the market is wrong when it falls. The market is never wrong; you just haven't thought it through. If you have, rises and falls are both opportunities; if you haven't, both are torment.

Conclusion

Conclusion: buy in tranches, don't rush. A good business at a fair price, seven-tenths of it understandable. The three-tenths I can't underwrite — the return on RMB 175 billion of capex — is protected by the price: buy a little here, double down below 430, and stop chasing above 560. This money won't be made quickly, but you'll sleep at night.
