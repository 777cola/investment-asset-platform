Six-Gate Screen — Microsoft

Microsoft (MSFT) | Six-Gate Screen

Core view: a good company at a fair price, but no discount

Four of the six gates are barely in dispute: subscriptions, compute and seats are sold on a revenue structure that is transparent at a glance; gross margin of 67.2% and operating margin of 45.1%; customers cannot switch away. The fifth gate is where it stalls. At 25.8x TTM and 23x FY2027 estimates, the stock is not expensive for a company that can still grow EPS by 20%, but its free cash flow yield is only 1.9% — the price embeds no discount, only certainty. So the call is Buy, but only in tranches, because the 16% gap on July 30 has already priced in the good news.

The first three hurdles: understandable, quantifiable, defensible

Microsoft's business model can be summed up in one sentence: enterprises pay a per-seat subscription every month for Office, Teams and Entra; when they need compute, they pay for Azure by usage; and now there is an extra layer, USD 30 per seat for Copilot. FY2026Q4 revenue was USD 90.01 billion, up 17.8% YoY, of which Intelligent Cloud contributed USD 39.3 billion, up 32%, and Microsoft Cloud totalled USD 59.3 billion, up 27%, or USD 214 billion for the full year. Two-thirds of revenue comes from annual, per-seat commercial contracts—income inherently more predictable than advertising or hardware.

The evidence of a good business lies in the margins: gross margin 67.2%, operating margin 45.1%, net income USD 35.77 billion, net margin 39.7%. Fewer than ten companies worldwide deliver all three at once. Note, however, that gross margin fell 1.4 percentage points YoY—selling software carries 80% gross margin, selling compute carries 60%, so every step up in Azure's mix pulls the blended gross margin down a step. That is structural, not a misstep.

Moat score: 9. Not because customers like Microsoft, but because they cannot switch: for a company with 50,000 employees, replacing Office + Entra + Azure means rebuilding its entire IT architecture and re-signing every identity, permission, audit and compliance document. That depth of embedding produces inertia, and inertia is more reliable than preference—preferences change, inertia does not.

Stage four: capability is fine, accounting is the problem

When Nadella took over in February 2014, Microsoft's market cap was USD 300 billion; it is now USD 3.45 trillion. He got exactly one thing right: switching from one-off licence sales to annual subscriptions, then moving that distribution capability onto the cloud. In 2014 this looked like strategy; in 2026 it looks like infrastructure.

The deduction relates to one item: the depreciation life was extended from 15 years to 25 years. Assuming the affected assets account for half the depreciation base and the life is stretched to 25 years, annual depreciation is roughly USD 4–5 billion lower, equivalent to about USD 0.5 of EPS, or less than 4%. The amount is small, but the direction is worth remembering—it defers costs rather than eliminating them. In the same report, FY2027 capex guidance "changed" from USD 190 billion to USD 175 billion, also a definitional adjustment (some leases and third-party capacity are no longer counted in capex); cash spending did not fall. Together, the two make the reported figures look better than the cash.

Score: 8. Capable enough, transparency discounted, but not enough to warrant a veto.

Gate Five: margin-of-safety math hinges on cash flow, not P/E

At 25.8x TTM against 18% revenue growth and over 20% EPS growth, the PEG is roughly 1.2 — expensive by 2020 standards, mid-range against Microsoft's own 22–32x band over the past five years. The real issue is not the P/E but cash: FY2026 free cash flow of USD 67 billion on a USD 3.45 trillion market cap, a 1.94% yield, with quarterly capex of USD 41 billion, +70% YoY, and free cash flow of USD 19.6 billion, -23% YoY.

In other words, you are buying rising profit alongside shrinking cash. That is normal in a capex upcycle, but it caps the valuation: until capex peaks, Microsoft's cash flow cannot support a multiple above 30x. The base case of USD 564 (FY2028 EPS 23.5 × 24x) implies just 21% upside from the current USD 464.72; the bear case of USD 400 is -14%, a reward-to-risk ratio of 1.5:1. That is enough for a company this certain, but not enough to build the full position in one go. Score 6, a marginal pass.

Gate 6: Can the buy thesis be written as three falsifiable sentences?

Yes. First, Azure growth held above 35% in all four quarters of FY2027 (43% in Q4, with guidance of 45% for the next quarter). Second, of the RMB 678 billion in commercial RPO, the 30% recognized within 12 months converted to revenue on schedule, implying a commercial bookings conversion rate of no less than 30% over the next four quarters. Third, paid Copilot seats rose from 30 million to 60 million, and the company began disclosing ARPU.

All three can be reconciled quarterly, which is Microsoft's biggest advantage: it does not ask you to believe any story, only to look at the numbers. Discipline scores 7; the deduction is for execution difficulty. The stock rose 19% in the two days after earnings, so a one-time purchase means paying for someone else's unrealized gains. Scaling in is not market timing; it is refusing to let a single-point judgment determine the entire cost basis.

Beyond the six gates: two things easily overlooked

First, RPO quality matters more than the headline number. RPO stands at RMB 678 billion, up 84% YoY, but only +25% excluding OpenAI. Non-frontier-model customers are providing a floor, yet OpenAI alone accounts for a large chunk of the increment. Weighted duration is 2.3 years, with only 30% recognised within 12 months—the order book is not revenue, it is a commitment.

Second, the counterparty on the other side of capex is shifting. Microsoft plans to invest USD 175 billion in FY2027, equivalent to 53% of full-year FY2026 revenue. Amazon and Google are doing the same, and Oracle is taking OpenAI orders to grab capacity. The three no longer compete on whose technology is better, but on whose money is cheaper and whose power arrives sooner. This layer of competition does not show up in the financials.

Conclusion

Conclusion: Buy, in tranches. The trigger is price, not news. Open the first tranche (one third) at the current USD 464.72; add a second, doubled, on a pullback below 430; complete the third once the FY2027Q1 results confirm Azure growth of ≥40%; stop adding above USD 560. Veto items: none. Four items to verify: the depreciation curve behind the FY2027 capex of USD 175 billion; the price/volume split within Azure growth; conversion of the 30% of RPO due within 12 months; and Copilot's actual ARPU.
