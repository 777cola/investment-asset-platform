## Duan Yongping: Does this business require a genius?

His standard is simple: a good business should not require a genius to run. Microsoft comes close to that standard—enterprise customers cannot switch away from it, and they are unlikely to leave even if prices rise. Office has raised prices many times, and customers renew after complaining; that is the most honest proof of a moat. He would not look at the number of Azure racks, because that is not something he understands; he looks at only one thing: whether this company can sell the same thing to the same customers and collect a little more money each year. Over the past twelve years, it has done so.

He would ask one question: Does Copilot make Office more expensive, or does it make Office better? If the former, it is a price increase with a ceiling (enterprise IT budgets grow 5%–8% a year); if the latter, it is a new product with no ceiling. 30 million seats and net additions doubling quarter over quarter show that the market temporarily accepts the former; the real answer depends on renewal rates, not seat counts.

He would also like this business's "lack of fuss": no need to guess anew each year what customers want, no need to rely on hit products, and revenue follows contracts. The drawback is equally obvious—he would not pay a premium for this kind of business, only wait for the price. By his habit, he would buy a little at USD 464, and only start buying seriously below 400.

## Buffett: Moats vs. Capital Expenditure

He would admit this is his type: stable demand, prepayments, dispersed customers, trustworthy management, consecutive dividend growth. These were the checklist he used when he bought Coca-Cola in 1988. He would also point out one thing — this company is shifting from "asset-light rent collection" to "asset-heavy factory building". FY2026 cash capital expenditure was RMB 115.9 billion, free cash flow RMB 67 billion, a ratio of 1.7:1; FY2027 capital expenditure is RMB 175 billion, and the ratio will deteriorate further.

His framework is "every dollar retained must create more than a dollar of market value." The current capital expenditure is for revenue after 2028; the account is not yet settled. How would he test it? Not with next quarter's growth rate, but with ten years: will Azure still be here in ten years, will enterprises still be using Office. The answer is most likely "yes" — enterprises will not swap out their identity systems twice; the sunk cost of such a migration is too high.

His conclusion would most likely be: buy, but keep the position small enough that even if depreciation blows up in 2028, it won't hurt. He would not pay more because "AI is the future," but he would pay for "customers can't switch" — that has been his lifelong preference.

## Munger: What would make this investment lose money

He would ask directly about the loss paths, and there are three answers: ① AI demand peaks in 2028, data centers become idle capacity, and depreciation still gets charged; ② the model layer commoditizes, and the inference price war pushes Azure's gross margin down to 40%; ③ regulation breaks up the vertical integration of cloud and models (the EU is already investigating cloud licensing terms).

The first is the most realistic. The RPO weighted term is 2.3 years, meaning it has to be renegotiated in 2029; if AI demand growth falls to 15% by then, the RMB 175 billion invested today becomes a complete mess—not a loss of money, but return on capital falling below 5%, and the valuation derating from 24x to 15x. The second has a not-low probability; inference costs for open-source models halve every year, and Azure's premium can only come from compliance and capacity, not from an "exclusive model."

His conclusion is roughly: the odds are acceptable, but room for error must be left—tranches, not all in. He would also add his usual line: invert, always invert—if this business is so good, why does the market only give it 25x instead of 40x? The answer is right there in the capex line.

## Li Lu: The infrastructure tax of the AI era

He steps outside single-company analysis to look at structure. AI will ultimately resemble electricity and the internet: value concentrates in a few "toll gates" — compute, models, distribution. Microsoft holds both compute (Azure) and distribution (Office, Windows, GitHub), one of the very few positions that survives technology cycles — whichever model wins, enterprises still type in Office and still rent compute.

He also cautions: the fate of infrastructure is for returns to be compressed to the average by regulation and competition. Railways, telecom, cloud — all traveled the same road: excess profit first, then overcapacity, then price war, finally a utility. Today's 45% operating margin is excess profit; a decade out it most likely reverts to 35%.

The question becomes: what multiple do you pay for this "decaying 45%"? At 23x, the implied assumption is that margins fall only 10 points over ten years and revenue still grows 12% a year — if that holds, 23x is a fair price; if not, it is 30% expensive. His answer would be: 23x is fair, not cheap, so buy only a portion.

## Risk-reward: 1.5:1 — a buy, but not worth a heavy position

Neutral 564, bear 400, against the current price of 464.72, the payoff ratio is (564 − 464.72) ÷ (464.72 − 400) = 1.53:1. This ratio means: you make 21% when right and lose 14% when wrong, so the win rate needs to exceed 40% to be worthwhile. Given Microsoft's certainty, the win rate is likely 55%–60%, so expected value is positive and the stock is a buy.

But it does not warrant a "heavy position." At the same 1.5:1, a company with 70% certainty deserves an 8% position, while one with 55% certainty deserves only 3%–4%. Microsoft sits at the upper end of the latter—certainty comes from customer inertia, risk from the unknown return on capex.

After earnings, sell-side analysts clustered their target prices at USD 560–640 (Citi 600, Goldman Sachs 640), averaging about USD 561, broadly in line with the neutral case. That means 564 is not an aggressive target but the market consensus—the benefit of consensus is you won't be embarrassed; the drawback is excess returns can only come from exceeding expectations.

The real asymmetry lies in the bull case: if Copilot moves from "per seat" to "per seat + usage-based," TAM is no longer capped by enterprise headcount and the valuation framework shifts. This shift only just surfaced in FY2026Q4 (customer-service usage up 4x QoQ) and is not yet enough to support a re-rating. So the current price buys the neutral case, and the bull case is a free option—which is exactly the reason to buy in tranches.
