Core view

The value of a risk list lies not in how many items it holds, but in how they are ranked. Microsoft faces five categories of risk: capex misallocation, peak AI demand, price war, regulatory break-up, and accounting policy. The first two genuinely destroy money; the last three are either slow-moving or low-probability. Only by writing them as verifiable items can they be reconciled next quarter.

Risk 1: Capex misallocation (most realistic)

Capex of RMB 115.9 billion in FY2026 and RMB 175 billion planned for FY2027 totals RMB 290 billion over two years—more than triple the sum of all acquisitions in Microsoft's history. These assets carry a 5–8 year payback, yet AI demand is only visible 2–3 years out (RPO weighted term 2.3 years).

Funding 8-year assets against 2 years of orders is a duration mismatch. The risk is not financial—Microsoft can afford it, rated AAA—but a return-on-capital risk. If demand falls in 2028, the assets do not disappear; they become underutilised depreciation, compressing operating margin from 45% to 38%.

Metrics to watch: quarterly capex, RPO term, cloud utilisation (management gives only qualitative colour on earnings calls).

Risk 2: AI demand peaks (the most damaging)

Every valuation today rests on "sustained high growth in AI demand." If, three years from now, AI turns out to be just a better search and writing assistant, and enterprises are willing to pay USD 10 per user per month rather than USD 30, Azure growth falls from 43% to 15%.

What makes this risk frightening is that it is unpredictable and cannot be hedged. Diversification offers no protection—the entire tech sector is betting on the same thing. The only lever is position size.

Validation metrics: Azure growth, Copilot seat growth, and RPO growth excluding OpenAI.

Risk 3: Price war (the most chronic)

The model layer is commoditising: open-source models now match closed-source performance within six months rather than two years, and inference prices halve every year. That feeds straight through to Azure, where customers will ask why they should pay more for the same model.

Microsoft's counter is to move the battleground from price to "compliance + capacity". That works with financial, healthcare and government customers; it fails with internet customers, who look only at unit price.

Validation metric: cloud gross margin (currently 65%). A break below 60% would signal the start of a price war. The risk builds slowly, but once it starts it is irreversible.

Risk 4: Regulatory break-up (low probability, but irreversible)

Three regulatory fronts are in play: the EU's probe into cloud licensing terms, the UK CMA's review of the Microsoft-OpenAI relationship, and the US FTC's inquiry into AI partnerships. Every outcome so far has followed a "commitments to remedy plus a small settlement" template (CISPE, about EUR 20 million).

The real risk lies in structural remedies: forcing a separation of cloud and models, or requiring Azure to offer competitors equivalent terms. Should that happen, Microsoft's AI stack would be split in two and its valuation logic would have to be rewritten. We see the probability as low (10%), since breaking up one of America's largest infrastructure companies carries too high a political cost.

Risk 5: Accounting policy (not fatal, but it affects trust)

Three things stand out: extending the depreciation period from 15 to 25 years, FY2027 capex guidance "changing" from 190 billion to 175 billion, and Copilot disclosing seats but not ARPU. None breaks any rule, but all three point the same way.

The damage from this kind of risk is not in the numbers — a rough calculation puts it at 4–5 billion less depreciation per year, about USD 0.5 of EPS — but in trust. If the company loosens another accounting estimate next quarter, the market will hit it with a "profit-quality discount", typically 2–3x P/E. Once that discount is applied, it is very hard to take back.

A reverse checklist: when none of these risks matter

If AI demand genuinely compounds at a high rate for a decade, four of the five points above resolve themselves: capex turns out to be front-running demand, price wars never materialise (supply is insufficient), regulators hold back for fear of losing competitiveness, and nobody cares about accounting policy because growth overwhelms everything.

That is what makes these companies so hard to judge—the risk list and the optimistic scenario are two sides of the same coin. You cannot buy only the optimistic scenario and sidestep the risk; the two come as a package. The only way to handle this is to price the risk through price and position sizing, not to eliminate it through judgment.

Timeline of risk: which year it breaks, and how painful

Mapping risks onto a timeline beats listing them:

  • 2H 2026–1H 2027: Depreciation accelerates as short-lived assets invested in FY2026 enter service, and operating margin may slip from 45% to 43%. The share price comes under pressure on the "growth intact, profit slower" narrative.
  • 2H 2027–1H 2028: Depreciation begins on the RMB 175 billion invested in FY2027 and total depreciation may double; over the same period RPOs come up for renegotiation, and lower renewal pricing would slow Azure growth.
  • 2028–2029: The reckoning. Either demand proves sufficient (margin holds at 42%, free cash flow returns above RMB 90 billion), or overcapacity sets in (valuation de-rates to 15x).

The point of this table: don't sell in 2027 just because "margin fell two points"—that is planned, not a surprise.

Conclusion

Conclusion: of the five risks, the first two determine whether this investment makes money; the last three determine how much. Capex misallocation and peak AI demand are two sides of the same problem, and neither resolves before 2028. Until then, only three things can be done: control position size (≤5%), buy in tranches (average down cost), and reconcile three numbers every quarter (Azure, RPO, cloud gross margin).
