Core view

Most people still picture Microsoft as it was in 2015: selling Office and Windows, collecting subscription fees, 70% gross margins, asset-light, a cash cow. That picture is out of date in 2026. What you buy today is a company spending USD 175 billion a year building data centers — half software vendor, half infrastructure provider. Its valuation model, cyclicality and sources of risk have all changed.

The old map: three assumptions about software companies

Over the past decade, investors valued Microsoft on three assumptions: first, marginal cost is close to zero—selling one more Office seat costs almost nothing; second, capex runs at about 10% of revenue—most of the money earned can be returned to shareholders; third, cyclicality is weak—enterprise IT budgets are rigid, and companies pay whether the economy is good or bad.

These three assumptions underpinned a P/E above 30x, and with it the belief that "buying at any time is never wrong." From 2019 to 2024, Microsoft's PE climbed from 25x to 35x, driven by this logic reinforcing itself.

New map: all three assumptions rewritten

First, marginal cost is no longer zero. Every additional unit of Azure compute sold requires more GPUs, more electricity and more rack space. FY2026Q4 gross margin was 67.2%, down 1.4 points year on year; cloud gross margin is 65% and still falling. Selling software carries an 80% gross margin; selling compute carries 60% — every extra dollar of compute sold dilutes the blended gross margin.

Second, capex is no longer 10% of revenue; it is 35% and heading to 53%. FY2026 cash capex was RMB 115.9 billion against revenue of RMB 331 billion; FY2027 is planned at RMB 175 billion. Free cash flow fell from RMB 74 billion (FY2025 level) to RMB 67 billion, and quarters down 23% year on year continue. The money earned no longer goes back to shareholders; it is buried in the ground and turned into racks.

Third, cyclicality has intensified. Enterprise IT budgets are rigid; AI compute demand is not. It tracks the intensity of model training and inference, and that intensity depends on whether the whole industry is willing to keep burning cash. Microsoft itself says "demand still exceeds supply" — a positive in an upcycle, but in a downcycle it becomes "overcapacity."

Why this reset is especially hard

Because both frameworks are right at the same time, and they are right on different time scales.

Over three years, it is a growth stock: Azure accelerating to 43%, RPO of RMB 678 billion, up 84% year on year, Copilot expanding from 20 million seats to 30 million. These numbers support a valuation of more than 25x.

Over ten years, it is a utility: operating margin will fall from 45% to 35%, because the fate of infrastructure is mean reversion in returns; capex will stay above 30% of revenue over the long term, and free cash flow cannot return to a state of "almost equal to net profit."

Both frameworks hold, so the market swings back and forth between 20x and 30x. The investor's job is not to pick one framework, but to be clear about how long they are holding. Hold for three years, and 25x is reasonable; hold for ten years, and 25x depends on growth being delivered, with little margin for error.

After the reset, which numbers matter

The old map looks at PE, EPS growth and buybacks. The new map looks at four numbers:

  • Capex / operating cash flow: now 74% (quarterly) and 63% (annual); this number sets the ceiling on free cash flow.
  • Depreciation / revenue: now just over 10%, potentially 18% by FY2028; this number sets the floor on margins.
  • RPO conversion rate: 30% of the RMB 678 billion is recognized within 12 months; this number determines whether orders turn into revenue.
  • Cloud gross margin: 65%; a break below 60% signals the start of a price war.

None of these four numbers appear on the traditional "value investing financial statement" checklist, but they are the truth of this company today. A cognitive reset does not mean changing the narrative; it means changing the dashboard.

A counterpoint: why the stock rose only 16% after earnings, not 40%

Some will ask: with Azure accelerating, RPO up 84%, and Copilot doubling, why did the market give such a strong earnings report only 16%? Back then, a single Nvidia earnings report could move the stock 25%.

The answer is that the market is doing the same addition. It applauds the growth (revenue +17.8%, Azure +43%) while docking points for cost (capex +70%, free cash flow -23%). The 16% is simply the net result of "growth above expectations" minus "cash flow below expectations."

This detail matters: it shows the market is not treating Microsoft as a mindless AI concept stock but as a company whose numbers have to add up. For a long-term investor, that is good news — it means there is still rationality in the price, and the bubble is not large.

Conclusion

Conclusion: treat Microsoft as a hybrid of "software + infrastructure" and value it with two sets of multiples. The software segment (Office, Dynamics, LinkedIn) deserves 25–30x; the infrastructure segment (Azure compute) deserves 15–20x. Weighted by revenue mix, the fair multiple is 22–26x—which is exactly why the base-case scenario in the working papers assigns 24x. Paying 30x means paying for the assumption that "it is still a pure software company," an assumption that no longer holds.
