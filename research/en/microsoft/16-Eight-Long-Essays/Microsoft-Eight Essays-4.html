④ Financial reality: five numbers, two of them fake — Microsoft

Core view

Microsoft's earnings look clean, but five numbers are most easily misread: revenue of USD 90.01 billion (real), net income of USD 35.77 billion (includes one-off items), operating cash flow of USD 55.4 billion (includes deferred revenue benefit), capex of USD 41 billion (includes leases), free cash flow of USD 19.6 billion (the truest measure). Understand these five numbers and you understand all of the contradictions inside this company today.

Figure one: revenue RMB 90.01 billion, up 17.8% year on year — the cleanest number of all

No room to adjust revenue: enterprises pay per contract, and revenue is recognized on delivery. Of the RMB 90.01 billion, Intelligent Cloud was RMB 39.3 billion (+32%) and Microsoft Cloud RMB 59.3 billion (+27%), with Azure at +43%.

The only caveat is FX. Azure's 43% is the rate including currency effects, while next quarter's guidance of 45% is on a constant-currency basis. Comparing the two bases gives the illusion of more acceleration—the real acceleration is 3 percentage points, not 5.

Number two: net profit RMB 35.77 billion, up 31.3% YoY — includes RMB 3.2 billion one-off

Net profit growth of 31.3% far outpaced revenue growth of 17.8%, which looks like striking operating leverage. But break it down: the RMB 3.2 billion fair value gain on the Anthropic investment contributed roughly 9 percentage points of the net profit increase. Strip that out and net profit is up about 21% year on year, a normal gap versus revenue growth (operating leverage plus tax rate benefits).

The tax rate is another hidden variable: the effective rate of 17% is below the statutory 21%, driven by R&D tax credits and the overseas structure. This tax rate is unsustainable; at 19%–20%, the same operating profit would yield 1.5 points less net profit. A three-year model needs to factor this in.

Figures three and four: operating cash flow RMB 55.4 billion vs capex RMB 41 billion

Operating cash flow of RMB 55.4 billion, up 30% YoY — impressive. But part of it is a "prepaid dividend": enterprises pay subscription fees annually, so the more new contracts signed, the more deferred revenue, and the better cash flow looks. This mechanism amplifies cash flow when growth accelerates and drags on it when growth decelerates.

Capex of RMB 41 billion, up 70% YoY, roughly two-thirds of which goes to short-lived CPU/GPU assets. Note the basis: RMB 41 billion including finance leases, RMB 35.76 billion for pure cash purchases (derived as 55.4 − 19.64). The two bases differ by RMB 5.2 billion, or 12.8% — use the former to analyze capacity, the latter to analyze cash.

Number five: free cash flow of RMB 19.6 billion, down 23% YoY — that is the real story

RMB 19.6 billion is the one number this quarter that cannot be dressed up. It is calculated as operating cash flow minus cash capital expenditure; both ends are cash, leaving no room for accounting estimates.

Full year: operating cash flow RMB 182.9 billion, cash capex RMB 115.9 billion, free cash flow RMB 67 billion. Against a market cap of RMB 3.45 trillion, that is a yield of 1.94%. And FY2027 plans to invest RMB 175 billion — if operating cash flow rises 20% to RMB 219.5 billion, free cash flow drops to just RMB 44.5 billion, down another third year on year.

That is the source of the valuation ceiling. Not insufficient profit, but profit intercepted by capex. On a DCF basis, once capex exceeds 35% of revenue, the discounted value cannot rise — no matter how bullish you are on Azure.

Two hidden numbers: depreciation and off-balance-sheet commitments

Depreciation: The company extended the depreciable life of some assets from 15 to 25 years, without quantifying the impact. Roughly assuming the affected assets account for half the depreciation base, this cuts annual depreciation by RMB 4–5 billion. Meanwhile, the RMB 115.9 billion invested in FY2026 will roll into depreciation over the next six years (about RMB 13 billion a year for the short-lived asset portion). Netting the two, total depreciation will still rise sharply in FY2027–FY2028.

Off-balance-sheet commitments: Microsoft has signed a large volume of long-term power and data-center lease contracts. These are not on the balance sheet but must be paid out of future cash flow. Microsoft cannot be judged on capex alone; look at the combined "capex + lease commitments" curve—that is the true investment intensity.

What these two figures share: neither affects how good the current income statement looks, but both affect future free cash flow. And valuation is determined by future free cash flow.

The Arithmetic of ROIC: How Capex Eats Returns

FY2026: NOPAT of about RMB 123.7 billion (operating profit RMB 149 billion × (1−17%)), invested capital of about RMB 400 billion (equity 340 + long-term debt 60), ROIC of about 31%. That number is top-tier in any industry.

After RMB 175 billion of investment in FY2027: invested capital rises to about RMB 550 billion. If NOPAT grows 15% to RMB 142.3 billion, ROIC = 25.9%. After another year (RMB 185 billion in FY2028), invested capital reaches RMB 700 billion, NOPAT RMB 163.7 billion, ROIC = 23.4%.

Roughly, RMB 170 billion of annual capex pulls ROIC from 31% toward 22%–23%, assuming profit still grows 15% a year. If profit grows only 8% (depreciation drag), ROIC falls to 19%. This is what "capital intensity changes the nature of the business" looks like in numbers—and why the multiple is 24x rather than 30x.

Conclusion

Conclusion: of the five numbers, revenue is the most real, net profit the most fake, free cash flow the most honest. Reading this report, first strip one-offs out of net profit, then switch capex to a lease-inclusive basis, and then ask one question: can FY2028 free cash flow return above RMB 80 billion? If yes, 25x is fair; if not, 20x is right.
