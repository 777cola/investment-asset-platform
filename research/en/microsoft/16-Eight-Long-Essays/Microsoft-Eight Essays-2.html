Core view

Strip away the narrative and Microsoft's business comes down to three sentences: subscription fees per head (M365), compute fees by usage (Azure) and AI surcharges per seat (Copilot). Three billing models map to three separate corporate budgets — and that is the real source of its growth.

First budget: IT operations, paid per head

M365 Commercial is priced on a per-employee, per-month basis. The appeal of this business is its extreme stability: headcount does not swing widely with the economic cycle, renewal rates have long held above 90%, and price increases do not trigger immediate churn given the high switching costs.

In FY2026Q4, Productivity and Business Processes plus More Personal Computing together came to roughly USD 50.7 billion (a rough calculation of 90.01 billion minus Intelligent Cloud's 39.3 billion), growing around 10%. The character of this business can be summed up in one sentence: not fast, but it won't fall. It is Microsoft's cash-flow foundation, and the confidence behind its willingness to invest 175 billion.

Second budget: compute, pay-per-use

Azure bills pay-as-you-go: virtual machines by the hour, storage by the GB, AI inference by the token. That revenue stream drove Intelligent Cloud to 39.3 billion in FY2026Q4, up 32% YoY, with Azure itself up 43%. Full-year Microsoft Cloud reached 214 billion.

The business is fundamentally different in nature. It has no concept of a "renewal rate," and customers can move their workloads elsewhere at any time (albeit at high cost). Gross margin is low at 65%, since it must absorb data-center and GPU costs, and growth depends on customers' own business growth rather than Microsoft's sales capability. But it is the only entry point that can capture AI demand—which is why Microsoft is willing to invest 175 billion in it.

Third budget: productivity tools, priced per seat

Copilot is priced at "USD 30/month on top of the existing seat" — the most elegant kind of pricing. No new customers, no new channels; it simply stacks on top of the existing 400 million-plus seats.

Seats reached 30 million, up from 20 million last quarter, with net additions doubling QoQ. At list price that annualises to roughly USD 10.8 billion, or 3.3% of revenue.Still too small to move growth, but it changes the answer to whether Microsoft can raise prices.Office has raised prices several times over the past decade; customers complained but renewed anyway. Now Microsoft has found a way to raise prices that still looks justified — not by raising the price of Office, but by selling something new.

How the three budgets relate to one another

The key point: these three budgets are not independent; they reinforce one another.

  • M365 customers are natural Azure customers — both are signed off by the same IT department.
  • Azure compute is what lets Copilot run, and Microsoft itself is the largest inference customer.
  • Copilot makes M365 seats more valuable, which is the justification for price increases.

Amazon and Google lack this closed loop: Amazon has compute but no applications, Google has applications but no enterprise channel. Microsoft is the only company with all three — its most underappreciated structural advantage in the AI era.

But the loop has a cost: the three businesses earn different returns on capital. M365 is asset-light at 80% gross margin; Azure is asset-heavy at 65%. As the revenue mix shifts from the former to the latter, the overall return on capital must drift lower — this is not an operating issue, it is arithmetic.

The Cost of the Closed Loop: Why a Good Structure Does Not Equal Good Returns

The closed loop sounds elegant, but it carries a side effect: it leaves Microsoft no option to "not invest."

Suppose AI demand slows in a given year. A pure cloud provider can halt expansion immediately and sit on its cash; Microsoft cannot—it still has over 400 million Office seats to serve, Copilot inference to run, and customers who would ask, "If you can't even guarantee compute, why would I hand you my identity system?" Under the closed loop it must stay ahead on capacity forever, even as returns decline.

That is why its capex ignores short-term returns. Amazon can expand on demand, Google can serve only itself, but Microsoft must satisfy both external customers and its own applications. That constraint is an advantage when demand is strong and a burden when it weakens.

Conclusion

Conclusion: this is a "three budgets plus one closed loop" business, with quality ranking in the global top five. Growth does not depend on new customers; it depends on extracting a little more from each of the three budgets — seat price increases, compute growth and AI add-ons. The risk is not demand but whether the third budget (Copilot) can shift from "price increase" to "new category" — that determines whether it trades at 25x or 35x.
