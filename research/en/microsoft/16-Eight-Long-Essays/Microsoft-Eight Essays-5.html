⑤ Industry and competition: an arms race among the big three, with the fourth player left to pick up the scraps — Microsoft

Core view

Capital across the AI supply chain is being reallocated: upstream chip and memory makers are capturing excess profit, midstream cloud providers are burning cash to lock up capacity, and downstream model companies are buying share at a loss. Microsoft sits midstream — the largest buyer on this chain, and its biggest gambler.

Industry structure: three layers of profit, three fates

Upstream (chips, memory, power) captures excess profit. Nvidia's gross margin exceeds 70%, the three HBM makers doubled prices in 2026, and power and transformers are turning scarce. Pricing power at this layer rests on "only I can supply it."

Midstream (cloud providers) earns scale profit. Gross margin is 60%–65%, but more than half of operating cash flow must be reinvested. Here pricing power rests on scale — without enough of it, there is no room to survive.

Downstream (models and applications) is polarizing. The application layer (Office, Salesforce, Adobe) posts 75%–88% gross margins and collects rent steadily; the model layer (OpenAI, Anthropic) has low or even negative gross margins and survives on financing.

Microsoft spans both the midstream and the downstream application layer, making it the only company "collecting money at both ends." That structure means it will not be strangled by upstream (though its pricing power there is weak), nor crushed by downstream commoditization (the application layer has high stickiness).

Cycle position: mid-cycle, neither peak nor trough

Three signals frame the cycle: orders (RPO RMB 678 billion, +84%), pricing (cloud gross margin 65%, down only 1–2 points) and capacity (management says demand still exceeds supply). All three point to "still trending up."

Watch investment intensity, though: Microsoft plans USD 175 billion for FY2027, equivalent to 53% of FY2026 revenue, and the four major North American cloud players are all raising 2026 capex.Historically, cloud capex has never exceeded 40% of revenue — the intensity of this round is unprecedented. The greater the intensity, the steeper the cycle top.

What signals the top? Order growth falling below 20%, capacity still coming online, and cloud gross margin breaking below 60%. At the current pace, watch these three numbers in 2028.

Competition: three rivals, three kinds of threat

Amazon is the scale threat: AWS is cheaper and reaches a broader customer base, but it has no entry point into enterprise software, so it captures new workloads rather than displacing incumbents.

Google is the structural threat: the only company that builds all three layers in-house — models (Gemini), chips (TPU) and cloud. Microsoft's AI stack sources models from OpenAI and chips from Nvidia, so it pays at both ends; Google pays at neither. If inference cost becomes the core variable, Microsoft's disadvantage compounds every year.

Oracle is the price threat: it entered via OpenAI's outsized contract and uses elastic capacity to bid up prices and win customers. Its delivery capability is questionable, but near term it is genuinely taking share.

Google is the most dangerous, because what it threatens is not share but gross margin.

Three-year outlook

Three scenarios:

First (45% probability): the three oligopolists hold steady. Azure, AWS and GCP each defend their turf, price competition stays limited and gross margins hold at 55%–60%. Microsoft keeps second place through its enterprise gateway, with revenue growing 12%–15% a year.

Second (35% probability): overcapacity and price war. Demand growth slows to 15% in 2028, capacity from all four players comes online at once, a cloud price war breaks out and gross margins fall to 45%–50%. Microsoft de-rates to 15x.

Third (20% probability): AI demand keeps exploding. Inference demand grows at a high rate for a decade, compute stays scarce long term, cloud providers become "the power grid of the AI era," margins hold steady and scale triples. Microsoft's revenue grows more than 20% a year and its valuation holds at 28–30x.

Weighting the three scenarios gives an expected fair multiple of 22–24x — the basis for our neutral valuation.

Quantifying the upstream profit pool: who is capturing AI's money

A rough breakdown of how the money along this chain is split. Microsoft's FY2026 capex is USD 115.9 billion, of which about two-thirds (roughly USD 77 billion) goes to chips, servers and networking equipment. Within that:

  • GPUs and accelerators account for about half, roughly USD 38.5 billion; Nvidia captures most of it, at gross margins above 70%.
  • HBM and memory make up about 30%–40% of GPU cost, roughly USD 12–15 billion, split among the three memory makers.
  • Server assembly, optical modules, racks and so on carry gross margins ranging from 5% to 40%.

In other words, for every USD 1 Microsoft invests, about USD 0.30–0.40 turns directly into upstream gross profit.Microsoft's own cloud gross margin is 65%—which looks high, but that is after depreciation; on a cash basis, its true return is lower than reported.

This split explains why chips and memory, not cloud providers, were the best performers in 2026.

Conclusion

Conclusion: the industry structure is settled (three oligopolists), but profit distribution is not (upstream takes too much). Microsoft holds the strongest position of the three (enterprise gateway plus application layer); its weakness is the heaviest reliance on external parties (models, chips). Investing in it amounts to betting on "AI demand persists + Microsoft defends share" — both are more likely than not, but neither is certain.
