Core view

When Nadella took over in February 2014, Microsoft's market cap stood at USD 300 billion and the consensus view was that it had missed mobile. By July 2026 the market cap is USD 3.45 trillion. A tenfold gain in twelve years came not from technological breakthroughs but from two things: converting licences into subscriptions, and moving those subscriptions onto the cloud.

The first thing he got right: bringing Office to iPad

His first controversial call after taking office in 2014 was to bring Office to the iPad—unthinkable in the Ballmer era, when the iPad was "the enemy's device." The logic was simple: Office should go wherever users are; protecting Windows would only kill Office along with it.

What matters about that decision is not the product but the culture. It signalled a shift at Microsoft from "protecting existing assets" to "serving existing customers." The later opening up of Azure, the GitHub acquisition and the OpenAI investment are all extensions of the same logic.

Second, reposition Azure from "virtual machine rental" to "an extension of enterprise IT"

Early Azure amounted to little more than "renting servers." The real turning point came when it was wired into Entra (identity), M365 (office) and Dynamics (business systems): moving to the cloud then became "upgrading the IT architecture once over" rather than "relocating servers elsewhere."

That positioning shifted Azure's customer base from "technical teams comparing prices" to "CIOs making decisions." The former look at price; the latter look at risk. Microsoft is not selling compute; it is selling "a choice that requires no one to take responsibility." Microsoft Cloud revenue of USD 214 billion in FY2026 validates this positioning.

Third: trading capital for time — the riskiest move of all

In 2019 it invested USD 1 billion in OpenAI and has since committed roughly USD 13 billion in cumulative funding; following the October 2025 restructuring it holds about 27% and secured model and IP usage rights through 2032. In 2023 it acquired Activision Blizzard for USD 68.7 billion, a deal regulators delayed by 21 months.

The two bets differ in nature. OpenAI was about buying time — no need to build models from scratch, straight into the top tier; Activision was about buying content — shoring up the gaming business.The former succeeded; the latter is still being validated.

It has now taken this playbook to its limit: USD 115.9 billion of capex in FY2026 and USD 175 billion in FY2027 to build data centers. This is buying capacity with capital — a bet that AI demand persists. Whether it pays off will not be known until 2028.

Deduction: accounting policy and information transparency

Two points stand out. First, the depreciation life was extended from 15 to 25 years in a year when capex hit a record high, and management did not quantify the impact. Second, Copilot discloses seat count only—no ARPU, no renewal rate. Selective disclosure.

Neither is a violation, but both betray the same instinct: management wants the market to see "growth," not "cost." Investors should not respond by doubting everything; they should build the costs in themselves when valuing the company—operating profit rather than net profit, cash capex rather than the lease-inclusive figure, and longer depreciation assumptions.

Succession: no Plan B, and none needed

The board has about 12 members, and Nadella also serves as chairman, with a lead independent director in place. Born in 1967, he will be 59 in 2026. The internal candidates are CTO Kevin Scott and commercial chief Althoff, but he is more likely to serve another five years.

Implications for investors: Strategic continuity over the next five years is extremely high; there is no need to pay a premium for a "leadership change," nor a discount for one. That is rare for a company worth USD 3.45 trillion — most giants are anxious about succession.

But keep one thing in mind: his record contains no "failed big bet." The 175 billion is his first true heavy wager. Whether it succeeds will determine whether he is remembered as "an excellent professional manager" or "a generational entrepreneur."

A Failed Control: Lessons from the Ballmer Era

Between 2000 and 2014, Microsoft's revenue rose from USD 23 billion to USD 86.8 billion and profit tripled, yet the stock went nowhere for fourteen years. The problem was not weak results: it poured the money it earned into projects with very low returns — the Nokia handset acquisition (USD 7.2 billion, written down by USD 7.6 billion two years later), years of losses at Bing, and the failure of Zune.

That lesson matters today. Nadella's M&A record beats Ballmer's, but the USD 175 billion of capex is the single largest concentrated bet of his tenure — closer in nature to building fabs than to buying companies. The advantage of building fabs is that they are not written down; the disadvantage is that depreciation hits every year. If returns fall short, the result will not be a one-off impairment like Nokia's; it will grind down margins over five to ten years.

For investors, the latter is harder to take: there is no clean-up day when the bad news is fully out, only the feeling every year that "next year will be better."

Conclusion

Conclusion: management 8/10 — twelve years of proven execution, two points off for transparency. Buying Microsoft is largely a bet on Nadella's judgment, and that judgment now rests on RMB 175 billion of capex. Two things to watch: whether accounting policy loosens further, and whether Copilot disclosure improves.
