## Screening: cut on scale and business model first, not valuation

The first step does only two things: cut the companies whose cloud or software revenue is insufficient to create scale effects, and cut those whose AI monetization path cannot be clearly articulated. Valuation comes last — business first, price second.

Salesforce and Adobe are out: Both are good companies (gross margins of 77% and 88%), but their size means they cannot fund the capex for AI infrastructure (USD 100 billion a year) and can only rent compute. That makes their role in the AI era that of "application-layer tenants," with bargaining power in the hands of the cloud providers. Adobe also faces a direct hit to its creative tools from generative models, with growth of only about 10%.

Oracle is out: It won a large OpenAI contract, and order elasticity is enormous, but two problems cannot be resolved — highly concentrated customers (a single customer drives the incremental business) and negative shareholder equity (caused by large long-term buybacks). A 45x PE reflects an "order narrative," not cash flow. Such companies rise the most in upcycles and fall the hardest in downcycles, which does not fit the certainty this framework demands.

## Round two: why Amazon was also eliminated

Amazon's cloud ranks first in the industry, with the lowest cost and the broadest customer base; on "business quality" it should make the final cut. Three reasons eliminate it:

- Impure profits: Overall margins are diluted by the retail business; the cloud's strength is masked by retail's thinness, and part of the 38x PE is valuation noise from retail.

- No presence at the application layer: AWS lacks an entry point like Office that "customers must open every day"; in the AI era it can monetize only by selling compute, earning hard money.

- Equally huge capex: It faces the same depreciation problem as Microsoft, but without Microsoft's software cash flow as a backstop.

Put simply: Amazon is the better cloud company; Microsoft is the better business. This framework picks businesses.

## Final pick: Microsoft vs Alphabet — where the edge lies

Both are full-stack players with "model + cloud + distribution," both valued at 24–26x, and the difference lies in structure:

Alphabet's edge is cost: in-house TPU, in-house models, self-built data centers — inference costs are inherently more than 30% lower than Microsoft's. If AI turns into a price war, Alphabet is the only one that can win it. Microsoft's edge is distribution: over 400 million M365 seats, Entra's identity system, GitHub's developers — customer acquisition cost is near zero.

The case for Microsoft is "certainty first": a price war is a probability, distribution is a fact. Alphabet's advertising business is more exposed to macro (ad budget growth slows in 2026), while Microsoft's subscription revenue runs on contracts. For a framework that demands "still here in ten years," subscriptions are more reliable than advertising.

But one thing must be clear: this is not "Microsoft is better than Alphabet," it is "Microsoft is better suited to this money than Alphabet." If the goal is to bet on the winner of an AI price war, the answer flips.

## Why it: three sentences

First: it is the only company collecting two taxes at once — compute (Azure, RMB 214 billion/year) and seats (M365 + Copilot). Companies with a single revenue source deserve a valuation discount; those with two do not.

Second: its customers cannot leave. A switching-cost score of 9 is the highest among the six — not a technological advantage but an advantage of time: two decades of enterprise IT inertia.

Third: its price has no bubble. 25.8x TTM, versus Alphabet at 24x, Amazon at 38x and Oracle at 45x — lower middle of the range. Buyers pay for certainty, and this price is fair.
