Core call: the field narrows from six to one, and the win comes down to distribution, not technology

Take the six largest companies in global cloud and software: Microsoft, Amazon, Alphabet, Oracle, Salesforce and Adobe. The first screen eliminates the three that lack scale or have unclear models, leaving Amazon, Alphabet and Microsoft for the final round.

Microsoft wins the final round not on the strength of its technology but on the broadest distribution, the steadiest cash flow and the least extreme valuation. Alphabet is a close second: it wins on cost structure but loses on "certainty of shareholder returns".

Screening: scale and business model first, valuation second

The first screen does only two things: it eliminates companies whose cloud or software revenue is too small to generate scale effects, and those whose path to AI monetisation cannot be clearly articulated. Valuation comes last — business first, price second.

Salesforce and Adobe are out: Both are good companies (gross margins of 77% and 88%), but their size means they cannot fund the capex required for AI infrastructure (USD 100 billion a year) and can only rent compute. That reduces them to "application-layer tenants" in the AI era, with bargaining power sitting with the cloud providers. Adobe also faces a direct threat to its creative tools from generative models, with growth of only about 10%.

Oracle is out: It won a large OpenAI contract and order elasticity is enormous, but two problems cannot be resolved — highly concentrated customers (a single customer drives the incremental business) and negative shareholder equity (caused by large long-term buybacks). A 45x P/E reflects an "order narrative," not cash flow. Such companies rise the most in upcycles and fall the hardest in downcycles, which does not fit the certainty this framework demands.

Round two: why Amazon was also eliminated

Amazon's cloud business ranks first in the industry, with the lowest cost and the broadest customer base; on business quality it should make the final cut. Three reasons rule it out:

  • Impure profits: group margins are diluted by retail, which masks the strength of the cloud, and part of the 38x P/E is valuation noise from retail.
  • No presence at the application layer: AWS lacks an entry point like Office that customers must open every day; in the AI era it can monetise only by selling compute, earning hard money.
  • Equally huge capex: it faces the same depreciation problem as Microsoft, but without Microsoft's software cash flow as a backstop.

Put simply: Amazon is the better cloud company; Microsoft is the better business. This framework picks businesses.

Final pick: Microsoft vs Alphabet — where the edge lies

Both are full-stack players with "model + cloud + distribution," both trade at 24–26x, and the difference is structural:

Alphabet's edge is cost: in-house TPU, in-house models, self-built data centers — inference costs are inherently more than 30% lower than Microsoft's. If AI turns into a price war, Alphabet is the only one that can win it. Microsoft's edge is distribution: over 400 million M365 seats, Entra's identity system, GitHub's developers — customer acquisition cost is near zero.

The case for Microsoft rests on "certainty first": a price war is a probability, distribution is a fact. Alphabet's advertising business is more exposed to macro (ad budget growth slows in 2026), while Microsoft's subscription revenue runs on contracts. For a framework that demands "still here in ten years," subscriptions are more reliable than advertising.

But one thing must be clear: this is not "Microsoft is better than Alphabet," it is "Microsoft is better suited to this money than Alphabet." If the goal is to bet on the winner of an AI price war, the answer flips.

Why it: three sentences

First: it is the only company collecting two taxes at once — compute (Azure, RMB 214 billion/year) and seats (M365 + Copilot). Companies with a single revenue source deserve a valuation discount; those with two do not.

Second: its customers cannot leave. A switching-cost score of 9 is the highest among the six — not a technological advantage but an advantage of time: two decades of enterprise IT inertia.

Third: its price has no bubble. 25.8x TTM, versus Alphabet at 24x, Amazon at 38x and Oracle at 45x — lower middle of the range. Buyers pay for certainty, and this price is fair.

Conclusion

Conclusion: six narrowed to two, then one of two, and the final pick is Microsoft. The four eliminated are not bad companies; they fail this framework's requirements: two lack the scale to carry AI capex, one is too customer-concentrated, and one has profits diluted by retail. The final call came down to Microsoft versus Alphabet, and we chose the name with the more stable distribution. Buy in tranches; neutral at USD 564.
