Core view

In one sentence: enterprises pay a per-seat subscription fee, pay Azure usage-based fees when they need compute, and now pay an extra USD 30 per seat for Copilot. Three charging models stacked on the same customer base — one of the best business models in the world. There is only one problem — how long the third charge (Copilot) can last.

What makes this a good business: customers don't just "like" it, they "can't do without" it

In a company of 50,000 people, employees open Outlook every day to check email, meet on Teams, and store files in SharePoint. The IT department's identity authentication runs on Entra, and permissions, audit logs and compliance reports are all generated by this system. Replacing it is not swapping out software; it means rebuilding the entire IT architecture and explaining to auditors why the change was made. No one wants to propose that kind of decision. That is the moat.

Duan Yongping would not count how many server racks Azure has. He would look at something simpler: can this company sell the same thing to the same customers and charge a little more each year? The answer is yes — FY2026 revenue of USD 331 billion, up 17.8% year on year, and Microsoft Cloud USD 214 billion, up 27%. With the customer count near saturation, growth comes from unit price and depth of usage.

What has it now turned the money printer into?

Microsoft used to be an "asset-light rent collector": selling licenses and subscriptions, with near-zero marginal cost and gross margins above 70%. Now it has an added "heavy-asset factory builder" machine: FY2026 cash capex of USD 115.9 billion, with FY2027 planned at USD 175 billion.

These two things are fundamentally different in nature. Rent collection is certain; factory building is probabilistic—money invested today takes two or three years to reveal its return. Duan Yongping would acknowledge this is necessary (no investment means no compute to sell), but he would not pay a premium for it. His gist: don't earn money from what you can't understand. The return on capital expenditure is precisely the part he "can't understand."

Is Copilot a second business model, or a second price increase?

30 million paid seats, 20 million last quarter, net additions doubled quarter over quarter. At a list price of USD 30/seat/month, that is roughly USD 10.8 billion annualized, or 3.3% of revenue. That scale shows it is no longer an experiment, but it is not yet enough to change the company's growth rate.

To judge whether this is a "price increase" or a "new product," watch one metric: renewal rate. A price increase drags the renewal rate down (customers feel it is not worth it); a new product lifts the renewal rate (customers cannot do without it). For now the company discloses only seat count, not renewal rate, and not ARPU—that is where the information gap lies. If it ever discloses a Copilot renewal rate above 90%, then this is not a price increase but a new product, and the valuation has to be redone.

Where is the ceiling: enterprise headcount, or AI call volume

The ceiling for seat-based pricing is the white-collar headcount, about 1 billion globally. Microsoft covers around 400 million, and Copilot penetration currently stands at 7.5% (30 million ÷ 400 million, rough estimate). This pool can at most triple.

The ceiling for usage-based pricing is the number of AI calls, which has no upper limit. The signal in FY2026Q4 is that usage in customer-service scenarios rose 4x quarter over quarter—if Copilot shifts from "one seat per person" to "billing per call," Microsoft's TAM changes by an order of magnitude. This is the entire basis for the bull case of USD 700.

Conclusion

Conclusion: a good business, understandable, but the current price is paying for "price hikes," not "new products." 30 million seats prove demand exists; only renewal rates and ARPU can prove it is sustainable. Wait for these two numbers, or wait for the price to fall below 430—whichever comes first, act on it.
