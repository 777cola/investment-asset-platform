Core call: a CEO who turns narrative into revenue, at a company that has started spending big

When Nadella took over in February 2014, Microsoft's market cap was USD 300 billion; today it is USD 3.45 trillion — a tenfold increase in twelve years. His success boils down to two things: turning licences into subscriptions, and moving those subscriptions onto the cloud. The rest was execution.

The capital-allocation record is equally impressive: USD 68.7 billion for Activision Blizzard, USD 26.2 billion for LinkedIn and USD 7.5 billion for GitHub — none of the three big acquisitions became an impairment black hole — and FY2026 also returned USD 43 billion to shareholders. Two marks against: extending the depreciation life from 15 years to 25 years, and tying the company's fate to a single model supplier, OpenAI.

What Nadella got right was not the technology but the charging model

Microsoft's problem in 2014 was not a lack of technology but a lack of a charging model: Windows and Office were sold as one-off licences, customers upgraded on a five-year cycle, and revenue tracked PC shipments. Nadella's first move was to push Office 365 onto every platform, iPad included; his second was to turn Azure from "virtual machine rental" into "an extension of enterprise IT."

The numbers bear this out: FY2026 revenue of USD 331 billion, Microsoft Cloud at USD 214 billion, up 27% YoY, and commercial RPO of USD 678 billion. That is not luck—it is twelve years of consistent execution. The hardest thing for a USD 3 trillion company is not finding a new story but keeping the old business compounding—Office Commercial is still raising prices and still layering on Copilot surcharges with enterprise seats near saturation, direct evidence of pricing power.

Capital allocation: three large M&A deals without a misstep, the fourth is a bet

Microsoft's M&A record is clean by tech giant standards. LinkedIn, bought for USD 26.2 billion in 2016, was criticised at the time as expensive; today it is the only enterprise-social sample. GitHub, bought for USD 7.5 billion in 2018, was an underappreciated deal — its value today lies not in code-hosting revenue but in the distribution channel formed by 225 million developers, which supplied Copilot's first users. Activision Blizzard at USD 68.7 billion was held up by regulators for 21 months before closing; the cost was time, not money.

Capital allocation changed in nature in 2026: from "buying companies" to "building factories". FY2026 cash capex is USD 115.9 billion, with FY2027 planned at USD 175 billion — close to USD 300 billion over two years, more than twice all historical M&A combined. No historical data exists to benchmark the return on this spending; it can only be inferred indirectly from RPO and Azure growth.

Two deductions: depreciation life tied to OpenAI

Depreciation life extended from 15 to 25 years, while FY2027 capex guidance "falls" to 175 billion from 190 billion (some leased and third-party capacity no longer counted). Neither move breaks any rule — both are accounting estimates and definitional choices — but they point the same way: a slightly better-looking set of statements. The amounts are small (roughly 4–5 billion less depreciation per year, about USD 0.5 in EPS); what suffers is credibility — investors dislike seeing accounting policy loosen at a capex peak.

The OpenAI tie-up is the bigger structural issue. After the October 2025 restructuring, Microsoft holds about 27% and secures model and IP rights through 2032, while OpenAI is free to choose other clouds (Oracle has already won a large contract). The implication: part of Microsoft's AI revenue is "rented exclusivity." Q4 data offers a counterpoint, though — RPO still grew 25% excluding OpenAI, showing non-frontier-model customers are providing support.

Governance and succession: no Plan B, and none needed

The board has roughly 12 members, with Nadella also serving as chairman and a lead independent director appointed. Ownership is highly dispersed and dominated by institutional investors, and no shareholder can challenge management—the upside is fast decision-making, the downside weak checks and balances. In 2024, EU and UK antitrust reviews of cloud licensing terms ended in commitments to remedy, and the CISPE settlement came to about EUR 20 million—a "pay to make it go away" outcome.

On succession, Microsoft has no public COO or anointed heir. The internal candidates are CTO Kevin Scott and commercial chief Althoff, but the more likely answer is Nadella serving another five years—he was born in 1967 and will be 59 in 2026. The implication for investors: over the next five years strategic continuity is extremely high, so there is no need either to pay a premium for "what happens if leadership changes" or to apply a discount for it.

Conclusion

Conclusion: management 8/10 — full marks on capability, marks off for transparency. Buying Microsoft is, in a sense, buying Nadella's judgment, and that judgment now carries twelve years of track record. Two things to watch: whether depreciation policy loosens further (check the accounting policy notes in the next earnings report), and whether the OpenAI relationship shifts from "exclusive" to "one of several." The first bears on earnings quality; the second on the valuation logic.
