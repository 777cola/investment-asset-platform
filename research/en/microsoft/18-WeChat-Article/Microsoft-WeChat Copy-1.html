Title and lead-in

Title: Microsoft earned USD 35.8 billion this quarter, then spent USD 41 billion

Subtitle: Azure accelerated from 40% to 43%, and AI finally proved it can make money. But the bill has arrived too.

Look at three numbers first.

After the close on July 29, Microsoft reported FY2026Q4 results. Three numbers matter most:

Revenue USD 90.01 billion, up 17.8% year on year. Net income USD 35.77 billion, up 31.3% year on year. Azure growth accelerated from 40% to 43%, with guidance of 45% for the next quarter.

The stock gapped up 16% the next day and rose 19.5% over two sessions. The market put its money where its mouth is: Microsoft can actually monetize AI.

Flame: the order book says more than revenue

Beyond revenue, another number is underappreciated: commercial remaining performance obligations (RPO) of USD 678 billion, up 84% year over year, an increase of USD 51 billion in a single quarter.

This equates to "contracted but not yet recognized revenue." More critically: nearly all of the incremental growth came from customers other than OpenAI.

Why does this matter? Because over the past two years, the market's biggest question has been — is Microsoft's AI revenue just OpenAI's money passed through? Now that orders from non-OpenAI customers are underpinning the book, that concern can largely be set aside. Excluding OpenAI, RPO still grew 25% year over year.

Seawater: the RMB 41 billion bill

In the same quarter, capex was USD 41 billion, up 70% year on year. About two-thirds of that went into CPUs and GPUs — kit that becomes obsolete in six years.

The result is free cash flow: USD 19.6 billion, down 23% year on year. Full-year USD 67 billion against a market cap of USD 3.45 trillion, a yield of 1.94% — lower than the ten-year US Treasury.

The company says it will invest another USD 175 billion in FY2027. What does that number mean? It equals 53% of FY2026 full-year revenue.

So the truth of this quarter is not "Microsoft is raking it in" but "the money Microsoft earns goes straight into the ground and comes back as server racks." This is not a financial risk; Microsoft can afford it. It is a valuation constraint: until capex peaks, its cash flow cannot support a P/E above 30x.

A quick note: don't take that +31% net profit at face value

Net profit rose 31.3% year on year, far outpacing the 17.8% revenue growth. That looks impressive, but it includes a USD 3.2 billion investment gain (appreciation in the value of its Anthropic stake), which is one-off.

Strip that out and net profit grows about 21% year on year. That is the real growth rate of the core business — still strong, but less spectacular.

Similarly, the report contains a quiet adjustment: the depreciation period was extended from 15 years to 25 years. The company did not disclose the financial impact; by conventional estimates, this cuts annual depreciation by RMB 4–5 billion and adds about USD 0.5 to EPS. No extra cash was generated — the books simply look a little better.

By the way, Copilot: 30 million seats, and then what?

This is the most overhyped and most underestimated number of the quarter.

30 million paid seats, up from 20 million last quarter, with net additions doubling quarter over quarter. At a rough list price of USD 30 per seat per month, that is USD 10.8 billion in annualized revenue, or 3.3% of full-year revenue.

The scale shows it is no longer an experiment, but it is not yet enough to change the growth rate. The real question is: does this make Office more expensive, or does it make Office better?

The former is a price increase, capped by the growth rate of enterprise IT budgets; the latter is a new business, capped by AI call volume. The company disclosed neither ARPU nor renewal rates—until those two numbers are out, every valuation of Copilot is a guess.

So is it expensive now?

USD 464.72, 25.8x P/E, 23x FY2027E. For a company that can still grow EPS by 20%, not expensive, but not cheap either.

Three scenarios: bear case 400 (-14%), base case 564 (+21%), bull case 700 (+51%). Reward-to-risk 1.5 to 1.

Sell-side raised targets across the board post-earnings (Citi 600, Goldman Sachs 640), average 561 — roughly in line with the base case. That makes 564 a consensus number, not an aggressive target. The upside of consensus is you won't be embarrassed; the downside is excess returns can only come from beating expectations.

Someone asked: with such a good earnings report, why did it rise only 16%

Because the market is doing the math too. Revenue beat earns credit; capex and cash flow lose credit. Net the two out, and 16% is the net result.

Compare: in 2023–2024, a single Nvidia earnings report could move the stock 25%, because it was a pure beneficiary—the shovel seller doesn't care who strikes gold. Microsoft is different: it sells shovels (Azure), digs for gold (Copilot), and buys shovels (RMB 175 billion in capex). With three identities stacked together, the market will not value it on one-sided optimism.

That is not a bad thing. There is still rationality in the price, which means there is no bubble.

Finally, three definitions

First, the RMB 41.0 billion capex figure includes finance leases; pure cash spending is RMB 35.76 billion, a RMB 5.2 billion gap. Second, the RMB 35.77 billion net profit I cited includes RMB 3.2 billion of one-off investment gains; strip that out and it is RMB 33.1 billion. Third, the 25.8x P/E is based on GAAP net profit; on the ex-one-off basis it comes to about 27x.

The basis does not matter; what matters is not mixing them. The same set of financial statements can tell two different stories depending on the basis — which is why, when reading financials, the first question to ask is "how was this number calculated?"

One-line conclusion

Buy, but don't buy all at once.

Open one-third of the position at the current price, double it if the stock falls back below 430, and stop chasing once it rises above 560. The logic is simple: the certainty of this business justifies paying 25x, but the RMB 175 billion bill has not been settled yet, and the price embeds no discount.

Buy it because in ten years enterprises will still need Office and will still rent compute; buy in tranches because neither you nor I know whether AI demand in 2028 will be as large as everyone assumes today.

Earn money from certainty, not from imagination. That is the only thing I am sure of.
