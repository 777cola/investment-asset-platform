Earnings Deep Dive (Duan Yongping) — Microsoft

First look at whether this business makes money, then look at what it spent money buying.

Core view

Duan Yongping reads financial reports in a different order from everyone else: gross margin first, then cash flow, and capital expenditure last. The reasoning: gross margin tells you whether it is a good business, cash flow tells you whether the profit is real, and capital expenditure tells you where management is spending the money. Three looks, and Microsoft is "gross margin down slightly but still a good business, with cash flow spent on itself."

Step 1: gross margin 67.2%, a good business confirmed

A 67.2% gross margin is low for a software company (Adobe 88%, Oracle 70%, Salesforce 77%) and high for a hardware company. That position alone shows Microsoft is no longer a pure software company — it has RMB 39.3 billion in intelligent cloud revenue, selling racks, power and GPU time.

He does not mind the 1.4-point gross margin decline, because gross profit is still growing (+15.4%). What he minds is "why it declined": if customers are pressing prices down, competition is worsening; if the product mix is shifting, it is a deliberate choice. Microsoft is the latter — Azure's share is rising, and Azure's gross margin is inherently lower than Office's. That judgment decides whether this is "the moat being eroded" or "switching to a wider but shallower river".

Step two: operating cash flow is 1.55x net profit

Operating cash flow of RMB 55.4 billion against net profit of RMB 35.77 billion — a ratio of 155%. He likes this number: it shows there is no padding in the profit, depreciation and amortization depress book profit, and actual cash collected is higher. If a company earned RMB 10 billion in net profit but generated only RMB 6 billion in operating cash flow, he would immediately suspect receivables or revenue recognition; Microsoft is the reverse.

The prepayment model shows up here too: enterprises pay subscription fees annually, cash arrives first and the service follows, so operating cash flow naturally leads profit. This is one of the financial hallmarks of a good business, and the reason it can spend RMB 41 billion on capex while still retaining RMB 19.6 billion of free cash flow.

Step 3: capex of RMB 41 billion — the answer is on paper

RMB 41 billion in a single quarter, RMB 115.9 billion for the full year, RMB 175 billion planned for next year. Put these three numbers together and the message is clear: Microsoft will pour nearly all of its free cash flow into data centers over the next two years. That is why free cash flow fell from RMB 25.6 billion to RMB 19.6 billion, down 23% year on year.

His stance will be "I understand, but I won't pay up." He understands because without the spending there is no compute capacity to sell; he won't pay up because this business has shifted from "collecting rent" to "building factories," and the nature of the risk has changed. One of his favorite sayings: only make money on what you understand. The return on this capex won't be known until 2028, so he is only willing to buy when it is cheap.

Step four: Copilot's 30 million seats — a price increase or a new product

The most telling number in the quarterly report is this: 30 million paid seats, up from 20 million last quarter. At list price, that annualizes to roughly USD 10.8 billion. The question he cares about is not the seat count, but whether customers keep using what they bought.

A price increase looks like this: customers buy, use little, and cut back at renewal the following year. A new product looks like this: customers buy, use more and more, and add seats the following year. The company did not disclose the renewal rate, which leaves the question unresolved. He will file this under "to watch," not under "reasons to buy."

Conclusion

How his conclusion would read: a good business at a fair price; wait for a cheaper entry. Gross margin and cash flow prove the quality of the business; capex proves management is betting on the future; Copilot proves demand exists but not that it is sustainable. Put the three together: buy a little, add on dips.
