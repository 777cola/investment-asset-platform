Earnings Deep Dive (Buffett) — Microsoft

Capital intensity, ROIC and shareholder returns: three lines
Research date 2026/07/31  ·  Price $464.72 (+3.02%)  ·  Market cap About USD 3.45 trillion  ·  PE(TTM，FY2026) Approximately 25.8x
← Back to the hub

Core view

Buffett reads financial statements for one purpose: to judge how much capital the business must put in to sustain growth. Microsoft's answer: more and more. Twelve years ago it was an asset-light company; today it is an infrastructure company that must invest over USD 100 billion a year. That shift determines the multiple he assigns.

Capital intensity: from light to heavy over a decade

FY2026 cash capex was RMB 115.9 billion against revenue of RMB 331 billion, a ratio of 35%. Five years ago that ratio was around 12%; it has tripled in five years, while revenue only slightly more than doubled. In other words, every extra dollar of revenue now requires rapidly rising capital investment.

FY2027 is planned at RMB 175 billion. If revenue grows 15% to RMB 380.7 billion, capex would be 46% of revenue. The similar businesses he has seen are railways and utilities—both good businesses, but both command only 15x, because the money earned must be reinvested.

ROIC: A Good Number Being Diluted

On NOPAT of about RMB 123.7 billion and invested capital of about RMB 400 billion, ROIC is about 31%. That is a very good number, far above the 8%–9% cost of capital.

But the denominator is growing fast: after RMB 175 billion of investment in FY2027, invested capital will reach about RMB 550 billion. If NOPAT rises 15% to RMB 142.3 billion, ROIC falls to 26%. Two more years of investment and ROIC will be around 20%. Still excellent, but no longer the 31% of an "asset-light software" business. His valuation multiple will track this number: 30% ROIC gets 28x, 20% ROIC gets only 20x.

Shareholder returns: RMB 43 billion, small relative to market cap

FY2026 total shareholder return was RMB 43 billion (dividends about RMB 25.8 billion, buybacks about RMB 17.2 billion), a 1.25% yield on a market cap of RMB 3.45 trillion. The dividend yield is about 0.75% (annual dividend around USD 3.5), below the 10-year Treasury.

He dislikes this structure: if a company's free cash flow is eaten by capex, buybacks can only be symbolic and dividends cannot be raised. He prefers See's Candies-type businesses that "require no reinvestment and can distribute all cash." Microsoft is not that; Microsoft is a business that "must invest to avoid being displaced."

How he would value it: owner earnings, not net profit

He would use "owner earnings" = net income + depreciation and amortization − maintenance capital expenditure. The trouble is that Microsoft does not disclose how much of its capex is maintenance versus expansion.

A rough breakdown: assume maintenance capex equals depreciation (about RMB 35 billion), then owner earnings ≈ RMB 133.7 billion and market cap/owner earnings = 25.8x — the same as the PE. If maintenance capex is assumed to be 1.5x depreciation (because capacity is being expanded), owner earnings fall to RMB 116.2 billion and the multiple rises to 29.7x. The two assumptions differ by 15% — that is the concrete valuation price of "unknown return on capex."

Conclusion

His conclusion: a good company, but capital intensity has changed its nature. Buying at 25.8x a company whose ROIC is sliding from 31% to 20% and whose free cash flow yield is 1.9% is not cheap. He would buy, sizing the position so that "even if depreciation blows up in 2028, the portfolio is unaffected" — for him, roughly 2%–3%.
