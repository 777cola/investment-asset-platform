# JD.com（9618.HK）— Duan Yongping Supplement

- Research date：2026/10/26
- Price：99.4 HKD
- Market cap：Approximately HKD 280 billion

## Notes

This scenario-based Q&A applies Duan Yongping's publicly stated investment principles to JD.com — benfen, equanimity, a stop-doing list, buying assets rather than earnings, and investing only in what you understand. It is not a statement by him. All figures are drawn from JD.com's 2026Q2 results announcement and the 2026/10/26 close: cash and short-term investments of RMB 235.1 billion, a market cap of about HKD 280 billion, net cash per share of about HKD 91, new-business losses of RMB 9.85 billion, and food-delivery share falling from 31% to 7%–10%.

## Q: Is this a good business?

Not good. Retail is a tough business—I've been saying this for twenty years. Look at its 4.6% retail operating margin, and that was achieved only after cutting marketing spend by 24.8%—this is "profit saved," not "profit earned." Earned profit is sustainable; saved profit has a ceiling.

What makes a good business? One where I can raise prices and customers still stay. Moutai can raise prices. Apple can raise prices. Can JD.com? The moment it raises prices, users go to Pinduoduo. So this is a business that survives on efficiency and scale, not on pricing power. Tough, but survivable—just don't expect it to surprise you.

## Q: Will JD.com still be around in ten years?

On this point I am not worried. JD holds two things no one can take away: first, more than 1,500 warehouses and a 900,000-strong fulfilment force; second, the habit of buying big-ticket items on JD. Both took twenty years to build — money cannot buy time.

But "still here" and "better than now" are two different things. In ten years it may still be the best place to buy home appliances and 3C, except that market's growth rate is in the single digits. It is like a very good grocery store: it will never collapse, but it will never become a chain either. Do you want "won't collapse" or "will get bigger"? Settle that first, then talk about price.

What I am less sure about is instant retail. If 30-minute delivery really becomes infrastructure, JD's "next-day delivery" will go from fast to slow. I can see the direction of that change, but not the speed, and not whether JD can hold its ground in it — judging by the past two years' results, it cannot.

## Q: Can you understand it?

Retail is the easiest business to understand: there is no black box. What you pay for inventory, what you sell it for, how much stock sits in the warehouse, how many days of payment terms — it is all in the financial statements. JD.com gives you one thing more: segment data, disclosing retail, logistics and new business separately, down to operating profit.

The only thing I cannot calculate accurately is profit. I can estimate revenue, but not profit, because profit depends on how much management chooses to subsidise in a given quarter. When a company's profit is determined by management's willingness rather than by the business itself, that is my least favourite kind. So when I bought it, I was buying assets, not profit — assets are certain, profit is not.

## Q: At what price would you buy?

Quick maths: market cap HKD 280 billion against cash and short-term investments of RMB 235.1 billion — roughly 90% of market cap. Put another way, of the HKD 99.4 you pay, HKD 91 is cash; the remaining HKD 8 buys you retail, logistics and food delivery plus 600 million users.

That price is already cheap. But cheap alone is no reason to buy — "cheap and won't get worse" is. The problem is that "will it get worse" has no answer: RMB 40 billion poured into food delivery over two years, share falling from 31% to 7% — who can guarantee there won't be a next time?

So my answer is blunt: below HKD 90 I start looking, below HKD 80 I run the numbers properly, and if it is still burning cash then, I don't run them at all. One other condition matters more than price — it has to start returning money to shareholders, whether through buybacks or dividends. A company sitting on more than RMB 200 billion and not returning it to shareholders is just a number, however cheap the price.

## Q: What is the biggest fear?

What I fear most is not Pinduoduo, nor Meituan, but management opening another front. Pinduoduo takes the price-sensitive users, Meituan the immediate-demand users; both happen gradually, and I have time to react. But a single decision can burn two years of cash flow overnight, and to that I cannot react in time.

That is also why I look at the people first when I look at a company. Doing business is like being a person: integrity matters most. JD.com is upright on authentic goods, social insurance and after-sales service, and I respect that. But integrity is not the same as knowing how to run the numbers — putting RMB 40 billion into a market where it has no edge is not integrity, it is spite.

There is one more thing I fear: buying just because it is cheap. People lose discipline most easily in the face of cheapness. So I set myself a rule — first answer "will it turn bad"; when the answer is "I don't know", I don't buy, no matter how cheap.

## Q: If you could give just one sentence of advice?

Put it on the watchlist; don't rush to act. This company isn't going anywhere — it has RMB 235.1 billion in cash and generates RMB 30 billion of free cash flow a year, and in five years it will most likely still be here. The only mistake you can make is forgiving it on its behalf before it has stopped making mistakes itself.

Wait for two things: the numbers (Q3 retail revenue turning positive, food-delivery losses narrowing to within RMB 8 billion) and behaviour (no new battlefronts, and the start of cash returns to shareholders). When both the numbers and the behaviour arrive, price naturally stops mattering; if one is missing, don't touch it no matter how low the price. That is equanimity.
