Cognitive Reset: Dismantling Three Illusions About JD — JD.com

A 7x P/E is not free money, food delivery is not a second growth curve, and logistics does not justify a premium
Research date 2026/10/26  ·  Price HK$99.40 (-0.60%)  ·  Market cap Approximately HKD 280 billion  ·  PE (2026E adjusted) Approximately 7.5x

Core view

Three judgments about JD.com circulate in the market that sound right but in fact cost investors dearly. They lead investors to overestimate how cheap the stock is, overestimate its growth and overestimate its moat. Strip away these three illusions and what remains is a JD.com that can genuinely be valued.

Myth 1: A 7x PE is a free lunch

At 7.5x adjusted P/E, with cash on the books equal to 90% of market cap, it looks like a free lunch. But the maths has two holes. First, the cash is gross: the RMB 235.1 billion includes restricted cash (platform merchant deposits and the like) and is not net of interest-bearing debt, so the truly available money is smaller than it appears. Second, the cash is meant to be spent: roughly RMB 40 billion went into food delivery over the past two years, and a repeat would cut cash from RMB 235.1 billion to RMB 150 billion, coverage from 90% to 60%, and turn the 7.5x P/E into 12x.

The right framing, then, is this: at 7.5x P/E you are buying a promise that management will stop burning money, and that promise has no track record of being kept. Cheap is real; a free lunch is not.

Myth two: food delivery is the second curve

That judgment was mainstream in 2025; by 2026 the data has refuted it. There is only one test: did food delivery's high frequency bring users back to retail? The answer is no — delivery order volume share fell from a peak of 31% to 7%–10%, while JD Retail revenue fell 4.7% and the group 2.9% over the same period.

The deeper reason is that the infrastructure is not shared. Instant retail's system is "3 kilometers, 30 minutes, local merchants"; JD's system is "national warehouses, next-day delivery." The two systems are not shared, so users are not shared. The only player in the industry to achieve cross-category migration is Meituan (food delivery → in-store → flash shopping), because all three share the same 3-kilometer infrastructure. JD lacks this condition.

Myth three: logistics is a moat, so it deserves a premium

The logistics network is arguably JD's most defensible asset: over 1,500 warehouses, 900,000 ecosystem employees, 211 same-day delivery and a thousand autonomous delivery vehicles. But a moat's value lies not in having it but in whether you can charge for it. JD Logistics Q2 revenue was RMB 64.1 billion, +24.3%, with an operating margin of only 3.5%.

A moat that delivers only a 3.5% margin is of limited value to shareholders: it ensures JD can offer an experience others cannot, but it has not converted that experience into pricing power. Hence JD should trade at 8–11x, not 20x. Moat value = moat width × monetisation coefficient; JD scores 9 on the first and 3 on the second.

Beyond the hallucination: three real questions

Dismantling the illusion is not about turning bearish; it is about asking the right questions. Three questions matter: Will this company's cash return to shareholders? How much longer can its core categories hold? Where will management place its next bet?

The first is answered by the capital allocation record: an annual dividend and a USD 3 billion buyback plan since 2024, but against RMB 235.1 billion in cash the proportion is too low. The second is answered by the category data: 3C appliances at -11.8% is cyclical; daily necessities at +5.6% is evidence of erosion. The third has no answer and can only be observed—and that is precisely its most expensive uncertainty.

Together the three form an actionable framework: cash attribution sets the valuation floor, category defence the valuation centre, and the next bet the tail risk. All three point to 2027, so this report has a shelf life of four to six quarters, not ten years.

Under what circumstances does a hallucination become fact?

The three illusions are not permanently wrong; they are simply wrong right now. If three conditions materialise, the illusions will turn into facts in sequence: cash starts being used for buybacks (illusion one holds), food delivery's quarterly loss falls below RMB 5 billion and retail turns profitable (illusion two partially holds), and the share of external revenue in logistics exceeds 60% with margins rising to 5% (illusion three holds). None of these three conditions can be waited out; all require management action.

The test can be reduced to one sentence: watch whether it spends money on itself or on shareholders. Spend on shareholders, and all three illusions become reality together; spend on itself, and all three illusions become more expensive together.

Conclusion

Strip away the three illusions and JD.com's true picture emerges: a grind of a business whose margin is dictated by expenses, a moat that commands no premium, a failed expansion, and a cheap price that is cheap for a reason. Not sexy, but valuation-ready.
