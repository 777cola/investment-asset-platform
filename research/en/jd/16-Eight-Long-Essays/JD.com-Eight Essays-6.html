Management: the cost-cutter and the bold bettor — JD.com

Two people, two logics, one decision structure that cannot be vetoed
Research date 2026/10/26  ·  Price HK$99.40 (-0.60%)  ·  Market cap Approximately HKD 280 billion  ·  PE (2026E adjusted) Approximately 7.5x

Core view

JD.com's management must be assessed on two levels: the execution layer (Xu Ran) is A-grade, the strategy layer (Richard Liu) is D-grade. This is not a judgment of ability but of results—cost-saving has been taken to its limit, spending decisions have been badly wrong, and no one in the organization can overrule the latter.

Execution layer: cut costs to the limit

Xu Ran took over as CEO in May 2023. She comes from a finance background, having been an audit partner at PwC, and served as JD's CFO from 2019. Her track record is concrete: Q2 marketing expense -24.8%, R&D +37.7%, operating cash flow +54.5%, free cash flow +44.6%, and trailing 12-month free cash flow rising from RMB 10.1 billion to RMB 31.4 billion.

The nature of these moves needs to be seen clearly: this is the optimal solution for a company in contraction—preserve cash, preserve core capabilities, cut customer-acquisition spending. But it is not a growth solution. A CFO-turned-CEO is an asset during contraction, but not necessarily during expansion.

Strategic level: the RMB 40 billion tuition fee

The 2025 decision to go all-in on food delivery appears from public information to have been founder-led. The logic sounds sound: food delivery is high-frequency and can bring users back to retail. But the execution environment was this: JD.com had no rider network, no merchant base and no subsidy advantage, and its rivals were Meituan and Taobao Shangou, which had already pushed density to the limit.

After roughly RMB 40 billion was invested over two years, its share fell from 31% to 7%–10%, and retail revenue slipped 4.7%. This is not bad luck; it is moving against the grain of someone else's economies-of-scale curve. Worse is the sunk cost: two years in, exiting means admitting failure, and continuing to invest means continuing to bleed.

Governance: Why no one can call a halt

JD.com uses a dual-class share structure: each Class B share carries 20 votes, and the founder controls more than 70% of voting power with roughly 11%–13% of equity. When judgments are right, this structure safeguards long-termism; when they are wrong, it amplifies risk—outside shareholders have no substantive veto over major capital allocation.

This is why "management becoming rational" cannot be a base-case assumption. At companies with dispersed ownership, poor performance gets the CEO replaced; at JD.com, strategic missteps only get the executors replaced. So course correction can only be tracked through behavior: whether the food-delivery team is downsized, whether it exits cities, whether new investment is announced in Q4.

The two-column ledger of capital allocation: what is saved and what is spent

Split 2024–2026 capital allocation into two columns and the contrast is stark. The saving column is an A: marketing spend cut 24.8% a year, capex compression lifted free cash flow from RMB 10.1 billion to RMB 31.4 billion (trailing 12 months), while the cash balance rose from RMB 225.4 billion to RMB 235.1 billion. The spending column is a D: RMB 40 billion poured into food delivery, buying a 7%–10% share and a business losing close to RMB 40 billion a year.

Used for buybacks, that money would retire about 15% of the shares at a HKD 280 billion market cap; used for dividends, it equals two years of net profit. To shareholders, the same RMB 40 billion is a certain return in a buyback and a certain loss in food delivery.

The gap between the two columns says one thing: this company does not lack execution, it lacks the discipline of not doing. Capital allocation is, at its core, deciding what not to do—which is why Buffett says the CEO's most important job is capital allocation.

If Richard Liu Retires

This is a scenario that must be considered but cannot be used as an investment rationale. If he retires, JD.com would lose its sole strategic decision-maker, and two outcomes could emerge in the short term: first, a professional manager takes over, capital allocation turns conservative (more buybacks, less investment), and the valuation discount narrows; second, internal power fragments, business lines operate independently, and efficiency declines.

Judging by Xu Ran's capabilities, the first is more likely—her style prioritizes financial discipline, which is good for shareholders. But it also means JD.com would become a purely harvest-oriented company, losing any possibility of a second growth curve. For a stock at 7.5x PE, this is positive; for investors expecting growth, it is negative.

Conclusion

Management assessment: execution A, decision-making D, correction absent. The investment implication: do not assume management will improve on its own; wait for them to prove it through actions. This is also the basis for the score of 6 on the fourth of the six gates and 4 on the sixth.
