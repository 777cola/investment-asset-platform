Core view

Analysis has to end in execution. Why HKD 90

HKD 90 × 2.818 billion shares = HKD 253.6 billion ≈ RMB 232.6 billion, a 101% coverage ratio against RMB 235.1 billion in cash.In other words, HKD 90 is where the share price equals net cash per share—at that level, buying the stock means getting the retail, logistics and food delivery businesses for free.

This is not precise science; it is the dividing line between psychology and odds. At HKD 99.4, you pay HKD 8 per share for these three businesses; at HKD 90, you pay HKD 0. Same company, same financial report, completely different odds.

Why these two event indicators

"Retail revenue returning to growth" is the swing factor for revenue elasticity; without it, the HKD 125 neutral-case valuation does not hold. "Food delivery loss ≤RMB 8 billion" sets the cash burn rate; without it, the RMB 235.1 billion cash buffer is eroded by RMB 40 billion a year.

Both metrics can be verified within five weeks (Q3 results in mid-November, Double 11 close on 11/14). Indicators that can be verified quickly take priority over judgments that require long-term observation—this is part of discipline.

Why set a logic-based stop rather than a price-based stop?

If the stock falls from 90 to 75 after purchase while cash remains RMB 235.1 billion, that is a better price, not a reason to stop out (HKD 75 implies cash coverage of 121%). A price-based stop-loss on an asset-type company will wash you out at the cheapest point.

The real stop-loss is thesis failure: cash starts draining, the company opens another front, or free cash flow turns negative. If any one of these three occurs, exit regardless of price—because at that point you are not holding a "cheap asset" but a "melting asset."

How to use: avoiding self-deception

Every decision framework ultimately breaks down in execution, and nowhere more so than in the act of "waiting." Investors deceive themselves in two ways: first, mistaking "cheap" for "opportunity"—buying on a 7.5x P/E without asking why it is cheap; second, mistaking "waiting" for "giving up"—chasing a 15% rally on the grounds that "missing out would cost more."

The defence against both is to write the conditions in an indisputable form: HKD 90 is a number, dual compliance in Q3 is a number, a new investment announcement is a fact. None of the three is ambiguous, so there is no room for interpretation.

One further self-imposed constraint: this report is valid until the Q3 results are released (mid-November). If Q3 data arrives and neither condition is met, rewrite the report and reissue the rating rather than "maintain observation"—"maintain" is the most abused action; it makes people think they are tracking when in fact they are stalling.

When should this report be overturned?

Every report has conditions under which it fails, and stating them clearly matters more than writing elegantly. Four developments would force us to overturn and rewrite this report: (1) Q3 data show retail revenue turning positive and food-delivery losses at ≤RMB 8 billion (upgrade from "Watch" to "Buy"); (2) new large-scale investment appears (downgrade to "Avoid"); (3) cash balance falls more than RMB 20 billion in a single quarter (downgrade to "Avoid"); (4) major equity or governance changes occur, such as dual-class share reform or founder exit (reassess the valuation framework).

The first is an upside invalidation; the latter three are downside invalidations. Stating clearly "what would prove me wrong" is the only evidence that a call is disciplined. Without it, a report is essentially just packaging for an opinion.

Conclusion

Decision framework: wait, but stay close. Two triggers — HKD 90 or a Q3 double hit — should resolve within five weeks, with three thesis stop-losses on standby. At HKD 99.4, 7.5x adjusted P/E and cash at 90% of market cap, the price isn't bad, but it isn't good enough to pay for management's lack of discipline.
