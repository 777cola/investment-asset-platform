Quality Screen — JD.com

JD.com (9618.HK) | 02 · Quality Screen
Research date 2026/10/26  ·  Price HK$99.40 (-0.60%)  ·  Market cap Approximately HKD 280 billion  ·  PE (2026E adjusted) Approximately 7.5x
← Back to the hub

Core thesis

Three of seven hard criteria pass (operating cash flow quality, free cash flow, net cash), one awaits verification (shareholder returns), and three fail (revenue growth, operating margin, net margin). All three failures sit on the margin side, and margin is precisely the industry attribute of the retail business, not an illness specific to JD.com. Only one failure truly falls outside industry attributes and is truly fatal: negative revenue growth of 2.9%. The conclusion of the negative screen is not elimination but downgrade — treat it as a "cash flow asset" rather than a "growth stock", and the valuation method must change accordingly.

Which three passed, and how much substance is there

Point one: cash flow quality at 5.3x. Operating cash flow of RMB 37.8 billion against net profit attributable to shareholders of RMB 7.1 billion — this multiple is typical of the self-operated retail model: collect first, pay later, and the payment cycle itself generates cash. Part of the 5.3x is seasonal (Q2 is the 618 stock-up season, payables rise) and cannot simply be annualized. But it has held above 3x for several consecutive quarters, showing profit is real money, not receivables.

Point two: free cash flow of RMB 31.8 billion, up 44.6% year on year. The harder metric is the trailing 12-month figure of RMB 31.4 billion versus RMB 10.1 billion a year earlier — a threefold increase. This is JD's most solid point right now: a company with negative revenue growth tripled its free cash flow, showing it is cutting investment and recovering capital.But note that this "improvement" is itself a defensive move, not an offensive result.

Point three: net cash. RMB 235.1 billion in cash and short-term investments against a market cap of RMB 256.9 billion — a coverage ratio of 91.5%. This is extremely rare among large caps in both the A-share and Hong Kong markets — most retailers' cash is there for working capital, but JD's cash has reached "market-cap-scale assets".

Three negatives: two are industry traits, one is a real problem

The 1.3% operating margin and 2.1% net margin necessarily fail the 10% and 8% thresholds. But a distinction is needed: no global self-operated retailer clears this threshold—Costco's operating margin has long run at 3%–4%, Amazon's retail business is lower still, and Walmart sits around 4%. Screening retailers on a 10% operating margin screens out the entire industry. Failing these two tests means "lacks the characteristics of a high-margin business," not "the company has a problem."

Revenue -2.9% is different. This is not an industry attribute—total retail sales were still growing over the same period, and Pinduoduo's growth rate was above JD's. It is JD's own problem: electronics and home appliances -11.8% (high base from the national subsidy), product revenue -5.4%, retail segment -4.7%. The subsidy rollback explains part of it, not all of it, because general merchandise grew only 5.6%—the categories that should have grown faster did not fill the gap.

The 2.1% net margin carries another hidden risk: this figure is extremely sensitive to subsidies. Q2 operating profit was RMB 4.5 billion; if food delivery quarterly losses widen by another RMB 4.5 billion, the group returns to breakeven. A low net margin means no room for error.

The one still to be verified: shareholder returns

Criterion seven is marked "to be verified" only because there is no explicit dividend commitment. JD's cash pile has reached the stage where it must answer "whose money is this": RMB 235.1 billion in cash and short-term investments, which at a 30% payout ratio would imply a dividend yield of about 4% on the current market cap; if used entirely for buybacks, it could theoretically repurchase ninety percent of the market cap. Neither has yet been institutionalized as a committed ratio.

Why this matters: for a company that does not grow and does not commit to dividends, the cash on its books creates no value for shareholders—only optionality for management. And management has just used that optionality to fight a two-year food-delivery war. Criterion seven does not pass; its "to be verified" status is in fact the core risk of this entire report.

But is it fatal: a tiered judgment

Rank the four failed criteria by severity: negative revenue growth (fatal, but with a clear repair path), low net margin (structural; cost control has hit its limit), low operating margin (industry trait, not grounds for elimination), unclear shareholder returns (a governance issue that can be offset by a valuation discount).

So the conclusion is not "eliminate" but "switch valuation language." Under a growth-stock framework, JD.com passes three of seven criteria and should be eliminated; under a cash-flow-asset framework, it has RMB 31.4 billion in free cash flow, RMB 235.1 billion in net cash and a market cap of RMB 256.9 billion — equivalent to paying RMB 21.8 billion for a machine that generates over RMB 30 billion of free cash flow a year, provided that machine is no longer deployed into a food-delivery war.

Conclusion

Three of seven criteria pass, one awaits verification, three fail. Two of the three failures are inherent to the retail industry; only one is a genuine ailment: revenue declined 2.9%. The adverse-screen treatment of JD.com is a downgrade, not an elimination—from "growth stock" to "cash-flow asset," with valuation shifting from PE to free cash flow and net cash.

This downgrade yields two actionable judgments: first, 7.5x adjusted PE is too expensive for a growth stock and very cheap for a cash-flow asset, indicating the market has already priced in the downgrade; second, once revenue returns to positive growth, the market will re-rate it as a growth stock—that is a call option to the upside. Trigger to watch: Q3 retail revenue turning positive.
