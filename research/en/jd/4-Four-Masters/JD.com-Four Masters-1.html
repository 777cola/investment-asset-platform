Four Masters — JD.com

Core thesis

Four investors, four questions, one conclusion. Duan Yongping asks whether the business is worth holding for the long term: it is understandable, but not appealing, because margins come from saving rather than earning. Buffett asks whether the price offers a margin of safety: it does, but management is the gatekeeper of that margin. Munger asks how the money is being burned: by opening a second front in its own weak spot. Li Lu asks where JD.com sits in the endgame of instant retail: third place, with the flywheel falling further and further behind. All four conclude that the current price is not a buying point but has entered a range worth watching — cash cushions the downside, while the upside must wait for management to prove itself.

Duan Yongping: Understandable, not to my liking, but I won't deny it is benfen

Duan Yongping's view of retail has not changed in twenty years: it is a tough business with no pricing power, where profit is squeezed out bit by bit through scale and efficiency. JD.com's 4.6% retail operating margin already counts as good in this industry, but he would immediately flag the nature of that number—it is the result of cutting marketing spend by 24.8%. In other words, 4.6% is a ceiling saved out, not a normal state earned out. A saved margin has a ceiling: expenses cannot be cut to zero, and once they are cut, revenue decline follows—and Q2 revenue of -2.9% is precisely the price paid after the cuts.

He would acknowledge JD.com's fundamentals: self-operated authentic goods, 211 delivery speed, social insurance for couriers, twenty years without touching counterfeit goods. These choices are costs in the short term and trust assets in the long term, and they do constitute differentiation. But he would press one question: if differentiation cannot charge money, does it count as a moat? JD.com's answer so far is "it counts as mindshare, not profit"—users recognize it, but will not pay 3% more for it.

So his conclusion would be: I understand this business, but I do not like it; if I am to hold it, the reason must be "the price is cheap enough," not "the business is good enough." The difference between these two statements determines whether you get trapped the next time it expands. He would add one more line: understanding but not liking it, the best action is to do nothing, and wait until it is cheap enough that you do not need to like it.

Buffett: the arithmetic is elegant, but the gatekeeper is not a number

Buffett would run three calculations first. First, market cap of HKD 280 billion (about RMB 256.9 billion) against cash and short-term investments of RMB 235.1 billion — 91.5% coverage. Second, cash per share of about HKD 91 versus a share price of HKD 99.4: you are paying HKD 8 for the retail, logistics and food-delivery businesses plus a platform with 600 million users. Third, 2026 adjusted net profit of about RMB 34 billion, or 7.5x market cap and 0.6x to 1x enterprise value.

Then he would ask the real question: on what grounds does this cash return to shareholders? That is the source of the valuation discount. The market is not blind to the RMB 235.1 billion; it simply does not believe the cash will become dividends or buybacks — because in 2025 management spent a year proving it will burn RMB 20 billion on strategic goals, roughly RMB 40 billion over two years.

In his framework, margin of safety = price discount + business certainty + management credibility. JD.com scores full marks on the first, middling on the second (revenue declining but cash-cow status intact), and fails the third. Multiply the three and a margin of safety still exists, but it is "conditionally cheap" — the condition being that management stops expanding. His move would be: don't buy, put it on the "too hard" pile, and wait for either a lower price or a change in management behavior. To paraphrase his own words: "7.5x P/E doesn't require the company to get better, only that it not get worse. But I can't assume it won't get worse unless someone proves it."

Munger: Invert—how did this RMB 40 billion get burned?

Munger would invert the problem: what does JD.com need in order to win food delivery? Rider density, merchant coverage, a fulfillment-cost advantage built on order volume, and users whose first instinct on opening the app is "food." Meituan leads on all four; Taobao Shangou has traffic and merchant-side support; JD.com has none.

The outcome was predictable in advance: share fell from a peak of about 31% in Q2 2025 to 7%–10%, new businesses lost RMB 9.85 billion in a single quarter, and cumulative investment over two years reached about RMB 40 billion. "They thought high frequency would drive low frequency, but forgot one thing: users who come to JD.com to buy an iPhone won't order food twice more just because the app offers delivery." Frequency did not migrate across categories — a pattern the e-commerce industry has verified repeatedly, not an accident.

He cares more about the decision structure than the outcome. Two years in, exiting now means admitting failure, while continuing to invest means continuing the bleed — the sunk-cost trap Munger hated most, and the position most likely to push management into irrational choices. His test for this situation is simple: ask "if we were starting from zero today, would we still enter this market?" The answer is clearly no, so the right move is to stop. He would therefore watch one signal: whether the narrower losses in Q3 and Q4 reflect "deliberate contraction" or "seasonal fluctuation." The former is a return to rationality; the latter is stalling.

Li Lu: Right trend, wrong company

Li Lu would zoom out. 30-minute delivery is rebuilding the infrastructure of urban retail: forward warehouses, rider networks, real-time inventory. Once this system reaches sufficient density, it will change the distribution path of the entire FMCG sector. This is a civilisation-level efficiency gain; the direction is certain and does not change based on who wins or loses.

But his real question is always: who captures the excess profit in the endgame? Instant retail has stronger scale effects than traditional e-commerce—order density determines fulfilment cost, fulfilment cost determines price, price determines order volume. This is a self-reinforcing flywheel, and the threshold to start the flywheel is very high. Today the division of labour is clear: Meituan holds mindshare and fulfilment density (51% of order volume), Taobao Shangou holds traffic and merchants (42% combined), and JD holds an "also here" position (7%–15%).

Third place gets left further and further behind in this kind of flywheel, because its fulfilment cost per order is higher than the leader's, while price must follow the leader. So his conclusion is: to buy this trend, one should buy the leader, not the third place's transformation story. JD's true long-term value lies not in instant retail but in the "self-operated + self-built logistics" infrastructure itself—this is scarce in China; the only question is whether it can monetise it. He would keep this point as a reason to revisit in ten years.

Risk/reward: 20% downside, 26% to 76% upside

Based on HKD 99.4: bear case HKD 80 (-20%), base case HKD 125 (+26%), bull case HKD 175 (+76%). On the simplest expected-value math, assigning 30%/50%/20% probabilities to the three scenarios gives an expected value of 0.3×80 + 0.5×125 + 0.2×175 = HKD 121.5, implying +22%. That number does not look bad, but consider where it comes from: 60% of the expected return rests on a single assumption—that "management stops burning cash"—rather than on business growth.

This is the odds trap: the denominator is price, the numerator is assumption. If food delivery keeps burning RMB 30 billion a year through 2027, base-case net profit of RMB 36 billion falls below RMB 25 billion, the target price drops straight back to the HKD 80 range and expected value turns negative. The level of the odds therefore ultimately hinges on management, not on valuation.

All four of us agree on this point: the current price is not a buying point. Nor is it a selling point—downside is cushioned by cash and free cash flow, unless the company itself removes that cushion. The sensible move: put it on the watchlist and set two triggers (price at HKD 90; event at Q3 dual targets met); whichever comes first, act on that one.

Conclusion

The four masters' consensus: the cheapness is real, and so are the reasons for it. Duan Yongping dislikes the profit-margin structure of the business, Buffett accepts the price but not management's credibility, Munger sees a textbook capital-allocation failure, and Li Lu sees the right trend but the wrong company position.

None of the four would buy at HKD 99.4, and none would exit entirely—RMB 235.1 billion in cash and RMB 31.4 billion of trailing free cash flow form a real floor. The next step hinges on two numbers: whether the new businesses' quarterly loss can narrow to within RMB 8 billion, and whether JD Retail revenue can turn positive. If both land, the neutral-case HKD 125 has an anchor; if only one does, the target is only around HKD 105.
