Industry Research (Munger's Lens) — JD.com

Competitive structure: China e-commerce and instant retail

Core view

China e-commerce is no longer a growth market. It is four players fighting for share of an existing pie: Pinduoduo owns price-sensitive demand, Douyin owns content-driven traffic, Meituan owns immediacy, and Alibaba owns merchants and payment infrastructure. JD.com's position is "authentic goods + speed" — worth money in 3C and home appliances, worth little in daily necessities and fresh produce. The core tension: fulfillment cost sets price, price sets share, and order density sets fulfillment cost — a winner-takes-all structure.

Density determines cost — the industry's first principle

Retail moats have only three sources: procurement scale, fulfillment density and user mindshare. JD.com is no weakling on procurement scale — annual procurement of 3C appliances runs into the hundreds of billions, giving it hard bargaining power over brands — and it is the strongest in far-field fulfillment density, with over 1,500 warehouses and 211 delivery. Its instant fulfillment density, however, is close to zero.

That is the fundamental reason it cannot win the food-delivery battle. The cost curve of instant retail is set by order density: a rider delivering 5 orders an hour versus 2 orders an hour produces a 2.5x difference in cost per order. Meituan has pushed density to the limit in core cities, while JD.com starts from zero, so its per-order fulfillment cost is inherently higher than rivals', yet prices must follow the competition. Every order therefore loses money, and the more it scales the more it loses; the higher the share, the faster the losses.

Munger would say: this is not an execution problem, it is a math problem. Math problems cannot be solved with determination.

The industry's second principle: frequency does not migrate across categories

The strategic case for food delivery rests on "high frequency drives low frequency": users order delivery daily and casually buy on JD.com along the way. The data does not support this. JD's food delivery order share fell from a peak of about 31% in Q2 2025 to 7%–10%, while JD Retail revenue fell 4.7% over the same period and general merchandise rose only 5.6%. If frequency really could drive low-frequency categories, general merchandise should be growing at a double-digit rate.

Only one company in the industry has genuinely made cross-category migration work: Meituan migrated food delivery users into in-store services, travel and hotel bookings, and instant shopping, because these three businesses share the same infrastructure of "3 kilometers, 30 minutes, local merchants." JD's retail infrastructure is "national warehouses, next-day delivery" — a completely different system from food delivery's "3 kilometers, 30 minutes." If the systems are not shared, the users will not be shared.

Cycle position: mid-stage zero-sum game, not the bottom

Industry backdrop: retail sales growth is running in the low single digits, online penetration has peaked, and the four players' shares are broadly stable even as each encroaches on the others. The national subsidy policy inflated the home appliance base in 2025 and rolls off in 2026, which directly explains JD's -11.8% in electronics and home appliances. That is a cyclical factor and will be fully absorbed in the first half of 2027.

Structural factors are harder to absorb: instant retail's substitution of far-field e-commerce is a long-term shift. Once 30-minute delivery becomes the default expectation, users' patience for next-day delivery erodes, particularly in high-frequency, low-ticket categories such as daily necessities, fresh food and pharmaceuticals. JD has no position in this migration — its biggest industry risk on a ten-year view.

The cyclical position, then: the cyclical bottom from the subsidy roll-off falls between the second half of 2026 and the first half of 2027, but the structural position is deteriorating — the two point in opposite directions. Revenue will therefore turn positive, but when it does it will be worth only single-digit growth.

Conclusion

Industry view: JD.com is strong in far-field e-commerce but ranks third in instant retail, and the industry is migrating from far-field to instant. Its losses in food delivery are not an execution problem but a matter of density math: starting from zero where rivals have already proven the flywheel means a higher cost per order.
