Risk Assessment (Li Lu's perspective) — JD.com

Under what circumstances would you lose money permanently?

Core view

Risk is not a 20% share-price decline; it is permanent capital loss. JD.com faces three paths to permanent loss: first, sustained cash burn (two more years of food delivery and RMB 235.1 billion becomes RMB 150 billion); second, instant retail hollowing out the core business's category mindshare (categories outside 3C steadily erode); third, a governance structure that leaves shareholders unable to constrain capital allocation (dual-class shares plus founder control). Of the three, the first is the most immediate, the third the most fundamental, and the second the slowest but most lethal.

Rule one: path dependency of cash burn

This is the most realistic risk because it is already playing out. Some RMB 40 billion went into food delivery over two years in exchange for a 7%–10% order-volume share and a business losing close to RMB 40 billion a year. The current quarter's loss is RMB 9.85 billion, only RMB 500 million below Q1's RMB 10.35 billion—on this trajectory it will not fall to RMB 8 billion until mid-2027, and will only approach breakeven in 2028.

The key question Li Lu would ask is not "how much longer will it lose money" but "under what circumstances would management stop." The answer is uncomfortable: the condition for stopping is admitting failure, and the cost of that admission falls on the people who made the decision. This is path dependence—not that they do not know they should stop, but that whoever stops has to own the responsibility. The only reliable signal of a stop, then, is not the loss figure but organisational action: whether the food delivery team is downsized, whether cities are exited, whether subsidy spending is halted.

Second: the slow erosion of category mindshare

This is the slowest-moving risk but the most lethal over a ten-year horizon. JD.com's core asset is the "buy big-ticket, high-value items on JD" mindset, which underpins its 3C and home appliance share and its bargaining power on payment terms. That mindset has clear boundaries, however: it covers only low-frequency, high-ticket categories that require trust.

Instant retail is reshaping high-frequency categories such as daily necessities, fresh food, pharmaceuticals and FMCG. Once users are accustomed to 30-minute delivery of medicine, groceries and household goods, JD's next-day delivery confers no advantage in these categories. Q2 daily necessities at +5.6% looks decent, but that is low growth in a fast-growing category—share is being lost; only the absolute number is still rising.

In Li Lu's framework, this is "infrastructure downgrade": the company still makes money, but the infrastructure layer it sits on is being replaced by a new generation. JD.com's problem is not that a competitor beat it—it is that the era swapped out the foundation beneath it.

Article 3: Governance structure and capital allocation authority

This is the most fundamental point. JD.com's dual-class share structure gives the founder absolute voting control through Class B shares, leaving outside shareholders with no substantive check on major capital allocation. When the founder's judgment is right, that structure is an advantage: long-termism, resistance to short-term pressure. When it is wrong, it amplifies risk, because no one can call a halt.

The 2025–2026 food-delivery war is a case of the latter. A decision requiring tens of billions in sustained investment, taken against rivals holding every structural advantage, faced no veto mechanism at the outside-shareholder level. Li Lu's approach to such companies has always been consistent: either demand a sufficiently deep discount on price, or stay away. At 7.5x P/E, with cash at nine-tenths of market cap, one can call it a "sufficiently deep discount" — but a discount can hedge one mistake, not a series of them.

What won't make him sell, and what will

Would not make him sell: the stock fell from 99.4 to 85. Cash and free cash flow form a real floor, and a lower price is simply a better price. Would make him sell: management announces a new round of large-scale investment (a second food-delivery push, community group buying, overseas heavy-asset expansion), free cash flow turns negative, or the cash balance falls by more than RMB 20 billion for two consecutive quarters. Any one of these three breaks the "won't deteriorate" premise, and the valuation logic shifts from "cash floor" to "cash burn".

Conclusion

Risk perspective: Near term, the risk is continued cash burn; longer term, replacement at the infrastructure layer; structurally, shareholders cannot constrain capital allocation. None of the three is enough to put the company in trouble within three years, but together they explain why it trades at 7.5x P/E rather than 15x.
