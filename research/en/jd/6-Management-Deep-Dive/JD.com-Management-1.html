Core thesis

JD.com's governance structure is the hardest part of this report to quantify, and the part that cannot be ignored. Richard Liu controls more than 70% of voting power through Class B shares, while Xu Ran is the de facto head of execution; since 2023 the division of labour between the two has been very clear: one sets strategy, the other manages delivery. That division delivered strong results on cost control (marketing spend cut 24.8%, free cash flow +44.6%), but on strategic choices it came with RMB 40 billion in tuition (food delivery). The capital allocation record therefore splits into two columns: an A in saving money, a D in spending it.

Key people: one sets strategy, one drives execution

Liu Qiangdong's role has changed substantially since 2022: he no longer runs day-to-day operations, yet he has ceded no strategic decision-making authority. After Xu Ran took over as CEO in May 2023, JD.com's organisational structure became "founder sets direction + professional managers execute." Over 2024–2026, that structure produced two sharply divergent outcomes.

At the execution level, it worked. Xu Ran's finance background shows directly in cost control: 2026Q2 marketing spend was RMB 20.3 billion, down 24.8% YoY, with its share of revenue compressed from 7.6% to 5.9%, while R&D rose 37.7% against the trend to RMB 7.3 billion—not across-the-board cost cutting but a shift of spend from customer acquisition to technology, and the direction is right. Operating cash flow rose 54.5% and free cash flow 44.6%, taking a company that posted a quarterly loss back to profit within a year—execution worthy of a case study.

At the strategic level, it failed. The 2025 decision to go all-in on food delivery appears from public information to be a founder-led strategic choice rather than a managerial execution error. In JD.com's organisation, such a decision could hardly be vetoed internally—because the voting structure means no one is in a position to veto it.

Capital allocation: A for saving money, D for spending it

Split 2024–2026 capital allocation into two columns and the conclusion is stark.

The saving column: A. Marketing expense fell 24.8% in a year and capex compression lifted free cash flow from RMB 10.1 billion to RMB 31.4 billion (trailing 12 months), while the cash balance rose from RMB 225.4 billion at the start of the year to RMB 235.1 billion. Delivering all three against negative revenue growth is hard-won.

The spending column: D. RMB 40 billion poured into food delivery bought 7%–10% share and a business losing nearly RMB 10 billion a quarter. Spent on buybacks, that money would repurchase about 15% of shares at the current market cap; paid as dividends, it equals two years of net profit. Munger would call this "buying face with shareholders' money."

The contrast between the two columns says one thing: this company does not lack execution; it lacks the discipline to not do things. Capital allocation is, at its core, deciding what not to do.

Integrity and governance: no fraud, but a trust discount

On financial integrity, JD.com's record is clean. Its auditor has long been one of the international Big Four, quarterly disclosure is granular down to segment revenue and segment operating profit, and the gap between non-GAAP and GAAP is clearly explained (share-based compensation plus amortisation of intangible assets). There have been no major financial restatements or regulatory penalties.

Three governance issues, however, constitute a long-term discount. First, the dual-class share structure. Class B shares carry 20 votes each, and the founder controls more than 70% of voting power on roughly 11%–13% of equity, leaving outside shareholders no veto at the strategic level. Second, the 2018 Minnesota incident: no charges were filed, but the damage to the company's governance reputation is long-lasting and is one reason institutional investors have persistently applied a discount. Third, the 2024 organisational overhaul (crackdowns on attendance and proxy check-ins, and the founder's internal speech stressing hard work), which reflects declining organisational efficiency and anxiety over execution — a short-term lift, but a signal of cultural pressure over the long run.

None of the three makes the company uninvestable, but together they explain why a company trading at 7.5x P/E, with nine-tenths of its market cap in cash, has seen its valuation discount stay stubbornly wide.

Succession: no near-term worry, no medium-term fix

JD.com's succession question has two layers. The operational layer is settled: Xu Ran is the de facto operating chief, with the heads of finance, supply chain and logistics all reporting to her, and the transition since 2023 has been smooth. The strategic layer is not: Liu Qiangdong remains the only person who can decide whether to fight a battle, and there is no public succession arrangement for that seat.

The implication for investment judgment: you cannot use "replace management" as an assumption for a valuation re-rating. At a company with dispersed ownership, poor results get the CEO replaced; at JD.com, a strategic misstep replaces the executors, not the decision-maker. So "management stops expanding" can only be observed through behaviour, not forced through governance mechanisms.

This is the underlying reason the fourth of the six gates (management) scores only 6 and the sixth (discipline) only 4 — not a lack of capability, but a lack of correction mechanisms.

Conclusion

Management assessment: Grade A execution, Grade D decision-making, no error-correction mechanism. Xu Ran did everything a CFO-turned-CEO could: cut costs, protect cash, raise R&D. Liu Qiangdong proved he is still willing to place big bets—only this time he bet on the wrong table.

Investment implication: do not treat "management turning rational" as a base case; treat it as an option that needs verification. Three observable checkpoints: whether the food-delivery team is downsized, whether it exits some cities, and whether a new round of subsidies is announced in Q4. If none of the three appears, the contraction is real; if any one appears, the story is not over.
