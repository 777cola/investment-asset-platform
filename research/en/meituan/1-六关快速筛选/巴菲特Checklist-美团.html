Gate one: can I understand this business?

★★★★☆ 4/5Through

In one sentence, explain what Meituan is doing.

Meituan is China's largest local life services platform—connecting consumers with offline merchants for food delivery, restaurant discovery, hotel booking, fresh grocery, and errand and proxy-purchase services. Three core businesses drive the company:Food delivery (on-demand)、In-store dining, liquor and travel、Instant retail (Meituan Instashopping)。

Deconstructing the business logic

  • How to make money:Commission per delivery order (about 15-20%) + merchant advertising fees + in-store group-buying commissions + delivery fees
  • Why it makes money:Nearly 100 million transactions a day dilute per-order delivery costs; merchants cannot do without platform traffic
  • Who are the customers:Both ends—C-end users (400-500 million annual active) + B-end merchants (~9 million active merchants)
  • Where is the moat:Three-sided network effect—more users → more merchants → more riders → faster delivery → even more users

Circle of competence assessment

✅ Food delivery is a high-frequency daily purchase with a short decision chain and clear user value, placing it in the category of "businesses that are easy to understand."
⚠️ Note that Meituan's moat isPhysical + digitalIts dual network effect makes it harder to disrupt than a pure internet platform and gives it a greater scale advantage than traditional retail. Understanding delivery-network economics takes time.

Conclusion:ThroughThe business itself is not hard to understand and fits within our circle of competence.

Three proofs of a good business

  • From a USD 3.6 billion loss to a USD 5 billion profit: A USD 3.6 billion loss in 2021 and a USD 5 billion profit in 2024 — proof of this business model's profit potential. Not a false premise of "burning cash for growth."

Gate three: is the moat deep enough

★★★★☆ 4/5Through

The four layers of Meituan's moat

Tier 1: Three-sided network effects (★★★★★)

This is Meituan'sCore moatUsers, merchants and riders reinforce one another: more users lead to more orders, which attract more riders, which speed up delivery, which draws in more merchants, which widen selection, which brings in more users. Once this flywheel spins, it is extremely hard for latecomers to break. Ele.me has failed to turn the tables despite years of backing from Alibaba.

Second layer: physical delivery network (★★★★★)

Meituan's delivery network is not software — it is a system built from millions of riders, an intelligent dispatch system and urban warehousing and distribution nodesPhysical infrastructureIt took 10 years and tens of billions to build this network. JD Food Delivery or Douyin Food Delivery cannot replicate it just by spending money—it requires time, organization and accumulated operational experience.

Tier 3: The brand is the category (★★★★)

In China, "Meituan" is almost synonymous with "food delivery." Once that brand mindshare takes hold, Meituan becomes the user's default option. Persuading users to install a second food-delivery app carries extremely high customer-acquisition costs.

Tier 4: Switching costs (★★★★)

For consumers, switching to another app for food delivery is easy, but they cannot be bothered. For merchants, Meituan's review system, order system and advertising tools are already deeply embedded in their operations, making migration costly. For riders, Meituan is the largest source of orders; switching platforms could halve their order volume.

Gate 4: Is management trustworthy

★★★☆☆ 3/5Conditionally approved

Wang Xing: the successes and failures of a serial entrepreneur

Wang Xing is one of the most seasoned serial entrepreneurs in Chinese internet history—Xiaonei (later Renren), Fanfou (the earliest Twitter-like product), and finally his great success with Meituan. HisStrategic resolve and executionProven: it fought its way out of the "thousand-group war" and survived and overtook the encirclement by Ele.me + Didi + Baidu Nuomi.

Capital allocation record

  • HighlightsIn 2022 it decisively cut cash-burning businesses (scaling back Meituan Select, shutting down the ride-hailing unit) to focus on core food delivery plus in-store. Owning up to mistakes when warranted is the mark of an excellent manager.
  • QuestionableThe Mobike acquisition (2018, USD 2.7 billion) has essentially gone to zero, wasting substantial capital on bike-sharing.
  • QuestionableNo dividend, limited buyback scale (about USD 1 billion in 2024), insufficient attention to shareholder returns.

Governance structure

  • A/B share structure, Wang Xing holds ~60% voting power,Control is extremely secureThe upside is that management can stick to its strategy over the long term; the downside is weak constraints from external shareholders.
  • The RMB 3.44 billion antitrust fine in 2021 reflected the regulatory tug-of-war. After the fine, Wang Xing cooperated with rectification and did not escalate tensions further.

Conclusion:Conditionally approvedWang Xing is an excellent strategist with strong execution. But his shareholder-return orientation (no dividends, few buybacks) does not fit Buffett's preferences. Competent but not outstanding.

One-sentence summary

"A great business + a wide moat + extremely low valuation = Meituan is one of the most compelling deep-value opportunities in the Hong Kong stock market today."

Core buy thesis (one-sentence version)

You bought a platform business at PS 1.3x with an FCF yield of 10.6% and net cash at 32% of market cap. The market is pessimistic about "slowing growth plus intensifying competition" — but that pessimism is fully priced in, and Meituan's physical delivery network and network effects have not disappeared. Buying a good company at a fair price is exactly this.

Risks that must be acknowledged

  • The biggest risk is a "value trap":If growth slides all the way to 0-5% and competition prevents any margin improvement, 1.3x PS may be "fair" rather than "cheap." Meituan could shift from a growth stock to a "toll-collector" — akin to a stable utility.
  • The discount on China concept stocks may persist long term:Poor liquidity in Hong Kong stocks, sustained foreign outflows and uncertainty in China-US relations could keep valuations range-bound at low levels for a long time. Even if a company's value is recognized, Mr. Market may take his time to play along.
  • Policy uncertainty:Rider social insurance, data compliance, platform-economy regulation—these are not one-off shocks but persistent cost pressures.
