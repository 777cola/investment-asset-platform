1
What is the essence of Meituan?
From group-buying site to "tech retail": a 13-year evolution
▾
In one sentence, the essence of Meituan's business:Meituan is a tech retail platform with high-frequency "food" consumption as its core entry point, instant delivery as its infrastructure, and LBS (location-based services) as its technological core.

I. 2010-2015: Survivor of the Thousand Group-Buying War

In March 2010, Wang Xing founded Meituan. At the time, group buying was a red-hot track in China's internet sector—at its peak, more than 5,000 group-buying sites nationwide fought in the "war of a thousand group-buying sites." Lashou, Wowo, Nuomi… every one of them burned cash on a spectacular scale.

Meituan survived not because it had more money, but because of two things:

First, Wang Xing's obsession with "efficiency".While everyone else was spending heavily on advertising to acquire users, Meituan stuck to data-driven operations. Wang Xing borrowed a line from The Art of War: "win first, then seek battle" — work out the cost structure before deciding whether to fight. When the group-buying bubble burst in 2011, Lashou.com collapsed after burning through USD 170 million, while Meituan still had ample cash on hand.

Second, Meituan spotted the super-variable of the "mobile internet" early.Meituan launched its mobile app in 2011, while most competitors still relied on PC web pages. What did mobile mean? It meant "location" became the core data dimension. When you know where a user is, you can recommend the best restaurant nearby. That was the starting point of LBS.

By 2015, Meituan had merged with Dianping, completing its absolute leadership in the "in-store" scenario. Meituan at this point was essentially aLocal life information service platform—Helping users find stores, helping stores acquire customers.

II. 2015-2020: The decisive factor in the food-delivery war

Meituan entered food delivery in late 2013. It was a bloodier battlefield than group buying—its rivals were Ele.me, backed by Alibaba, and Baidu Waimai.

Why is Wang Xing fighting this hard battle? Because "in-store" is a low-frequency scenario (how many times does a person eat at restaurants in a month?), but "food delivery" isA daily-active super-high-frequency entry pointHigh-frequency use cases beating low-frequency ones is the most brutal form of dimensional strike in the internet world.

Meituan struck late and overtook Ele.me in 2017 to become the industry leader. Two decisions proved decisive:

1. Self-built delivery system.Meituan did not simply operate a platform-matching business; it invested heavily to build its own nationwide instant-delivery network. Meituan Waimai's delivery force exceeds 1 million people (including crowdsourced and full-time riders), making it the world's largest instant-delivery network. This infrastructure later became the foundation for all of Meituan's new businesses.

2. The underlying logic of the "T-shaped strategy".Wang Xing's T-shaped strategy: the vertical stroke is food delivery, the high-frequency entry point; the horizontal stroke is the businesses extended around "eating" — in-store services, hotel and travel, and Instashopping. High frequency drives low frequency: food delivery's low gross margin is subsidized by the high gross margins of other businesses.

By 2020, Meituan's food delivery daily order volume exceeded 40 million orders, with over 6 million annual active merchants. Meituan was no longer an "information platform" — it had becomeA tech retail platform with physical-world fulfillment capabilities。

III. 2020-2023: Seeing the ceiling of "everything to your door"

After 2020, Meituan did something many found hard to understand: it kept investing in its already loss-making food delivery business while opening new fronts such as Meituan Instashopping and Meituan Select.

The strategic judgment behind this is:The order-volume ceiling for food delivery may be far higher than the market expects, but the more important ceiling lies "beyond food delivery" — instant retail.

Meituan Instashopping's logic is simple: if I can deliver a meal in 30 minutes, why not a case of beer, a bouquet of flowers, a phone? This is not category expansion, this isReuse of fulfillment capabilitiesThe delivery network is already built, marginal cost is near zero, and every additional category dilutes fixed costs.

Meanwhile, Meituan Select (community group buying) is expanding aggressively in lower-tier markets. Although this business is losing money heavily, its strategic significance lies in:Extend Meituan's supply chain and delivery capabilities to tier 3–6 cities。

Wang Xing himself once said: "Meituan is not a food delivery company, but a technology-driven platform that builds infrastructure around the core scenario of 'eating'."

This positioning means that Meituan's core competitive moat is not traffic, butFulfillment efficiency in the physical world—How fast, at what cost, can you deliver what to whom.

V. The one-sentence version

Meituan is "China's largest and the world's leading instant-fulfillment tech retail platform" — over 10 years it has built a physical delivery network that reaches almost every Chinese city within 30 minutes, and on that network it runs a multi-layered business model spanning food delivery, instant shopping and in-store hotel and travel.

This nature determines all of Meituan's strengths (deep moat, strong network effects) and all of its weaknesses (asset-heavy, low gross margin, fierce competition). To understand Meituan is not to understand how much money it made today, but to understand how much this "30-minute delivery network" is really worth.

2
Is food delivery a good business?
Deep dive into unit economics per order
▾
Food delivery is a "high-frequency, low-margin" business with "crushing scale"—this is almost an investing axiom. But to truly understand it, we must work through the economics of every single delivery order.

1. A RMB 30 food-delivery order: where does the money go?

Suppose a user in Beijing orders a RMB 30 takeout meal. We break down where every yuan goes:

User payment:      30 yuan
   ├─ Merchant receives:      ~21 yuan   (70%)
   ├─ Rider delivery fee:    ~5 yuan    (17%)
   ├─ Platform net commission revenue: ~3 yuan    (10%)
   └─ Payment channel fee:    ~1 yuan    (3%)

From this RMB 3 in net commission revenue, the platform must also deduct roughly RMB 1.5-2.5 in costs such as user subsidies, technology R&D and marketing expenses. Net operating profit per order is only about0.5-1.5 yuan—Less than the price of a cup of milk tea.

Profit per order = RMB 3 (net commission revenue) - RMB 2 (operating expenses) = about RMB 1

2. Why do hundreds of companies still want a piece of a low-margin business?

Because of two numbers:Frequency × scale.

High frequency: the engine of user stickiness.An urban white-collar worker places about 200 food-delivery orders a year, spending roughly RMB 6,000. That frequency ranks behind only social networking and short video among all internet services. High frequency generates user behavioral data, locks in consumption habits and opens cross-selling entry points.

Scale: the mathematical magic of unit economics.Food delivery has extremely high fixed costs (technology R&D, delivery network) and very low variable costs. When a platform's average daily orders rise from 10 million to 50 million, delivery density increases, technology costs are diluted, merchant bargaining power strengthens, and the data flywheel accelerates — Meituan's per-order delivery cost fell about 35% from its peak.

III. Cost structure of food delivery

Rider costsis the largest single expense, accounting for 17-20% of platform revenue. Note in particular: China's food-delivery rider costs (low labor costs, 100% e-bike penetration, high urban population density), compared with Uber Eats and DoorDash, haveStructural advantagesThis is also the biggest challenge facing Meituan's overseas expansion.

IV. Why is "thin profit" an irreplaceable moat?

Counterintuitive conclusion:Precisely because food delivery is a low-margin business, it is a good business.

Thin margins mean: the barrier to entry for new players is extremely high (break-even requires 50 million orders a day); giants are reluctant to commit fully (margins far below their core businesses); and subsidy wars become a contest of efficiency (the efficient lose far less per order).

Zhang Yiming once said: "Don't enter low-gross-margin industries." Wang Xing's response: "Real moats are built in the hard, dirty work."

V. Conclusion: an unexpectedly "good business"

Think of Meituan as a shopping mall: food delivery is the bubble tea shop in that mall that never makes money but always has a queue—its own margins are razor-thin, but it keeps the whole mall alive.

In terms of ecosystem value: food delivery anchors the usage habits of 680 million users, financially supports a delivery network of 1 million riders, serves as the foundational entry point for the digitalization of 6 million merchants, and is the cash cow and source of operational experience for Meituan's new businesses.

Food delivery is not a high-margin business in itself, but it is a strategic one with a durable moat, no substitute, and the capacity to support a vast commercial ecosystem.

How deep is Meituan's moat?

II. Four layers of the moat

Layer one: economies of scale (easiest to replicate)

6 million orders per day → rider density advantage → data accumulation advantage. But it is not unreplicable.

Second layer: delivery infrastructure (harder to replicate)

Covering more than 2,800 counties and urban districts nationwide, with about 1 million active riders. Operational complexity is extremely high, and the physical network cannot be skipped—no matter how much money you burn, you cannot build a nationwide network with the same efficiency within a year.

Third layer: three-party network effects (extremely hard to replicate)

Once the "tipping point" is crossed, latecomers can hardly catch up. In a three-sided market, share concentrates toward the leader — Meituan's food delivery share rose from 50% in 2017 to 70% in 2024.

Layer four: brand and mindshare (hardest to replicate)

"Ordering takeout" is nearly synonymous with "opening Meituan." The power of habit is the deepest moat of all.

III. Douyin's offense: has the moat been weakened?

Douyin's 2024 local-life GTV was about RMB 300 billion, roughly 25-30% of Meituan's in-store business. But in food delivery, Douyin has three fatal weaknesses: no delivery network, no capacity for a protracted war (the paradox of subsidizing low-margin business with high-margin profits), and a different user mindset (scrolling videos vs ordering takeout).

IV. Alibaba's renewed offensive

After Alibaba acquired Ele.me, its share fell from 40% to around 25%. The core problem is not a lack of money but a lack of focus. Alibaba's local-life businesses each fought their own battles.

Is Wang Xing trustworthy?
CEO in depth: serial entrepreneur, rationalist, long-term thinker
▾

I. Founding history: from a "serial failure" to the "king of local life"

Six startup attempts, the first 5 all failed—Duoduoyou (social networking), Youzitu (photo sharing), Xiaonei (campus social networking, sold successfully but overtaken), Hainei (business social networking, failed), Fanfou (microblogging, shut down). Only Meituan survived.

Repeated failures gave Wang Xing two extremely rare qualities:Extreme emphasis on "survival"(Always maintain 18 months of capital) andDeep skepticism toward "models" and the ability to iterate。

II. The rationalist's mental model

Wang Xing's reading volume ranks at the top among Chinese CEOs—he has cited The Art of War, Sapiens, Finite and Infinite Games, The Innovator's Dilemma, The Black Swan, Antifragile... He does not "read and pretend to understand"; he genuinely uses frameworks to analyze business problems.

The double-edged sword of rationalism:The good side is that all major decisions were backed by clear data; the contentious side is the "boundaryless" expansion—Mobike (acquired for USD 2.7 billion, never profitable) and Meituan Select (huge losses). But all the diversified businesses share one thing: they reuse the delivery network or LBS capabilities.

Biggest controversy:Zero buybacks, zero dividends. USD 26 billion cash on the books, USD 6.5 billion FCF a year, zero returns. Wang Xing's capital allocation philosophy: invest in growth first > ensure safety > return to shareholders.

IV. Long-termism or grandiosity?

Evidence for long-termism: six years of delivery losses while sustaining investment in the delivery network; no layoffs during downturns; the "slow money" logic behind overseas expansion Keeta. Grounds for the grandiosity critique: massive losses at Meituan Select; multiple fronts diluting organizational focus; an internal culture of "competing to the extreme".

V. Overall assessment

In one sentence: Wang Xing is not a man who lets investors feel "at ease" (because he is forever chasing growth), but he is a man investors "trust" — because his decision-making logic is consistent, traceable and testable.

His real competitive edge lies in:He can see the endgame 10 years out and has the discipline to build the infrastructure that leads to it.Among Chinese internet CEOs, he ranks in the top three for long-term trustworthiness—behind only Zhang Yiming and Pony Ma.

Meituan vs JD.com vs Alibaba — the instant retail three-way battle
Meituan Instashopping vs JD Hourly Delivery vs Alibaba Instant Retail
▾
Instant retail—"everything to your door"—is the hottest battleground in Chinese internet from 2024 to 2026. The market is worth about RMB 800 billion and is expected to exceed RMB 2 trillion by 2030.

I. Comparing the starting lines of the three contenders

Meituan Instashopping: delivery network reuse

Core strengths: the food-delivery network is reused directly, marginal cost is extremely low, and coverage scales very fast. Weaknesses: insufficient supply-chain depth (no self-built supply chain for fresh food or daily necessities), and low average order value (RMB 40-60 vs JD.com's RMB 80-120).

JD Instant Shopping: supply-chain leverage

Core strengths: 20 years of self-built logistics and supply chain, the strongest quality control and pricing power in 3C electronics and general merchandise. Weaknesses: delivery density below Meituan (street level), and an insufficient instant-delivery DNA (accustomed to "planned routes and batch delivery" rather than "accepting orders at any time").

Alibaba Instant Retail: Wolf Pack Tactics

Ele.me, Taoxianda, Freshippo, Tmall Supermarket, Amap… each line has a dedicated team working it in depth. The biggest problem: internal horse-racing and integration challenges, no unified entry point, and Ele.me itself struggling with losses.

I. Three drivers of the turnaround to profitability

Driver 1: Structural gross margin improvement (most important)

Gross margin rose from 23.7% to 38.4% (+14.7 percentage points). Drivers: higher food-delivery efficiency (per-order cost down 35%), a larger share of high-margin in-store business, and advertising revenue growth. Real and sustainable.

Driver 2: The "leverage effect" of operating expenses

R&D spend flat in absolute terms (USD 2.9 billion vs USD 3.1 billion) while revenue grew 69%. Rapid growth in SG&A (USD 10.4 billion) is a concern — at 22.1% of revenue it remains elevated.

Driver 3: Scale leverage

Revenue grew 69% but fixed costs did not scale proportionally. Of every USD 1 of incremental revenue, USD 0.2-0.3 converted into profit.

FCF is 1.3-2.5x net income, because depreciation and amortization (USD 1.1-1.4 billion) and stock-based compensation (~USD 700 million) are non-cash expenses.

III. Hidden risks

① Meituan Select is still burning cash:Annual losses of about USD 1.5-2 billion, but USD 26 billion in cash is tolerable.
② Irreversible SG&A growth:Facing competition from Douyin, marketing spend is hard to cut in the short term.

④ The cycle of competitive subsidies:Black-swan risk of JD.com/Alibaba heavily subsidizing instant retail.

Meituan's turnaround is real and fundamentally driven, not an accounting trick.The core drivers of the swing from net loss to net profit are all genuine operational improvements. But deterioration in any one of SG&A expenses, Youxuan losses or competitive risk could stall profit growth in 2025-2026.

Meituan Goes Global: The Keeta Story in Saudi Arabia and Brazil
Overseas expansion: progress, challenges and the ceiling
▾

I. Why Saudi Arabia?

Keeta launched in Hong Kong in 2023 and entered Riyadh, the Saudi capital, in late 2023. Reasons for choosing Saudi Arabia: low food-delivery penetration (10-15% vs 30%+ in China), GDP per capita of USD 30,000 (high acceptance of delivery fees), highly concentrated population (three major cities), and friendly government policy (Vision 2030).

In mid-2025, Keeta entered São Paulo, Brazil—Meituan's second overseas country.

II. Four major challenges in overseas expansion

Challenge 1: Structural disadvantage in delivery costs (the biggest challenge)

Chinese riders earn USD 0.4-0.7 per delivery; Saudi riders earn USD 3-5 (5-10x). Cost gap between electric bikes and petrol motorcycles. Population density: Riyadh 4,000 people/km² vs 20,000 people/km² in China's tier-1 cities (5x).Meituan's per-order delivery cost in Saudi Arabia is expected to be 5-8x that in China.

Challenge two: brand awareness and user habits

Saudi users stick to HungerStation, Jahez and Careem (Uber Eats). The cost of getting users to switch apps far exceeds that of expanding into new cities in China.

Challenge three: localisation management is difficult

Saudization requirements under Saudi labor law drive up employment costs. Managing a workforce that spans different cultures, languages and legal regimes becomes exponentially harder.

Challenge 4: local competitors block the way

HungerStation (~35%), Jahez (~25%), Careem (~20%) — local players have built up merchant networks and user habits over years.

IV. Assessment: neutral to bullish

Overseas food-delivery markets have not produced a winner-takes-all outcome—Uber Eats, DoorDash and Deliveroo each hold their own territory. Meituan's "efficiency advantage" may be erased overseas by cost structure, but its "slow expansion" strategy is sensible (no cash-burning for growth).

Keeta's value lies not in how many orders it delivers today, but in whether it proves that Meituan's management system and algorithmic capabilities can be replicated across different cultural, economic and regulatory environments.
