## Upstream: Meta is the largest buyer and holds the least bargaining power

Meta's 2026 capex is USD 135–145 billion, alongside USD 825 billion of signed lease and purchase commitments, making it one of the world's largest buyers of AI infrastructure. Upstream, however, it has no pricing power: GPUs have to be queued for, HBM capacity fought for, and power locked in through 15- to 20-year contracts.

The most counterintuitive point in the supply chain: a company with 79% gross margin has almost no bargaining power on procurement. Its demand is rigid—without buying compute, its recommendation models fall behind and ad unit prices stop rising. Upstream knows this, and so upstream sets the price.

In-house chips are the only hedge. MTIA has already replaced GPUs in some inference scenarios, but training still depends on Nvidia. This path can save 10%–20% of costs, but it cannot change the landscape.

## Profit pools are shifting upstream

A rough but intuitive calculation: Meta's quarterly advertising revenue was USD 63.06 billion against quarterly capex of USD 34.91 billion—meaning for every 100 dollars of ad revenue, 55 dollars went straight to equipment and data-center suppliers. Three years ago that ratio was around 30 dollars (2022 capex was about 30% of revenue).

Operating profit: USD 12.99 billion (including the settlement) or USD 22.88 billion (excluding it). Capex is 1.5x adjusted operating profit. In other words, Meta did not earn enough this quarter to pay for its equipment; it covered the gap with cash accumulated in the past and stock just issued. That is the profit pool shifting upstream from platforms, in numbers.

## Midstream: platforms collect the tax, and the auction sets the rate

The platform layer captures value in a distinctive way: it produces no content, owns no data and provides only matching. Meta's "tax" is the ad unit price, which rose 14% per ad in Q3 2026. Meta does not set that tax rate; advertisers bid it up—though Meta can lift the equilibrium price by using algorithms to improve matching efficiency.

Google (search, USD 82.01 billion/quarter), Amazon (retail venues, USD 19.64 billion), ByteDance (TikTok, unlisted), Snap, Pinterest and Reddit also sit in the midstream. What they share: whoever controls user intent can levy a higher tax. Search captures "what I want to buy"; feeds capture "what you might want to buy." Search's tax rate has historically been higher, but in 2026 it is being eroded by AI summaries.

## Downstream, advertiser budgets are concentrating toward performance

Downstream sit tens of millions of advertisers, and their behaviour is shifting in two consistent directions: budgets are moving from brand to performance advertising, and from the open web to walled gardens. Both shifts favour Meta—performance advertising is its home turf, and the walled gardens hold behavioural data on 3.64 billion people.

But downstream is pushing back. Amazon blocking Muse from operating on its website is retailers' instinctive response to "entry points being hijacked." If more large platforms ban agents, a big chunk of Muse's shopping use cases gets cut off—downstream countering the midstream, and 2026 has only just begun.

## Cycle position: capex overheating, ad demand moderate

Split the chain in two. Upstream (chips, memory, power) is at a cyclical peak: the four major cloud providers spent a combined USD 207.8 billion on capex in 2026Q3, up 115.5% YoY. Midstream platforms are growing steadily: Meta advertising rose 26%, Google 10.6%, Amazon 10.9%.

The risk sits in the speed gap between the two. Upstream is adding capacity at 115% while midstream monetises at 10%–26%, making overcapacity and price declines in between inevitable. Memory already rose 60%–70% in early 2026, and gains of that magnitude cannot persist for three years.

Implication for Meta: it pays the bill for upstream overheating and benefits from midstream stability. When the cycle peaks, its capex will fall first and its profit will recover first—but by then the market will already be worrying about demand.

## Its true position in the chain

In one sentence: Meta is a tax collector midstream but a taxpayer upstream. Gross margin is high because content production and distribution costs are pushed onto users; free cash flow is low because profits are handed over to chipmakers, utilities and builders.

That position is very comfortable when ad demand is strong — the tax rate can keep rising. It is very uncomfortable in an AI arms race — upstream can keep raising prices. 2026 is precisely the year the latter prevails.

## A counterexample: why ad-tech companies are losing value instead

The only link in the chain that is depreciating is ad tech and attribution. The reason is simple: Apple restricted tracking in 2021, Google is phasing out third-party cookies, and the platforms have pulled attribution into their own closed loops (Meta's Conversions API, Google's Privacy Sandbox). The value intermediaries can offer has been internalised by the platforms.

That is good news for Meta, which has pocketed gross profit along the industry chain that once belonged to third parties. It is bad news for downstream advertisers: measurement power is monopolised by the platforms, and their bargaining power weakens further. Every dollar that shifts along the chain is a dollar someone loses.
