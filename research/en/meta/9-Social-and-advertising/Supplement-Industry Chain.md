## Upstream: Meta is the largest buyer and the party with the least bargaining power

Meta's 2026 capex is USD 135–145 billion, plus USD 825 billion in signed lease and purchase commitments, making it one of the world's largest buyers of AI infrastructure. But upstream, it has no pricing power: GPUs require queuing, HBM requires fighting for capacity, and power requires signing 15- to 20-year long-term contracts.

**This is the most counterintuitive point in the supply chain: a company with 79% gross margin has almost no bargaining power on the procurement side.** Because its demand is rigid—without buying compute, its recommendation models fall behind, and ad unit prices stop rising. Upstream knows this, so upstream sets the price.

In-house chips are the only hedge. MTIA has already replaced GPUs in some inference scenarios, but training still depends on Nvidia. This path can save 10%–20% of costs, but it cannot change the landscape.

## Profit pools are shifting upstream

A rough but intuitive calculation: Meta's quarterly advertising revenue was USD 63.06 billion, quarterly capex USD 34.91 billion—**meaning for every 100 dollars of ad revenue, 55 dollars went straight to equipment and data-center suppliers.** Three years ago that ratio was around 30 dollars (2022 capex was about 30% of revenue).

Now operating profit: USD 12.99 billion (including the settlement) or USD 22.88 billion (excluding it). Capex is 1.5x adjusted operating profit. **In other words, Meta did not earn enough this quarter to pay for its equipment; it covered the gap with cash accumulated in the past and stock just issued.** This is the quantitative expression of the profit pool shifting from platforms upstream.

## Midstream: platforms are tax collectors, and tax rates are set by auction

The platform layer captures value in a distinctive way: it does not produce content, does not own data, and only provides matching. The "tax" Meta collects is the ad unit price, which rose 14% per ad in Q3 2026. **That tax rate is not set by Meta; it is bid up by advertisers**—but Meta can raise the equilibrium price by using algorithms to improve matching efficiency.

Also in the midstream are Google (search, USD 82.01 billion/quarter), Amazon (retail venues, USD 19.64 billion), ByteDance (TikTok, unlisted), Snap, Pinterest and Reddit. What they share: whoever controls user intent can levy a higher tax. Search captures "what I want to buy"; feeds capture "what you might want to buy." Search's tax rate has historically been higher, but in 2026 it is being eroded by AI summaries.

## Downstream: advertiser budgets are concentrating toward performance

Downstream are tens of millions of advertisers. Their common behavior: budgets are shifting from brand advertising to performance advertising, and from the open web to walled gardens. **Both shifts favor Meta**—performance advertising is its home turf, and the walled gardens hold behavioral data on 3.64 billion people.

But downstream is fighting back too. Amazon blocking Muse from operating on its website is retailers' instinctive response to "entry points being hijacked." If more large platforms ban agents, a big chunk of Muse's shopping use cases will be cut off—this is downstream countering the midstream, and 2026 has only just begun.

## Cycle position: capex overheating, ad demand moderate

View this chain in two segments. Upstream (chips, memory, power) sits at a cyclical peak: the four major cloud providers spent a combined USD 207.8 billion on capex in 2026Q3, up 115.5% year on year. The midstream platforms are growing steadily: Meta advertising rose 26%, Google 10.6%, Amazon 10.9%.

**The gap in speed between the two segments is where the risk lies.** Upstream is expanding capacity at 115%, while midstream is monetizing at 10%–26%; overcapacity and price declines in between are inevitable. Memory already rose 60%–70% in early 2026, and gains of that magnitude cannot persist for three years.

Implication for Meta: **it pays the bill for upstream overheating and benefits from midstream stability.** When the cycle peaks, its capex will fall first and its profit will recover first—but by then the market will already be worrying about demand.

## Its true position in the chain

In one sentence: **Meta is a tax collector in the midstream, but a taxpayer upstream.** Its gross margin is high because it shifts content production and distribution costs onto users; its free cash flow is low because it hands its profits over to chipmakers, utilities and builders.

This position is very comfortable when ad demand is strong — the tax rate can keep rising; it is very uncomfortable in an AI arms race — upstream can keep raising prices. **2026 is precisely the year the latter prevails.**

## A counterexample: why ad-tech companies are losing value instead

The only link in the chain that is depreciating is "ad tech and attribution." The reason is simple: Apple restricted tracking in 2021, Google is phasing out third-party cookies, and the platforms have brought attribution into their own closed loops (Meta's Conversions API, Google's Privacy Sandbox). **The value intermediaries can offer has been internalized by the platforms.**

This is good news for Meta—it has pocketed gross profit along the industry chain that once belonged to third parties; it is bad news for downstream advertisers—measurement power is monopolized by the platforms, and their bargaining power weakens further. Every dollar that shifts along the chain is a dollar someone loses.
