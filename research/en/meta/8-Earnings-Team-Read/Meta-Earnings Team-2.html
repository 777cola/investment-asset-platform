Earnings Deep Dive (Buffett) — Meta

The income statement is an opinion; the cash flow statement is a fact

Core view

Buffett asks only three things when reading financial statements: how much capital the business needs to operate, how much that capital earns, and what management does with the excess. Meta's answers are, respectively: more and more, cannot be calculated for now, and betting it on AI.

Capital intensity: from light to heavy over a decade

Capex was 15% to 20% of revenue in 2016, 36% in 2025 and about 55% in 2026. Over the same period gross margin fell from 86% to 79.3%, and operating margin fell from 45% to 20% (GAAP) and 35% (adjusted). Rising capital intensity and falling margins are two sides of the same coin.

One reason he bought Apple was that the company could grow without much capital. Meta is now the opposite: it needs an enormous amount of capital just to sustain growth, and the return on that capital has yet to be proven.

ROIC: A Good Number Being Diluted

At roughly USD 95 billion annualized adjusted operating profit and a 17% tax rate, with invested capital crudely estimated as equity of USD 225 billion plus interest-bearing debt of about USD 30 billion less cash of USD 82 billion, ROIC exceeds 50%, which is very high. But that denominator is expanding fast: USD 70 billion of incremental capex in 2026, and more in 2027.

The denominator grows while the numerator takes three years to show up; the financials in the intervening two years will look ugly. Buffett's approach is to wait for the numbers to appear rather than guess ahead.

Shareholder returns: from RMB 30 billion buyback to RMB 95 billion financing

From 2019 to 2025, Meta repurchased USD 20–30 billion of stock annually, the most aggressive of the Magnificent Seven. In the first nine months of 2026, buybacks were zero; dividends were still paid, about USD 1.3 billion per quarter, a yield of less than 0.4%.

Meanwhile, on October 26 it launched a USD 37.5 billion equity raise: USD 25 billion of common stock priced at USD 625, USD 12.5 billion of mandatory convertible preferred with a 6.5% coupon, plus a USD 60 billion ATM. Paying a 6.5% preferred dividend on one side and a 0.4% common dividend on the other — that spread is the market's pricing of its credit risk.

How would he value it?

A DCF is unusable because free cash flow is negative. The only option is normalized earnings times a reasonable multiple: assuming capex falls back to USD 9 billion in 2028, advertising returns to 15% growth, and depreciation is absorbed by revenue, normalized EPS comes to roughly USD 35 to 38; at 18 to 20x, fair value is USD 630 to 760.

But every assumption behind this math rests on 2028. Buffett would not pay today for normalization three years out unless the price first carries a 30% discount.

Conclusion

Close-read verdict: first-class asset, second-class capital allocation, third-class timing. He would concede this is a great company, then set his buy price below 15x normalized earnings — just over USD 500. At USD 606.73, it is out of range.
