Risk Assessment (Li Lu's perspective) — Meta

Six risks, two already materializing
Research date 2026/11/02  ·  Price $606.73 (-1.34%)  ·  Market cap About USD 1.58 trillion  ·  P/E (TTM) Approximately 21x

Core view

The risk is not the grand question of "will AI fail" but specific events to which a timing and a probability can be assigned. Of the six risks, unclear returns on capex and equity dilution are already playing out; regulation, macro and entry-point substitution are queued up; the probability of failure on the technology route is in fact not high—because what Meta is buying is compute and distribution, not a single model.

Two items have already occurred: for 2027 expenses, management gave only qualitative guidance of a "significant increase"; and the USD 37.5 billion equity financing priced on October 26, with the USD 60 billion ATM pending launch.
Category 1: Money problems (already happening)

Li Lu's first test of a company is whether the flow of money aligns with shareholder interests. Meta's problem is not spending but the way it spends: capex of USD 72.2 billion in 2025, USD 135–145 billion in 2026, and zero buybacks in the first nine months of 2026. Over the past decade this "buyback machine" retired 2%–3% of shares annually; now it runs in reverse, adding 6% a year.

Shareholders have gone from "receiving cash back" to "being asked to put money in" — a sufficient condition for a lower valuation centre.At USD 625 on October 26, 20% below the USD 777.59 of September 24, the company itself was selling stock on the way down — a detail that says more than any guidance.

Category 2: Rule-related problems (recurring)

In August Meta reached a settlement with the attorneys general of 52 states and territories worth up to USD 18.59 billion, payable over ten years, and switched teen safety settings to default-on; on 26 October the EU fined it a further EUR 798 million over advertising-technology antitrust, which Meta said it will appeal. The two differ in nature: the former permanently changes product rules (default restrictions for minors, parental authorisation), the latter is a one-off fine.

The product-rule change carries the larger long-term impact: teens are the future users of social products, and default restrictions will suppress this generation's time spent.That will not show up in next quarter's results; it will show up in the daily-active-user mix five years from now.

Third category: the entry-point problem (may not materialise)

If agents replace the feed as the primary entry point, Meta shifts from "tax collector" to "content supplier." That is the most lethal risk and the least certain one. Li Lu's approach is usually to ask "who owns the user relationship" — agents do not yet own the social graph; they own tasks. As long as person-to-person relationships remain in Meta's graph, the entry-point migration will not be a cliff event.

Viewed the other way, Meta is spending RMB 140 billion precisely to ensure it is not the one being migrated away from. The nature of this risk: when it does not materialize, you feel the money was wasted; when it does, you feel you spent too little.

Where could this risk assessment be wrong?

If Muse subscription revenue reaches the USD 10 billion scale in 2027 and cost efficiency genuinely improves tenfold, both risks—"unclear return on investment" and "equity dilution"—reverse at once: the capital raised turns into high-margin revenue, and dilution becomes value accretion. That is the optimistic scenario, to which I assign a 20%–25% probability.

Conversely, if I am underestimating advertising resilience and advertising can still grow 20% in 2027, the depreciation math works out, free cash flow turns positive in the second half of 2027, and the current price is cheap. So the conclusion of this risk assessment is not "dangerous" but "uncertain"—and when uncertainty is high, position size should be low, not the conviction behind the judgment stronger.

Conclusion

Risk assessment: manageable but not ignorable; two risks have already materialised. Where the money goes and equity dilution are negative facts verifiable today; regulation is a recurring cost; portal substitution is a long-term variable. Taken together, these risks do not support "avoid," but they are enough to support "do not buy at the current price."
