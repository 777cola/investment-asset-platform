Moat anatomy: four real, two fake — Meta

Core view

Meta is credited with six moats. Four are real—network effects, the data flywheel, switching costs and brand—and two are not: cost advantage and licences. Real moats protect revenue; fake moats cannot protect profit—that is the dilemma.

The real number one: network effects

3.64 billion DAU, with the social graph in Meta's hands. The defining trait of this moat is that cold start is impossible: a new platform can build a better product, but it cannot build "all your friends are on it." In 2022, when TikTok was at its most formidable, Meta copied the product with Reels and users did not leave — that is the proof.

But network effects have boundaries: they protect "users don't leave," not "users spend more time." DAU up 3% while time spent gets siphoned off by short video shows the moat is holding — but holding with difficulty.

The real second rule: the data flywheel

Ad conversion data and content engagement data together drive the recommendation model flywheel: the more users engage, the more accurate the model; the more accurate the model, the more valuable the ad inventory; the more valuable the inventory, the more capital available to buy compute. This flywheel is the fundamental reason Meta's unit price has risen continuously over the past five years.

When Apple restricted tracking in 2021, the market feared the flywheel would break. Meta instead offset the impact through its own Conversions API and on-platform closed loop, and unit price resumed its rise from 2023. The lesson: the data closed loop has proved more resilient than the market expected.

False: cost advantage and licences

Cost advantage is a negative. Google has TPU, Microsoft has Azure and Amazon has AWS — the same capex can be sold to external customers to earn back its cost, whereas every GPU Meta buys serves only its own ads and Muse. For the same USD 100 billion of investment, its unit cost is the highest. In-house MTIA can cover part of inference, but not training.

Licenses are less a moat than a liability. The teen-safety settlement (USD 18.59 billion), the EU antitrust fine (EUR 798 million) and future privacy legislation are not barriers to entry but recurring cost items.

The boundary of the moat: protects revenue, not profit

This is the single most important sentence in the entire piece. The moat lets Meta keep taking share in the ad market (growing 2.4x as fast as Google), but it cannot stop upstream price increases, depreciation or capex. A moat is a defensive tool, not a profit tool.

Valuing this company therefore takes two layers: the moat determines whether it survives; capital allocation determines what it is worth. On the first layer the answer is that it survives; on the second, that its value cannot be pinned down right now. The investment decision stalls at the second layer.

The Cost of the Walled Garden

A data flywheel presupposes that "users are active inside your walled garden." Meta's garden is vast, but regulators are prying bricks out of the wall: Apple limits tracking, the EU mandates data portability, and teen rules restrict data use.

Moat and regulation are two sides of the same coin. The bigger the garden, the more accurate the ads, and the more regulators pay attention; the tighter the regulation, the less data, and the less accurate the ads. This tension will not disappear—it will only tighten or loosen cyclically. 2026 is a year of tightening.

Addendum

A Munger-style follow-up: how many people will still remember the Facebook brand in ten years? Young people may not, but they remember Instagram and WhatsApp. Brand moats shift as products are replaced; Meta's advantage is that it owns multiple brands, its disadvantage that the lifespan of each is not up to it.

Another often-overlooked fact: the user base is aging. Facebook's core users are over 35, Instagram's 25 to 34, and younger users are mostly on TikTok and Snap. This is not the moat leaking, it is the riverbed sinking—older users retain well, but their advertising value has limited growth. In ten years this becomes a revenue problem.

Conclusion

Moat verdict: four real, two false. The real moats are enough to keep it from being displaced within a decade; the false ones leave it at a cost disadvantage in the AI arms race. This is not a company that will be beaten; it is one that its own spending could hold back.
