# Meta（META）— Duan Yongping Supplement

- Research date：2026/11/02
- Price：606.73 USD
- Market cap：About USD 1.58 trillion

## Notes

This piece restates the analysis in this report through the lens of Duan Yongping's publicly stated investment principles (circle of competence, benfen, stop doing list, rough estimation) and does not quote his personal views. Financial data are sourced from Meta's 2026Q3 earnings report; dilution calculations are in the m01 note.

## Q1: Is this a good business?

Good. Good on two counts: users come back on their own, and advertisers bid on their own. Few businesses worldwide can do both. A gross margin of 79.3%, a net margin of 30.6% (adjusted) and 3.64 billion daily active users — put those three numbers together and you have a top-tier business.

But I would add one thing: **a good business is not the same as a good investment.** A business is a business; a price is a price. Many people lose money not because they bought the wrong business, but because they bought it too expensive.

Compare it with Apple and the point becomes clear. Apple's business also runs on "users come back on their own, suppliers queue up", but Apple does not need to plow all its earnings back in every year, so its free cash flow is over 90% of profit. Meta's is negative this year. The two businesses are of similar quality; their cash quality differs by a notch. **Business quality determines whether you can hold for the long term; cash quality determines what it is worth.**

## Q2: Will it still be around in ten years?

Yes. People need to deal with people, and that need will not disappear. Facebook may grow old, Instagram may be diverted by other products, but "connecting people to people" will still be there ten years from now.

What is uncertain is **whether it will still be the one collecting the toll**. Ten years from now, if everyone uses agents to find things and buy things, the feed goes from being the front door to being a corridor. That change will not happen overnight, but once it does, it is hard to turn back. So the company is spending RMB 140 billion to buy a ticket to "keep standing at the door." I understand the logic; I make no judgment on the price.

## Question three: Can you understand it?

The advertising part I understand. Advertising is an attention business: whoever has an audience can sell it. That I get.

What I don't understand is that RMB 140 billion. Buying GPUs, building data centers, signing twenty-year power contracts, and it all ends up as Muse, as agents — what this thing looks like in three years, I can't say. **And what I can't explain, I won't bet big money on.** Not because it's bad, but because I don't know where it's good, or how good. If you can't understand it, don't touch it — that's discipline.

## Question 4: At what price to buy

A rough estimate: earnings per share of RMB 28 in 2027, and 20x is not expensive for a business like this, which gives 560. It is now 606, a little expensive. But that "little" is before dilution — it has just issued RMB 95 billion of stock, a 6% dilution, which turns the 28 into just over 26, and 20x that is around 530.

**So my price is roughly below 500. I will buy a little if it gets there, and wait if it does not.** Waiting is not a waste of time; waiting is part of investing. People who rush to buy usually fear missing out, and people who fear missing out usually lose money.

## Question 5: What is the biggest fear

The biggest fear is not that AI fails, but that **the money gets spent and the habits change anyway**. What does that mean? It means that to save compute and build agents, it damages its advertising products — users find them less usable, and advertisers find them less cost-effective. At that point it has lost on both fronts.

The second fear is people. When a company spends RMB 140 billion a year, there must be a very strong rationale, but only a handful of people know what it is. **If those few people judge wrong, no one can stop it.** I look closely at this in a company — not whether the boss is smart, but whether anyone dares to say "no".

There is another layer: over the past decade this company has been fined many times, totaling tens of USD billion. The fines themselves are not fatal, but they reveal one thing — **when it makes decisions, it does not take external rules very seriously.** That trait is an advantage during expansion and a risk in maturity.

## Question 6: If you must buy, how should you buy?

Then buy a little less, in several tranches, and treat it as a company you "half understand." If you only half understand it, use half a position — that is a very simple rule.

Also, do not check on it every day. With a company like this there is a new story every quarter — Muse launch, model release, fines, share issuance — and watching too closely makes your hands itch. **After buying, get on with your life and look again in five years.** If you cannot do that, it means you do not really understand it, and you should not buy it.

## Question 7: What should existing holders do?

Hold and do nothing. The reasoning is simple: the reason to sell should be "the company has deteriorated" or "the price is too expensive," not "it fell recently." Neither condition fully holds right now — the company has not deteriorated, with advertising still growing 26%; nor is the price too expensive, at 21x.

But if your cost basis is above 700, you need to think one thing through clearly: **did you buy it because you were bullish on advertising, or because you were bullish on Muse?** If the latter, then what you bought is an option, and a 20% drop in an option is normal — you should not console yourself with "value investing." Thinking this through matters more than deciding whether to sell.
