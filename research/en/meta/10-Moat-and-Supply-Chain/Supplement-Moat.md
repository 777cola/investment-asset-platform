## Position in the value chain: a midstream tax collector squeezed from both ends

Meta sits mid-chain: it buys compute upstream, where it has no pricing power, and sells advertising downstream, where it does. The position is asset-light and high-margin, but Meta controls physical resources at neither end—it does not make chips, generate power or build fibre.

In 2026 this structure worked against it. Upstream prices rose across the board—DRAM contract prices up 60%–70%, long-term power contracts locking in prices—while downstream advertiser budget growth was only around 10%. The platform in the middle got squeezed, and gross margin fell from 82.0% to 79.3%. This is not a decline in competitiveness but a redistribution of profits along the industry chain.

## The Real Boundaries of the Moat

Network effects and the data flywheel are hard moats because they cannot be bought. You can spend USD 100 billion on data centres, but you cannot buy the social graph of 3.64 billion people—as 2022 proved, when TikTok was at its most aggressive: Meta cloned the product with Reels and users did not leave.

But moats have boundaries: they protect revenue, not profit. Users will not watch more ads because Meta's capex is high, and advertisers will not pay more because its depreciation is heavy. The moat can therefore defend 26% revenue growth, but it cannot defend free cash flow.

## Three supply-chain bottlenecks, each of which will slow the pace of investment

First, HBM. DRAM contract prices rose 60%–70% in early 2026, HBM supply falls short of demand, and institutions expect the shortage to persist into 2027. Meta is not the largest buyer — Nvidia, Microsoft and Google rank ahead of it — and the order in which buyers secure supply determines deployment speed.

Second, power. Data centres cannot be built on money alone; they need substations, grid connections and 15- to 20-year power purchase agreements. Meta has signed USD 825 billion in lease and purchase commitments, most tied to compute and energy — essentially a deposit on "future electricity."

Third, construction timelines. A hyperscale data centre takes 18 to 30 months from site selection to power-up. Money spent in 2026 will only become usable compute in 2028 — which is why management says "greater flexibility" arrives only in 2028.

## Why does the cost advantage item score negative?

Google has TPUs built in-house over a decade, with unit inference costs far below buying GPUs, and its cloud business can sell idle compute; Microsoft has Azure, so the same capex serves both internal and external customers; Amazon has AWS, where capex turns directly into revenue.

Meta has none of this. Every GPU it buys serves only its own advertising and Muse, with no second outlet. Spend the same RMB 100 billion: Google recovers RMB 30 billion, while Meta recovers only incremental advertising — hence its cost advantage score of 4/10. In-house MTIA can fill part of the gap, but it covers inference only, not training.

## An underappreciated moat: advertisers' organisational inertia

Small and mid-sized merchants treat Meta as their default channel not because they signed a contract, but because their teams, creative assets, campaign strategies and attribution reports are all built around it. The cost of switching platforms is not money — it is relearning everything. That inertia only strengthens when budgets are tight: no one has time to run migration tests in the off-season.

It also explains why Google Ads grew only 10.6% while Meta grew 26% — not because Google's product got worse, but because advertisers prefer to put marginal budget where they can "see conversions directly." Inertia plus performance: together these two form the part of Meta that is hardest to replace.

## The most dangerous rival: Google

This is not a TikTok story. TikTok competes for time spent, and Meta has already held it off with Reels; TikTok's US position is itself unstable.

The Google threat is structural: Gemini (models), TPU (chips), cloud (the monetisation outlet), and Android and Chrome (distribution) — all four in place. For the same USD 20 billion of investment, Google's risk exposure is only half of Meta's, because it can sell compute to customers to recoup the cost. In the agent battle, Google also has search and email as two natural task entry points.

The second-layer threat is entry-point migration. If agents become the primary entry point, Meta's feed degrades from an entry point to a content source. That will not materialise within a year, but it is the only reason worth committing RMB 140 billion of capex — the bet is on not being pushed downstream by Google and OpenAI.

## Where will the moat be breached first?

The warning sign will not be advertising revenue; it will be user time. DAU of 3.64 billion grew just 3%. If that figure hits zero growth in any given year, user time declines first, and only then do ad impressions and pricing follow. That transmission chain lags by roughly four to six quarters, so DAU is the earlier indicator than ad revenue.

The second data point is teenagers. The settlement agreement requires teen accounts to have time limits enabled by default, which will reshape this generation's usage habits. When they become the core consumer group ten years from now, Meta's user mix will give you the answer.
