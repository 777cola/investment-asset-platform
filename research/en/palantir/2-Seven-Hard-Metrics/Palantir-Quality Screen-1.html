Quality is not the problem; price is

Seven hard metrics, six passed. Operating margin 47%, net margin 55%, revenue growth 93%, operating cash flow/net profit 117% and net cash of USD 9.2 billion are not marginal passes — they are crushing passes. The sole failure is equity dilution: share count up 2% a year, with stock-based compensation at 13.7% of revenue.

This screen exists to filter out companies whose business is itself broken. Palantir does not get filtered out here; on the contrary, it ranks among the top performers on these metrics. The problem is that none of the seven hard metrics governs price — a company that passes all seven can still cost investors half their money at 74x price-to-sales. Clearing the quality screen only earns a place in the valuation discussion, not a buy rating.

The sole item that fails: not the absence of a dividend, but reverse dilution

Palantir has paid no dividend since listing and has done no meaningful buyback—of the USD 1 billion authorisation granted in 2023, execution is far below what was authorised. On a shareholder-return screen it should be excluded, but I set criterion 7 to "equity dilution ≤1%/year" because its problem is more serious than not paying a dividend: it is not that it fails to return money, it is that it asks shareholders for another 2% every year.

Q2 stock-based compensation was USD 265 million, 13.7% of revenue. Annualised that exceeds USD 1 billion, equivalent to paying employees one-fifth of full-year free cash flow (Q2 annualised 4.88 billion). Tech companies routinely use stock to retain staff, and 13.7% is on the high side among peers (mature SaaS typically runs 8%–12%).

Is this not fatal? On its own, not fatal; combined with valuation, fatal. A 74x price-to-sales multiple assumes per-share value compounds 30% a year; 2% dilution a year turns 30% into 27.4%. Over six years that is a 15 percentage point gap—exactly the stretch between the neutral scenario and the current price.

Six more: not a squeaker, but a landslide

The 47% operating margin stands out most among these seven. In software, Salesforce runs an operating margin of about 20%, Snowflake is still near loss-making, and Databricks is private but publicly reports near break-even.Palantir has delivered the margin of an entire industry in a single company.

The 55% net margin needs a discount: net profit of USD 1.062 billion exceeds operating profit of USD 912 million, with the gap coming from interest and investment income on USD 9.2 billion of cash. That portion tracks interest rates and is not sustainable.True earning power is the 47% operating margin, which is also top-tier for the industry.

Operating cash flow at 117% of net profit shows the profit is real money, not receivables piled up. Customers are governments and large enterprises, paying in advance and on milestones, so cash comes in faster than profit. Net cash of USD 9.2 billion and almost no interest-bearing debt is especially valuable in a rate-hiking cycle—it does not need financing to survive.

The 93% revenue growth needs no elaboration. The caveat is sustainability: total customers grew only 24%, with growth mainly from existing customers raising orders. How long this runs depends on whether those 220 million-dollar contracts can keep upgrading to ten-million-dollar ones.

What this screen does: it filters out bad companies, not bad prices

All seven hard metrics are quality metrics; none touches valuation. That is by design—spend ten minutes screening out bad businesses first, and save the time for those worth a deeper look. On this step, Palantir's performance says: no need to screen further, go straight to deep research.

This is where investors most often err: seeing six of seven boxes ticked, they assume they have found a good target. Cisco in 2021, Lucent in 2000, Zoom in 2021—all were top of the class on the screen-out-bad-businesses test. Their quality problem was not in the financials; it was in the price.

So the conclusion of this module must be stated clearly: passing the screen-out-bad-businesses test ≠ a buy. Palantir's quality earns it a place on any core-holdings list, but a 74x price-to-sales ratio earns it a place on no buy list. The next step is not to build a position, but to put it on the watchlist and wait for the price.

Conclusion

Six of seven criteria pass; the sole failure is equity dilution, with SBC at 13.7% of revenue and share count growing 2% a year. On its own that is not fatal, but against a 74x price-to-sales ratio it is the last straw for per-share returns. Conclusion: the negative screen passes and company quality poses no risk, but this module grants no buy authorization. Should the SBC share fall below 10% and share count growth slow to within 1%, the fail can be reclassified.
