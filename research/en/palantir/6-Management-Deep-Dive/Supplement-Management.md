## Capital allocation: no dividend, no buyback, no M&A — cash is piling up on the balance sheet

Palantir's capital allocation over the past five years can be summed up in one line: everything stays inside the company. No dividend, no substantive buyback (a USD 1 billion authorization in 2023, with execution far below the authorized amount), and almost no M&A (only a handful of small technology acquisitions in its history). USD 9.2 billion in cash just sits there.

There are two ways to explain this. The charitable one: every dollar went into R&D and sales at a return far above buybacks—evidenced by R&D expense consistently above 15% of revenue and converted into 93% growth. The uncharitable one: management is unwilling to price its own stock in the open market, because any large buyback would be read as "management thinks the stock is cheap."

My judgment leans toward the latter. A company at 74x price-to-sales is right not to buy back, but a company whose cash equals 2% of market cap not even doing a token buyback shows that its view of its own stock price diverges from the sell side's. That says more than any insider-selling filing.

## Integrity and governance: three things you must know

First, the Class F super-voting rights. The three founders lock in nearly half the voting power through a special share class, and that voting power floats with their ownership stake. The effect: outside shareholders cannot change control, however they vote. Founder-led companies commonly adopt such structures, but here it leaves shareholders with only one lever — voting with their feet.

Second, internal tension from deep government ties. Contracts with ICE, the Department of Defense and law enforcement have repeatedly triggered employee protests and departures, most recently in 2025 as Maven-related contracts expanded. Karp's approach is to defend them publicly rather than duck. Morally contested, commercially correct — government business is the steadiest source of cash flow and the hardest to replace.

Third, the selling follows a plan; it is not stealth selling. Karp and Thiel both sell weekly or monthly through 10b5-1 plans, in full compliance. But "compliant" is not "bullish": when a CEO says publicly that "this is our best moment" while selling on a schedule, investors must judge for themselves which statement is closer to his real expectations.

## Succession and key-person risk: there is no number two to Karp

Palantir runs a "Karp external, Sankar internal, Cohen sales" structure. The division of labour is clear and has held for over a decade. But three things—external narrative, government relations and employee mobilisation—are heavily concentrated in Karp alone.

Were he to leave (health, politics or a board conflict), the company would not collapse: AIP's product strength, the government qualification barrier and customers' ontological dependence would all remain. But the "why Palantir" story would lose a piece, and much of the valuation rests on that story.That is the leverage of key-person risk at 74x price-to-sales: a 20% discount to the story is a 20% discount to market cap.

Sankar is the most likely successor—technically trained, familiar with government business and restrained in public. His first move would likely be to shift the narrative from Karp the individual to the product—good for the long term, bad for near-term valuation.

## Incentive structure: what the USD 1.1 billion pay package says

The Karp compensation package approved in 2024 caps out at about USD 1.1 billion, vesting over ten years against share-price milestones — one of the largest incentive designs among US-listed CEOs. The logic: "Karp only makes money if shareholders make money," which sounds perfect.

Look closely, though, at what it locks onto. The milestones are set on share price, not revenue, profit or ROIC. Management's incentive is therefore tied directly to market cap, not to value creation. At 74x price-to-sales, the two can diverge for a long time.

Now look at equity incentives across the workforce: RMB 265 million in a single quarter, 13.7% of revenue. That hands shareholders' money to employees at a scale exceeding USD 1 billion a year. For a company with net profit of RMB 326 million (TTM), it is equivalent to transferring a third of "profit" out in the form of stock. It does not show up in cash flow; it shows up in share count — 2% a year.
