## Capital allocation: no dividend, no buyback, no M&A — cash piling up on the balance sheet

Over the past five years, Palantir's capital allocation record can be summed up in one sentence: **everything stays inside the company.** No dividend, no substantive buyback (a USD 1 billion authorization in 2023, with execution far below the authorized amount), and almost no M&A (only a few small technology acquisitions in its history). USD 9.2 billion in cash just sits there.

There are two ways to explain this approach. The good explanation: it put every dollar into R&D and sales, at a return far above buybacks—the evidence being R&D expense consistently above 15% of revenue and converted into 93% growth. The bad explanation: management is unwilling to price its own stock in the open market, because any large buyback would be read as "management thinks the stock is cheap."

My judgment leans toward the latter. **A company at 74x price-to-sales is right not to buy back, but a company whose cash equals 2% of market cap not even doing a token buyback shows that its view of its own stock price diverges from the sell side's.** That says more than any insider-selling filing.

## Integrity and governance: three things you must know

**First, the Class F super-voting rights.** The three founders lock in nearly half the voting power through a special share class, and that voting power floats with their ownership stake. The effect: no matter how outside shareholders vote, control does not change. This is common at founder-led companies, but it means "voting with your feet" is the only lever shareholders have.

**Second, internal tension from deep government ties.** Contracts with ICE, the Department of Defense and law enforcement have repeatedly triggered employee protests and departures, and again in 2025 when Maven-related contracts expanded. Karp's approach is to defend them publicly, not duck. Morally contested, commercially correct — **government business is its steadiest source of cash flow and the part hardest to replace.**

**Third, the selling follows a plan; it is not stealth selling.** Karp and Thiel both sell weekly or monthly through 10b5-1 plans, fully compliant. But "compliant" is not "bullish": when a CEO says publicly that "this is our best moment" while selling on a schedule, investors must judge for themselves which statement is closer to his real expectations.

## Succession and key-person risk: this company has no number two to Karp

Palantir's organizational structure is "Karp external, Sankar internal, Cohen sales." The three have clear divisions of labor and have run this way for over a decade. But three things—external narrative, government relations, and employee mobilization—are highly concentrated in Karp alone.

If he left one day (health, politics, or conflict with the board), the company would not collapse—AIP's product strength, the government qualification barrier, and customers' ontological dependence would all remain. But the story of "why Palantir" would lose a piece, and a large part of this company's valuation is propped up by that story.**This is the leverage effect of key-person risk at a 74x price-to-sales ratio: a 20% discount to the story means a 20% discount to market cap.**

Sankar is the most likely successor—technically trained, familiar with government business, and restrained in public expression. His first move upon taking over would likely be "shifting the narrative from Karp the individual to the product," which is good for the long term and bad for near-term valuation.

## Incentive structure: what the USD 1.1 billion pay package says

The Karp compensation package approved in 2024 has a cap of about USD 1.1 billion, vesting over ten years against share-price milestones. It is one of the largest incentive designs among US-listed CEOs. The logic is that "Karp only makes money if shareholders make money" — which sounds perfect.

But look closely at what it locks onto. The milestones are set on share price, not revenue, profit or ROIC. **That means management's incentive is tied directly to "market cap", not to "value creation".** At 74x price-to-sales, the two can diverge for a long time.

Then look at equity incentives across the workforce: RMB 265 million in a single quarter, 13.7% of revenue. This hands shareholders' money to employees, at a scale exceeding USD 1 billion a year. **For a company with net profit of RMB 326 million (TTM), that is equivalent to transferring a third of "profit" out in the form of stock.** It does not show up in cash flow; it shows up in share count — 2% a year.
