## Layer one: revenue mix — an "American company"

US revenue was USD 1.573 billion, 81.3% of the total, up 115% YoY. Non-US revenue was USD 362 million, up about 33% YoY (backed out from the US growth rate). Put the two together and the company has become "a software contractor to the US government plus an AI deployment partner for large US enterprises," with overseas business an order of magnitude slower.

The segment split makes this clearer. US commercial was USD 764 million (+149%), a direct win from AIP; US government was USD 809 million (+90%), the compounding of credentials and track record. Together they are 81%, and part of the remaining 19% is allied projects such as UK NHS and NATO.

This structure explains two things. First, why it can charge a 47% operating margin — US government customers have the world's best ability to pay and renewal stability. Second, why its globalization narrative never materializes — governments in Europe, the Middle East and Asia all have their own calculations around data sovereignty, procurement processes and protection of local vendors. "US 81%" is the single most important line in this earnings report, more important than +93%.

## Layer two: gross profit and expenses—operating leverage is real

Gross margin is about 80%, slightly up from the same period last year. That is not the point. The point is the expense ratio: sales, R&D and G&A together are about 33%, versus about 46% in the same period last year (back-calculated). Revenue doubled while expenses rose only 45% — that is operating leverage.

Why were expenses held down? Because the sales model changed. It used to be six months on-site, custom development, trading headcount for contracts; after AIP it is a bootcamp — a prototype in five days, with customers adding use cases themselves. The sales expense ratio went from that of a consulting firm to that of a software company — that is the real mechanism behind the margin jump from 27% to 47%.

But mind the limits of this leverage. R&D is about 15% of revenue, already at a historical low; the sales expense ratio will keep falling, but only modestly. Further margin gains must come from revenue growth diluting costs, not from another model shift. In other words: 47% is probably near the high point of this cycle, not the starting point.

## Layer 3: Net profit and cash flow — read the two separately

GAAP net income was RMB 1.062 billion, a net margin of 54.9%, 7.8 percentage points above the operating margin. One reason alone explains the gap: interest income on USD 9.2 billion of cash (roughly USD 370 million annualized). Judge this company on operating profit, not net income.

Free cash flow was RMB 1.220 billion, a margin of 63.1%, 16 percentage points above the operating margin. Two items drive the gap: RMB 265 million of share-based compensation, a non-cash expense added back, and an increase in deferred revenue from customer prepayments. The first flatters the print; the second is real cash. Of the 63%, 13.7 percentage points is the share-based compensation add-back and the rest is customer prepayments — that structure matters more than the number itself.

The balance sheet is clean: USD 9.2 billion of cash, no material interest-bearing debt, no history of goodwill impairment. A company growing 93% that does not need to raise capital is a rarity among 2026 growth stocks.

## Tier four: orders — the only crack is here

Total contract value (TCV) of USD 3.373 billion, up 49% YoY, came in a full 44 percentage points below the 93% revenue growth rate. Contracts are the wellspring of revenue; when the wellspring grows slower than the drawdown, current revenue is eating into the stock.

The breakdown makes the cracks clear. US commercial TCV was USD 2.132 billion, +153%, with remaining performance obligations of USD 6.238 billion, +124% — this leg is extremely strong. The drags are government and international: government contract signing follows the budget cycle, and there were no large deals this quarter; international was already slow.

Large-deal activity in the quarter was not bad: 220 contracts above USD 1 million and 73 above USD 10 million. The problem is not deal size but total count — 220 large deals supported USD 3.373 billion of TCV, an average of USD 15.3 million each, showing these are mid-sized expansion deals and what is missing are the strategic mega-deals worth several hundred million dollars at a time. And historically, it was precisely strategic mega-deals that pushed US government revenue to new levels.
