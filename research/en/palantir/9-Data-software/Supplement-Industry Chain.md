## Upstream: compute and cloud, the most capital-intensive, with weakening bargaining power

The top layer is compute. AWS, Azure and GCP supply GPUs and storage; Palantir runs on top. This layer is defined by heavy capex, mid-range gross margins and very strong scale effects. In 2026, capex at North America's four largest cloud providers was raised again, with compute supply chasing demand.

Palantir has pricing power over the cloud providers for a simple reason: **customers can go multi-cloud, and so can Palantir.** Its Apollo layer was designed from the start to "deploy in any environment" — customer data centers, air-gapped environments, any cloud. This cloud-agnostic architecture keeps it from being locked in by any single vendor.

But the upstream is moving down the stack. Microsoft's Fabric bundles data integration, governance and BI into the default Azure package; AWS's Bedrock turns model invocation and agent orchestration into standard services. **They don't lack technology; they lack people who understand the customer's business — and that is precisely Palantir's moat.**

## Midstream: the product layer, and why it is the most profitable

What the ontology layer does, plainly put, is "translate": it translates the customer's business world into objects and relationships a machine can compute. What counts as an order, what counts as a fault, what qualifies as an anomaly, who has authority to approve — once these definitions are written down, every application above must follow them.

This is "semantic definition power." Once you hold it, the revenue structure becomes extremely stable: customers come to you for new scenarios, for new data sources, for AI. **Palantir's 62% adjusted operating margin and 63% free cash flow margin all stem from this position.**

Competition at this layer is not about technology but about "who gets into the customer's workflow first." Databricks attacks upward from the data entry point (has data, lacks business semantics); Palantir attacks downward from business semantics (has semantics, data comes via integration). **The 2026 landscape: Databricks wins with technical teams, Palantir wins with business owners.**

## Downstream: governments and large enterprises, weakest bargaining power but most stable budgets

Downstream customers fall into two groups. Government (defense, intelligence, law enforcement) has stable budgets, slow procurement and very high switching costs; large enterprises (manufacturing, energy, healthcare) have flexible budgets and move fast, but are ROI-sensitive.

Neither group has strong bargaining power, because substitutes are scarce. But their budget cycles differ: government runs on the fiscal year and the NDAA, enterprises on annual IT budgets. **When the two cycles are out of phase, revenue is smoother; when they align, volatility is amplified.** In 2026 both cycles point up together — the best possible setup.

One more downstream shift is worth noting: consultancies have begun delivering "AI implementation". Accenture and Deloitte are both building their own AI platforms, priced at half of Palantir. **They are not taking core customers; they are taking the accounts that "cannot afford Palantir" — precisely the pool that represents Palantir's future growth.**

## Cycle position: from "selling projects" to "collecting subscriptions" — currently midway

Palantir's transformation over the past five years is essentially a shift from "project-based contractor" to "subscription-based platform." In 2019, projects accounted for a high share of its revenue, and gross margin was dragged down by on-site labor; by 2026, subscription share has risen, gross margin has reached 80%, and operating margin has reached 47%.

Where does this transformation stand now? **About two-thirds of the way.** The evidence: customer count growth (24%) is far below revenue growth (93%), indicating that growth is driven by existing customers adding subscriptions; but government and international businesses remain project-based, weighing on overall TCV.

Cycle assessment: **it sits at the overlap of two cycles — "late-stage subscriptionization + early-stage AI demand."** The former gives it a margin floor, the latter gives it a growth ceiling. This combination has rarely appeared simultaneously in history — which is also why the market is willing to assign a 74x price-to-sales ratio. The problem is that neither cycle will rise forever.
