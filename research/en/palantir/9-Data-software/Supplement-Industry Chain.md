## Upstream: compute and cloud, the most capital-intensive, with weakening bargaining power

At the top sits compute. AWS, Azure and GCP supply the GPUs and storage; Palantir runs on top. The layer is defined by heavy capex, mid-range gross margins and very strong scale effects. In 2026, capex at North America's four largest cloud providers was raised again, with compute supply chasing demand.

Palantir holds pricing power over the cloud providers for a simple reason: customers can go multi-cloud, and so can Palantir. Its Apollo layer was built from the outset to "deploy in any environment" — customer data centers, air-gapped environments, any cloud. That cloud-agnostic architecture keeps it from being locked in by any single vendor.

But the upstream is moving down the stack. Microsoft's Fabric bundles data integration, governance and BI into the default Azure package; AWS's Bedrock turns model invocation and agent orchestration into standard services. They don't lack technology; they lack people who understand the customer's business — and that is precisely Palantir's moat.

## Midstream: the product layer, and why it is the most profitable

Put plainly, the ontology layer "translates": it maps the customer's business world into objects and relationships a machine can compute. What counts as an order, what counts as a fault, what qualifies as an anomaly, who has authority to approve — once these definitions are written down, every application above must follow them.

This is "semantic definition power." Once you hold it, the revenue structure becomes extremely stable: customers come to you for new scenarios, for new data sources, for AI. Palantir's 62% adjusted operating margin and 63% free cash flow margin both stem from this position.

Competition at this layer is not about technology but about "who gets into the customer's workflow first." Databricks attacks upward from the data entry point (has data, lacks business semantics); Palantir attacks downward from business semantics (has semantics, data comes via integration). The 2026 landscape: Databricks wins with technical teams, Palantir wins with business owners.

## Downstream: governments and large enterprises; weakest bargaining power, but the most stable budgets

Downstream customers split into two groups. Government (defense, intelligence, law enforcement) offers stable budgets, slow procurement and very high switching costs; large enterprises (manufacturing, energy, healthcare) have flexible budgets and move fast but are ROI-sensitive.

Neither group holds strong bargaining power, given the scarcity of substitutes. Their budget cycles, however, differ: government runs on the fiscal year and the NDAA, enterprises on annual IT budgets. When the two cycles are out of phase, revenue is smoother; when they align, volatility is amplified. In 2026 both cycles point up together — the best possible setup.

One further downstream shift deserves attention: consultancies have begun delivering "AI implementation". Accenture and Deloitte are both building their own AI platforms, priced at half of Palantir. They are not taking core customers; they are taking the accounts that "cannot afford Palantir" — precisely the pool that represents Palantir's future growth.

## Cycle position: midway through the shift from "selling projects" to "collecting subscriptions"

Palantir's transformation over the past five years amounts to a shift from project-based contractor to subscription-based platform. In 2019, projects accounted for a high share of revenue and on-site labour dragged on gross margin; by 2026, subscription share has risen, gross margin has reached 80% and operating margin 47%.

Where does that transformation now stand? Roughly two-thirds of the way. Customer count growth of 24% sits far below revenue growth of 93%, indicating that growth is driven by existing customers adding subscriptions; government and international businesses, however, remain project-based and weigh on overall TCV.

Cycle assessment: the company sits at the overlap of two cycles — late-stage subscriptionisation and early-stage AI demand. The former provides a margin floor, the latter a growth ceiling. That combination has rarely appeared simultaneously in history — which is also why the market is willing to assign a 74x price-to-sales ratio. The problem is that neither cycle will rise forever.
