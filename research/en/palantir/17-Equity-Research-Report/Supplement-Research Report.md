## Chapter 1 Company Overview

Palantir Technologies (NASDAQ: PLTR) was founded in 2003 by Peter Thiel, Alex Karp, Stephen Cohen, Joe Lonsdale and Nathan Gettings, and listed via direct listing in September 2020. It is headquartered in Denver and employs roughly 4,000 people, a large share of them "forward-deployed engineers."

Four product lines: Gotham (government and defense), Foundry (commercial), Apollo (cross-environment deployment) and AIP (AI platform launched in 2023). The core underlying asset is Ontology, which defines a customer's business objects, permissions and actions as a computable graph.

2026Q2: 1,049 customers (870 commercial) and revenue of USD 1.935 billion. The US accounts for 81.3%, split between US government at 41.8% and US commercial at 39.5%. The shares trade at USD 189.40, implying a market cap of USD 456 billion, −2.5% from the 52-week high of USD 194.17.

## Chapter 2 Industry Analysis

Data software splits into four layers: compute cloud (AWS/Azure/GCP), data infrastructure (Databricks/Snowflake), the ontology and decision layer (Palantir), and applications and consulting (Booz Allen/Accenture). Profit concentrates in the second and third layers, because those two layers hold the "power to define."

Palantir's addressable market is not "global IT spending" but "institutions that can afford USD 1 million a year for data integration" — roughly 5,000–8,000 worldwide. It now has 1,049 of them, a penetration rate of 15%–20%. At 6,000 institutions × USD 5 million average annual spend, TAM is about USD 300 billion, with current penetration at 2.7%.

Cycle position: government AI procurement and enterprise AI budgets are both trending up at the same time — the macro backdrop for 93% growth in 2026. Both cycles moving in the same direction also means the risk of them moving down together is underappreciated.

## Chapter 3 Business Model

Three words define Palantir: ontology, subscription, on-site. Ontology is the product — it translates the business world into a graph; subscription is the revenue model — annual fees with a high renewal rate; on-site is the sales method — engineers embed with the customer to rework processes.

Unit economics: TTM revenue of USD 6.162 billion ÷ 1,049 customers = USD 5.87 million average annual revenue per customer. Customers +24% YoY against revenue +93% — growth comes almost entirely from existing customers expanding their orders. US commercial remaining contract value of USD 6.238 billion, +124%, is the reservoir for the next three years.

The model's strength is extremely strong stickiness and pricing power (62% adjusted operating margin); its weakness is that expansion is constrained by delivery capacity, since each new customer requires a team stationed on-site for six months. AIP's bootcamp is changing this — a prototype in five days, with customers adding use cases themselves.

## Chapter 4 Financial Analysis

Revenue: Q1 USD 1.633 billion (+85%), Q2 USD 1.935 billion (+93%), up 18.5% QoQ and accelerating for a fourth consecutive quarter. Full-year guidance USD 8.150–8.158 billion (+82%).

Profit: GAAP operating profit USD 912 million (47.1% margin), adjusted USD 1.194 billion (61.7%). The expense ratio compressed to 33% from 46% a year earlier, reflecting the shift in the model from consulting to software. Net income of USD 1.062 billion (54.9%) includes about USD 90 million of interest income, which is not sustainable.

Cash flow and balance sheet: free cash flow USD 1.220 billion (63.1%); after deducting USD 265 million of stock-based compensation, shareholder earnings USD 955 million (49.3%). Cash USD 9.2 billion, no material interest-bearing debt and no history of goodwill impairment. The only red flag is orders: TCV USD 3.373 billion, +49%, trailing revenue growth by 44 percentage points.

## Chapter 5 Management

Alex Karp (CEO, co-founder, since 2003), Stephen Cohen (President), Shyam Sankar (CTO), Peter Thiel (Chairman), David Glazer (CFO, appointed 2024).

Capability: twenty-three years focused on one thing; AIP drove US commercial growth of +149% in two years, with no opportunistic M&A.Governance: Class F super-voting rights let the founders lock in close to half the voting power, leaving outside shareholders unable to change direction; stock-based compensation is 13.7% of revenue and share count grows 2% a year; Karp's 2024 pay package caps out at about USD 1.1 billion, unlocking on share-price milestones.

Capital allocation: no dividend, no meaningful buyback, no large M&A, with USD 9.2 billion of cash left on the balance sheet. At 74x price-to-sales, not buying back stock is the right call; but the absence of even a token buyback shows management's view of the share price diverges from the sell side's.

## Chapter 6 Growth Drivers

Four engines. ① AIP ramp: US commercial +149%, TCV +153% — the hardest one. ② Government order ladder: Maven-related contracts are in execution; the next-generation command and control system (NGC2) procurement is the next step up. ③ Customer expansion: of 1,049 customers, 220 contracts above USD 1 million were signed in the quarter and 73 above USD 10 million, showing add-on orders are deepening. ④ Internationalization: currently only 19% of the total, growing 33% — the weakest link, and the one with the most room.

Of the four engines, ① and ③ are already delivering, ② awaits validation, ④ has yet to start. The valuation assumes all four deliver.

## Chapter 7 Competitive Landscape

There is almost no peer at the same layer (C3.ai is an order of magnitude smaller). The threat comes from both ends: upstream from Microsoft (Fabric bundled into Azure, leveraging distribution and procurement convenience) and downstream from consultancies (Accenture and Booz Allen price at half, capturing the downmarket).

Microsoft is the most dangerous competitor because it competes not on product but on procurement habits — a CFO is more likely to approve "adding a module to an existing cloud contract" than "signing a new vendor."

Anduril is a structural threat: it binds software (Lattice) to hardware (drones, sentry towers). If DoD procurement logic shifts from "buying software" to "buying capability," Anduril is the biggest beneficiary. Overall moat score 7.6: government side 9, commercial side 6.

## Chapter 8 Risk factors

Ranked by probability of "killing this investment": ① Valuation de-rating (60%) — at 74x price-to-sales, neutral −37%, bear −63%, no company problem required; the stock fell 85% on its own from 2021 to 2022. ② Government budget and concentration (20%) — the US government accounts for 41.8%, and NDAA appropriations and procurement rules are external variables. ③ Getting "bundled" (12%) — cloud vendors make the capability a default feature, or an open-source ontology framework emerges. ④ Equity dilution (5%) — 13.7% SBC, 2% annual dilution. ⑤ Governance and key-person risk (3%) — voting power locked up, dependence on Karp.

One further point: this report may be wrong on the discount rate. If the risk-free rate declines structurally over the long term and the market's discount rate for growth stocks falls systematically, 24x price-to-sales could become 36x and the neutral scenario would be USD 180. That is a macro assumption, not a company judgment.

## Chapter 10 Valuation

Use P/S rather than P/E: GAAP net income is distorted at both ends by interest income and stock-based compensation, and a 140x P/E carries too much variance.

Three scenarios: bear case 10.5 billion × 16x = USD 70 (−63%); base case 11.8 billion × 24.5x = 289 billion = USD 120 (−37%); bull case 13 billion × 36x = USD 195 (+3%).

Two independent paths cross-check the base case: ① 2027: 11.8 billion × 24.5x = 289 billion; ② 2032 perfect execution (45 billion revenue × 34% net margin × 30x = 459 billion) discounted 6 years at 8% = 289.2 billion. The two paths differ by 0.1%, confirming that USD 120 is calculated, not guessed.

## Chapter 11 Investment Recommendations

Rating: Avoid. Target price USD 120 (Neutral, 12 months).

Rationale: a good business with real profits and a wide moat (government side), but 74x P/S already discounts six years of flawless execution. The bull case offers just 3% upside, the base case −37% and the bear case −63%; at these odds, being right or wrong does not matter.

Three conditions for reassessment: ① P/S (TTM) ≤30x (about USD 80); ② TCV growth back above revenue growth (or TCV/revenue ≥1.5x on an upward trend); ③ non-US revenue growth rising from 33% to above 50%. ① is a hard condition; ② and ③ are pluses.
