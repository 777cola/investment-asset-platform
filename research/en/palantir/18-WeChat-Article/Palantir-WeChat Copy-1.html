Title: A company growing 93% with 47% margins — why I rated it "Avoid"

The conclusion first: this is the best earnings report I have read this year, and also the most expensive stock I have looked at. Both are true at once, and there is no contradiction.

Palantir's Q2 revenue was USD 1.935 billion, up 93% year on year. GAAP operating margin 47%, free cash flow margin 63%. On any software company, those three numbers would be historic. More remarkable still, it did not jump on one big deal — it is still accelerating sequentially: USD 1.633 billion in Q1, USD 1.935 billion in Q2.

US government contributed USD 809 million, +90% year on year; US commercial USD 764 million, +149%. Commercial growth is 1.65x government growth — evidence it is no longer just a company feeding off government budgets.

But the report hides a more interesting number: total customers grew only 24%, while revenue grew 93%.

That arithmetic admits only one explanation — existing customers are piling on orders. US commercial remaining contract value is USD 6.238 billion, +124% year on year, more than three times current-period revenue. In plain terms: most of next year's money is already locked into contracts.

The good news: the product genuinely solves a problem. No company doubles its spend in a year just to flatter a vendor. The bad news: it also means a narrow customer base — 1,049 customers support a USD 456 billion market cap, or USD 430 million per customer on average. Any budget shift among a group of large customers gets magnified by the market cap into double digits.

Now that 47%.

The number is real, and it already deducts USD 265 million of stock-based compensation. In software, Salesforce runs about 20% operating margin, Adobe about 35%, Microsoft about 45%. It ranks in the top three.

But the report also shows a 55% net margin, higher than the operating margin. At first glance impressive; in reality — USD 9.2 billion of cash sits in the bank earning USD 370 million a year in interest. 8% of net profit is deposit interest, not software sales.

So value this company on operating profit, not net profit. A one-point rate cut turns that 55% into 48% immediately.

There is another crack, hidden in the bookings.

Total contract value USD 3.373 billion, +49% year on year; revenue +93% year on year. Contracts are the water source, revenue is the water drawn — the source is growing at half the rate of draw, meaning current revenue is eating into the stock.

The breakdown is clear: US commercial contract value +153%, extremely strong; government and international dragged the total down. The quarter signed 220 contracts above USD 1 million, averaging USD 15.3 million each — all expansion deals, none of the USD 100-million-plus strategic mega-deals.

This is not a crisis, but it is the thing to watch most closely over the next two quarters.

Right, that covers the business. Now the price, which is the sole reason for my "Avoid".

The October 5 close was USD 189.40, market cap USD 456 billion, price-to-sales 74x. What do peers trade at? Microsoft 12x, Snowflake 13x, C3.ai 3x. It is six to twenty times its peers.

Whether that is expensive needs no gut feeling — just do the math.

Assume flawless execution: USD 8.15 billion revenue in 2026, then 33% average annual growth for six years to USD 45 billion by 2032; 34% net margin; a 30x P/E. Market cap = 450 x 34% x 30 = USD 459 billion.

Today's market cap is USD 456 billion. Six years of flawless execution, and the market cap goes nowhere.

Add 2% annual share count growth (stock issued as equity compensation): 2.71 billion shares in 2032, USD 169 per share — 11% below today's USD 189.40.

Another angle: discount the 2032 USD 459 billion back at 8%, present value USD 289.2 billion, USD 120 per share. That is my base case, 37% below the current price.

The bull case? USD 13 billion revenue in 2027, market still paying 36x sales — a share price of USD 195, 3% upside.

3% up, 37% down, 63% down in the extreme case.

This payoff structure does not require you to be right or wrong — only to stay out. Because if you are right (the company does execute flawlessly), you make 3%; if you are wrong (growth drops to 40%), you lose 37%. That is not investing, that is buying a lottery ticket that must hit every number.

Some will ask: with a company this good, won't waiting for a pullback mean missing out?

My answer: missing out does not cost you money; buying wrong does. However good a company is, the wrong price makes it a losing trade — Coca-Cola in 1998 was a fine company throughout, and anyone who bought at 40x earnings made no money for a decade.

So my move is simple: no buy, no short, put it on the first line of the watchlist. Every quarter, watch four numbers: revenue growth, contract-to-revenue ratio, operating margin, stock-based compensation as a share of revenue.

When to buy? When price-to-sales falls below 30x, roughly USD 80. At that point, even if growth drops to 20%, I still make money.

And if it never gets there? Then keep waiting. Investing does not require answering every question.
