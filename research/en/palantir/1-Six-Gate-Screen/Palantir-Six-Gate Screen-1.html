Six-Gate Screen — Palantir

Palantir (PLTR) | 01 · Six-Gate Screen
Research date 2026/10/05  ·  Price $189.40 (+0.34%)  ·  Market cap About USD 456 billion  ·  PS(TTM) Approximately 74x
← Back to the hub

Core call: business scores 9, price scores 1 — combined, not enough

Six gates cleared, 35 points (out of 60), Avoid. The first three gates are all but uncontested: the business explains in one sentence, its 47% GAAP operating margin has no peer in software, and all three moats are real. The score starts slipping at gate four — Karp's product judgment earns 9 points, while 13.7% stock-based compensation cuts it to 6. Gate five yields just 1 point: a USD 456 billion market cap on 74x TTM P/S leaves no discounted cash flow model standing. Gate six earns 3 points, because the only reason to buy is "it will get more expensive."

This report questions the price, not the company. Run the numbers to 2032: even at USD 45 billion in revenue, a 34% net margin and a 30x exit multiple, the market cap six years out matches today's, for a zero annualized return; add 2% annual share count growth and each share is worth 11% less. Perfect execution buys you nothing more than breaking even — that is not a price worth investing at.

Gate one: the business can be summed up in a sentence, but truly understanding it is not easy

What Palantir does, in one sentence: it links a client's data scattered across dozens of systems into a single "Ontology", defines business objects, permissions and actions on that graph, and then lets AI run decisions on top of it. Governments use it to find intelligence leads and schedule logistics; enterprises use it for production scheduling, fraud detection and supply-chain management. The charging model is subscription plus implementation projects, with contracts of three years or more and high renewal rates.

I give it 8 rather than 10 because "understanding the product" and "understanding why it can charge so much" are two different things. A large chunk of its revenue comes from on-site engineers rewriting client workflows — that is closer to high-end consulting than to standard software. Judging whether it will still be here in ten years means judging whether the "ontology layer" becomes a default feature of the cloud vendors. I cannot answer that question with full confidence, so I deduct 2 points.

But it passes the circle-of-competence test. No commodity prices, no inventory, no regulated pricing, no FX exposure — only three variables: number of customers, budget per customer, renewal rate. All three can be tracked quarterly.

Second hurdle: a 47% GAAP operating margin — no other software company comes close

2026Q2 revenue USD 1.935 billion, +93% YoY; GAAP operating profit USD 912 million, a 47.1% margin; adjusted operating profit USD 1.194 billion, a 61.7% margin; free cash flow USD 1.220 billion, a 63.1% margin. Put those three together and you get "margins a company growing 93% should not have."

The reason is no mystery: software is built once and replicated countless times, so marginal cost tends toward zero, and once a Palantir customer goes live, the incremental cost of adding modules the following year is minimal. Revenue was USD 1.633 billion in Q1 (+85%) and USD 1.935 billion in Q2 (+93%), a single-quarter QoQ of +18.5% — and that QoQ is accelerating, which tells you this is not a one-off step-up from a large contract.

On the "Rule of 40" it scores 156 (growth 93 + free cash flow margin 63). Few software companies even reach 60. So the second gate scores 9, with 1 point deducted on gross margin — about 80%, unremarkable against the 75%–85% range for pure SaaS, because it carries substantial upfront on-site staffing costs.

Gate three: three genuine moats, one of which is exposure rather than a barrier

Security clearance 9/10. Deployable in air-gapped environments and cleared to handle classified data — a barrier that alone shuts out most software companies. This is not technology but credentials plus history, and new entrants cannot buy it at any price.

Ontology 8/10. Once a client's business logic, metric definitions and permission structures are encoded in this graph, switching vendors means redoing the past five years of process. Rebuild cost is measured in years, not months.

Migration cost 8/10. With on-site teams plus custom models, no one inside the client fully understands how the system runs. The hardest factor to quantify, but the most useful at the renewal negotiating table.

A cold shower on the fourth point: "government relationships" are not a moat, they are concentration. The US government accounts for 42% of revenue (USD 809 million) and, including international government clients, government overall is more than half (rough estimate). A moat means the client cannot leave you; depending on one client group for 40% of revenue means you cannot leave it. In a year of budget tightening, both turn against you at once.

Gate 4: Karp score of 9, cut to 6 by equity incentives

Alex Karp is a rare CEO in this era who genuinely has conviction. AIP went from a concept in 2023 to US commercial revenue up 149% YoY in 2026Q2 — in two years. He did not pursue opportunistic M&A, did not cut R&D to protect short-term EPS, and his public remarks are harsh but consistent. There are not many people like this in Silicon Valley.

The deduction concerns alignment of interests. Q2 stock-based compensation was USD 265 million, 13.7% of revenue; share count rose about 2% over the year. That is, for every 100 of revenue the company earns, 13.7 goes to employees in stock; shareholders are diluted 2% a year.For a company at 74x price-to-sales, 2% dilution mathematically equals USD 9 billion of market cap evaporating out of thin air every year.

Karp's and the core team's sales are executed under 10b5-1 plans, procedurally clean. But when a company's CEO says "this is the best moment in our history" while selling a fixed amount every week, investors should at least acknowledge:his expected return from this company over the next six years is probably not as high as the sell-side reports suggest.That is where the 6 comes from.

Gate five and six: lay out the numbers plainly — only simple arithmetic remains

Skip the DCF; the arithmetic is simpler. Assume flawless execution: USD 8.15 billion of revenue in 2026, then +33% a year for six years to reach USD 45 billion in 2032; a 34% net margin (below today's 55%, as interest income is unsustainable and taxes will normalise); and a 30x P/E. Market cap = 45 × 34% × 30 = USD 459 billion.

Today's market cap is USD 456 billion. Six years of flawless execution, and the market cap goes nowhere. Factor in 2% annual share count growth and 2032 brings 2.71 billion shares, or USD 169 per share, 11% below today's 189.40. Annualised return: −1.9%.

Another angle: discount the 2032 figure of USD 459 billion back at 8% and the present value is USD 289.2 billion, or USD 120 per share — this is the report's neutral case, 37% below the current price. Buyers today are therefore paying a 58% premium for "flawless execution."

Gate 5 scoring 1 point and Gate 6 scoring 3 points both follow from this arithmetic. Margin of safety means "price below value"; here price is 58% above value, and there is nothing to debate.

Conclusion

Six gates, 35 points, Avoid. If there is a veto item, there is only one: margin of safety. Three items to verify are listed here; they do not change the conclusion, but they determine when to revisit: ① P/S falls back below 30x, around USD 80; ② order TCV growth again exceeds revenue growth, and non-US revenue growth rises from 33% to above 50%; ③ stock-based compensation as a share of revenue falls from 13.7% to below 10%. If two of the three appear, the 35 points must be recalculated.
