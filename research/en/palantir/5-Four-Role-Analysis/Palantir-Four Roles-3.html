Industry Research (Munger's Lens) — Palantir

Data software: who does the business structure favor
Research date 2026/10/05  ·  Price $189.40 (+0.34%)  ·  Market cap About USD 456 billion  ·  PS(TTM) Approximately 74x
← Back to the hub

Core thesis

Map the industry chain and the answer to where the money sits is clear: the closer to decision-making, the higher the gross margin; the closer to compute, the heavier the capital. Palantir sits at the ontology and decision layer — the highest-gross-margin layer, and the one most exposed to being squeezed from both upstream and downstream.

Munger asked only two questions of any industry: why can this layer capture the industry's economics? And why won't upstream or downstream players take that money? Palantir's answer to the second is weakening.

Industry structure: three tiers, with profit concentrated in the middle

The first layer is compute and cloud — AWS, Azure, GCP. Capex is enormous, gross margins run 35%–65%, and scale is what makes money. The second layer is data infrastructure — Databricks, Snowflake, gross margins 65%–75%, monetising the "data entry point." The third is the ontology and decision layer, where Palantir sits, at gross margins of about 80%. The fourth is application and consulting delivery — Booz Allen, Accenture, gross margins 15%–25%, monetising headcount.

Why do profits concentrate in the second and third layers? Because those two layers do the defining: what a customer is, what an order is, what counts as an anomaly. Once you hold the power to define, downstream players can only act according to your definitions, and upstream players can only supply you compute. This is the most classic structural advantage in software: whoever defines the semantics collects the rent.

Government money works differently. The US federal IT budget is about USD 110 billion a year (public budget basis), a large share of which is services procurement, priced per person-day at 20% gross margin. Palantir can carve a 62% adjusted margin out of this pool because it redefined itself from contractor to platform. That redefinition is its greatest achievement of the past decade.

TAM: large, but not as large as imagined

Sell-side TAM for a company like this is usually framed as "USD 5 trillion in global IT spending" — a meaningless number. Palantir's addressable market is better defined as large institutions that can afford on-site implementation: roughly 30,000 to 50,000 government agencies and large enterprises with more than 2,000 employees worldwide, of which perhaps only 5,000 to 8,000 can spend more than USD 1 million a year on data integration. It now has 1,049 customers, implying penetration of roughly 15%–20%.

That penetration rate explains two things: first, there is still room to grow (more than 4,000 remaining); second, growth will naturally decelerate — the further it goes, the larger the customers and the slower the decisions. Munger would say: "A market with only 5,000 customers cannot support a USD 45.6 billion market cap unless every customer pays USD 100 million." The current average is USD 5.87 million, a 17x gap.

Competitive structure: pressure from upstream is the most dangerous dynamic

Competition from the same layer is less intense than imagined. C3.ai targets the same customers at an order of magnitude smaller scale; Databricks is strong in the data lakehouse but weak at turning data into business action; Snowflake is strong in analytics but does not touch decision execution. The truly formidable threat comes from upstream.

Microsoft's Fabric bundles data integration, governance and BI into Azure's default package — customers "already have it" and do not need to sign another Palantir. This is not a product battle but a procurement-habit battle — a CFO is more likely to approve "adding a module to an existing cloud contract" than "signing a new vendor."

Munger's verdict would be: on the government side it is irreplaceable within a decade (security clearances plus track record); on the commercial side its position is more fragile than the market believes (cloud vendor bundling plus open-source alternatives). And commercial is precisely the half where it grows fastest and on which its valuation most depends.

Government budgets and the AI capex cycle are overlapping

Revenue rides two cycles. On the government side, watch defence budgets and AI procurement priorities—both trending up in 2026, the backdrop for its +90% government business. On the commercial side, watch enterprise AI budgets: we are now in the "pilot to production" phase, where budgets are ample but decisions are cautious.

Both cycles now point up in the same direction—the best of times. But cycles moving up in tandem also mean the risk of them moving down in tandem is underappreciated. If defence budget growth slows in 2027 and enterprise AI budgets shift from "must do" to "wait and see," both engines decelerate at once—and a 74x price-to-sales ratio leaves no room for that scenario.

Conclusion

Industry view: It sits at the highest-gross-margin layer of this chain, and at the layer most exposed to being squeezed from both sides. Its structural advantage on the government side is secure for a decade; on the commercial side, cloud vendors are amplifying its distribution disadvantage. The TAM supports five years of growth, not a market cap of RMB 45.6 billion. Munger would add: in this industry, the ones who end up making money are usually not the best at technology but the strongest at distribution.
