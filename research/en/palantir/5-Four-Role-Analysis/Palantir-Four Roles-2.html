Financial analysis (Buffett's perspective) — Palantir

Is the 47% operating margin real, and where did the money go?
Research date 2026/10/05  ·  Price $189.40 (+0.34%)  ·  Market cap About USD 456 billion  ·  PS(TTM) Approximately 74x
← Back to the hub

Core thesis

Conclusion first: the 47% GAAP operating margin is real, the 63% free cash flow margin is broadly real, and the 55% net margin is illusory. The gap across the three reflects differences in calculation basis, not fraud.

For a growth stock with a USD 456 billion market cap, the statements are unusually clean: no interest-bearing debt, no history of goodwill impairment, no complex off-balance-sheet structures, and short receivables days. Buffett would like this balance sheet, then frown at the share-based compensation in the income statement.

Income statement breakdown: 80% gross margin to 47% operating margin

Q2 revenue was RMB 1.935 billion, with gross margin of about 80% and gross profit of about RMB 1.55 billion. The remaining RMB 640 million covers three expense lines — sales, R&D and G&A — an expense ratio of about 33%. That is a healthy structure: a company growing 93% typically plows more than 60% of gross profit into sales and R&D to buy that growth, whereas Palantir spent only 33% and still delivered 93%.

Why can it do this? Because its sales model is product-led plus bootcamp: customers come to it, test a working product in five days, then sign a contract. Product strength substitutes for sales spend. This is the most valuable structure in software, harder than any moat narrative.

Stock-based compensation of RMB 265 million sits in expenses, 13.7% of revenue. That means the 47% operating margin already deducts stock-based compensation — more honest than most tech companies. The adjusted 62% adds that 13.7% back; readers need to know which version they are looking at.

55% of net profit is inflated: interest income and tax rate

GAAP net income was USD 1.062 billion, a 55% net margin and 8 percentage points above the operating margin. Only one accounting explanation fits: non-operating income exceeds interest expense plus tax. The source is USD 9.2 billion of cash—at a rough 4% short-end rate, that is about USD 370 million of annualised interest income, or roughly USD 90 million a quarter.

In other words, about 8% of net income is "deposit interest," not "profit from selling software." A 100 bp decline in rates cuts this profit by USD 90 million a year, a 2.8% hit to net income. Not fatal, but enough to make the 140x P/E denominator less reliable.

The tax rate is also low. Because of accumulated losses and R&D credits, the company's effective tax rate has long run below the 21% statutory rate. As profit scales and the credits are used up, the tax rate will return above 20%, and net margin will naturally revise down 3–5 percentage points. This is not a risk; it is arithmetic.

Cash flow and balance sheet: an almost flawless picture

Free cash flow of RMB 1.22 billion, a 63.1% margin, 16 percentage points above the operating margin. Two things drive the gap: stock-based compensation is a non-cash expense (added back), and deferred revenue is rising (customer prepayments). Of the two, the first flatters the accounts; the second is real cash.

Balance sheet: RMB 9.2 billion in cash, interest-bearing debt essentially zero, net assets estimated at RMB 8–9 billion. The company could lose money for ten straight years without raising capital—though it is not losing money now. Buffett would underline the point: in a rising-rate cycle, a net-cash company has the freedom of not having to ask anyone for anything.

Capital allocation, however, raises questions. The USD 9.2 billion in cash goes neither to buybacks (of the USD 1 billion authorization in 2023, very little was executed), nor to dividends, nor to large acquisitions. Management's explanation: "keeping it for strategic opportunities." At 74x price-to-sales, every dollar of buyback buys its own stock at a high price, so not buying back is correct; but it also shows management itself does not think the current price is cheap.

ROE roughly 35%–40% (TTM net profit of RMB 3.26 billion ÷ net assets of RMB 8–9 billion), and ROIC higher still, given no debt and almost no working capital needed. This is the kind of return on capital Buffett would score highly, except the price has already discounted all of it.

Conclusion

Financial quality conclusion: The profits are real, the cash flow is real, and the balance sheet is clean. Three items need a haircut: net profit includes roughly USD 370 million of annualised interest income, the effective tax rate will normalise, and equity incentives dilute 2% of share capital a year. Buffett would approve of these statements, then offer his usual line — "A good company, but the price has already taken the next ten years of returns away from me."
