Team Lead summary — Palantir

Four roles argued for three hours, and in the end there was only one point of disagreement
Research date 2026/10/05  ·  Price $189.40 (+0.34%)  ·  Market cap About USD 456 billion  ·  PS(TTM) Approximately 74x

Core thesis

The reports submitted by the four roles reach an identical conclusion: avoid. But consensus does not mean no disagreement—they diverge sharply on "what is this company worth in three years" and not at all on "should we buy today."

This summary is for decision-making, not for reading: three points of disagreement, the strength of evidence behind each, my ruling, and one trigger condition that would overturn the conclusion.

Disagreement one: how many more quarters can 93% growth sustain

The business role (Duan Yongping's lens) argues growth can run for four or more quarters, based on US commercial TCV +153% and remaining contract value +124%, with the reservoir more than three times current revenue. The financial role (Buffett's lens) argues growth must decelerate within two quarters, based on total TCV up only 49% and customer count up only 24%, with revenue eating into the existing base.

Verdict: the financial role is right. The two sets of numbers do not conflict; they simply look at different pools. The US commercial pool is filling (TCV +153%), while the government and international pools are draining (total TCV +49%). Blended growth will fall first, because government and international account for nearly 60% of revenue. Evidence strength for this call: medium-high, verifiable in the Q3 results.

But this disagreement does not affect the conclusion. Even if growth holds at 90% for four quarters, 2027 revenue would be just USD 11.8–13.0 billion, landing between neutral and optimistic, corresponding to USD 120–195. The best case offers 3% upside.

Disagreement two: how wide is the moat, really

The industry role (Munger lens) argues the commercial-side moat is narrower than the market believes, citing bundling and distribution by cloud vendors. The risk role (Li Lu lens) argues the ontology layer is genuine infrastructure—wide and durable. The commercial role sits in between: wide on the government side, narrow on the commercial side.

Verdict: assess by customer type separately; do not assign a single composite score. Government side 9 points (security clearance + fifteen-year track record, no one can replace it); commercial side 6 points (technology replicable, distribution weaker than Microsoft's, switching costs lower than government). Weighted, 7.5 points—not a low score, but below the "sole winner" implied by market pricing.

Evidence strength: high (the qualification barrier on the government side is verifiable, and 24% customer-count growth on the commercial side corroborates the difficulty of expanding the customer base).

Divergence 3: How much of this price is narrative

All three roles agree it is expensive, but their judgments on how expensive differ by a factor of two. The Buffett lens says 37% overvalued (base case); the Munger lens says 60% overvalued (multiple reversion to 24x); the Li Lu lens says roughly 50% overvalued (ten-year DCF discounting).

Verdict: adopt 37%, because it is the only calculation with an explicit chain of assumptions.2032 revenue of RMB 45 billion × 34% net margin × 30x = RMB 459 billion, discounted back to today at 8% = RMB 289.2 billion = USD 120. Every step in this chain can be challenged, but every step can be verified.The other two figures are larger but rest on coarser assumptions, so they are discarded.

One honest caveat: if the risk-free rate returns sustainably below 3% and the market's discount rate for growth stocks falls systemically, 24x sales could become 36x, and the base case would then be USD 180.This is the report's largest uncontrollable variable, and the only thing that could make "avoid" wrong.

The consensus: why all four say don't buy

Not because the company is bad. All four rated the business above 8 out of 10, management 6–7, and the moat 7–9. The disagreement is all in the details.

Where they agree is on one piece of arithmetic: upside of 3% in the optimistic case, −37% in the base case, −63% in the bear case. Under this payoff structure, being right or wrong does not matter—you are right (the company does execute perfectly), you make 3%; you are wrong (one deceleration), you lose 37%. Faced with these odds, the rational choice for anyone is not to participate.

There is another way to state this arithmetic: the current price demands that you be right every single time over the next six years, and the return only covers inflation. That is not investing; it is buying a lottery ticket that must hit every number.

Three lines for decision-making

Point one: Do not build a position; put it at the top of the watchlist. Three metrics to watch — quarterly TCV growth, non-US revenue growth, and stock-based compensation as a share of revenue. Reassess when the first two improve and the third declines.

Point two: If already held, trim to a weight that does not affect the portfolio (≤2%); set no price stop, set a thesis stop. Thesis stop defined as: TCV growth below half of revenue growth for two consecutive quarters, or US commercial revenue growth falling below 50%.

Point three: Fix in writing "what price to buy at"; do not decide on the fly. A 30x P/S corresponds to about USD 80; 20x to about USD 55. If it gets there, redo the research; if not, do not watch the tape. Making decisions off the candlestick chart is the standard path to losing money in this kind of stock.

Conclusion

Four roles, one conclusion: avoid, watch. The disagreement centers on "how long growth can run" and "how wide the moat is," but neither changes the payoff structure—the best case offers 3% upside. Only one thing would overturn the conclusion: a systematic decline in discount rates that makes the market willing to award growth stocks a price-to-sales ratio above 36x over the long term. Until then, stay out.
