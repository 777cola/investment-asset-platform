Business analysis (Duan Yongping's perspective) — Palantir

What is this business actually selling, and why do customers pay?

Core thesis

In one sentence: it sells "turning chaos into executable decisions." The pain point for governments and enterprises is not a lack of data; it is that data sits across twenty systems, definitions conflict with one another, and no one dares to sign off. Palantir connects this mess into a single ontology, giving decisions one authoritative version. This pain point is valuable, and the larger the organization, the more valuable it becomes.

The business has excellent economic characteristics: build once, replicate many times, and the marginal cost of adding modules after a customer goes live is close to zero. But note that part of its revenue is on-site manpower—this is the biggest difference from pure SaaS, and the reason gross margin is only 80% rather than 85%.

Why are customers willing to pay this price?

The government-side logic is "you don't dare not buy." Intelligence integration, logistics scheduling, battlefield situational awareness—in these scenarios, the cost of a single mistake is human life or the failure of an operation. Palantir's Gotham has run in these scenarios for fifteen years, from the Iraq battlefield to today's joint operations command. The cost of a customer switching out isn't money—it's the three years needed to re-validate everything.

The commercial-side logic is different: "the math works out." Airbus uses its Skywise to manage A350 production scheduling, spotting supply-chain delays days in advance; manufacturing customers use it for predictive maintenance, where an hour of line downtime costs hundreds of thousands of dollars. After AIP launched, the logic shifted again—previously you had to send engineers on-site for six months; now the customer's own business staff can build a working agent in five days at a bootcamp.This is the real reason its 2026 US commercial revenue grew +149% year over year: the sales cycle shrank from nine months to five weeks.

Duan Yongping would ask one question: "What happens if the customer doesn't buy?" The answer splits in two. If a government customer doesn't buy, projects get delayed and budgets go to someone else, but life goes on; if a commercial customer doesn't buy, they just keep using Excel and BI reports. So demand for this business isn't rigid—it's "efficiency-driven": it sells when the economy is good, and it's the first thing cut when budgets tighten. This point must be remembered.

Unit economics: customers up only 24%, revenue up 93%

This is the set of numbers in the entire earnings report that best captures the nature of the business. Total customers 1,049, up 24% year on year; revenue up 93%. Revenue growth is four times customer growth, meaning growth comes almost entirely from existing customers expanding their orders.

Revenue per customer (TTM RMB 6.162 billion ÷ 1,049 customers) is about USD 5.87 million. That figure is top-tier in software — Salesforce's average annual spend per customer is roughly a hundred-something thousand dollars. What it sells is not seats but "a system."

Why do existing customers expand? Because the ontology graph has network effects: the more data sources connected and the more use cases running, the lower the cost of launching the next use case. A customer buys one use case in year one, ten by year three, and by year five the whole department runs on it. US commercial remaining performance obligations stand at USD 6.238 billion, up 124% year on year — a reservoir more than three times current revenue, indicating that expansion is still accelerating.

The flip side carries a concern: a narrow customer base. 1,049 customers support a market cap of RMB 456 billion, or an average of USD 430 million in market cap per customer (rough calculation). This means any budget change among a group of large customers gets amplified by the market cap into double-digit percentage swings.

Pricing power: why it can charge a 62% adjusted operating margin

Pricing power comes from three things, all of which are indispensable. First, few substitutes—no more than three companies worldwide can deploy in classified environments and run AI on top of them. Second, high switching costs—once business logic is written into the ontology, changing vendors means redoing the process. Third, attributable value—customers can calculate how much money the system saves them, so they do not haggle.

Duan Yongping's test for pricing power is simple: "Do you dare raise prices, and did customers leave?" Palantir's answer over the past eight quarters has been clear: revenue per customer rises year after year, and churn is low. It has passed this test.

Where is the boundary? In small and medium-sized enterprises. Implementation costs are too high—customers paying less than a few hundred thousand dollars a year cannot support an on-site team. So its TAM is naturally limited to "big government + big enterprise," roughly 3,000 to 5,000 institutions worldwide. That pool is large enough (only 1,049 penetrated so far), but it also means its ceiling is not "all enterprises" but "all large institutions that can afford it."

Conclusion

Judging the business essence: this is a good business, with real demand, real pricing power and excellent unit economics. Its boundary lies not on the demand side but in its customer structure — 1,049 large customers support all of revenue, and growth comes from larger orders rather than a wider customer base, which gives it both high margins and high volatility. Duan Yongping would say he understands this business, and would also say he cannot bring himself to pay this price.
