Competitive moat: Palantir does not sell technology; it sells lock-in — "you can no longer change."

Moat scorecard: switching costs 9, regulatory clearances 9, Ontology 8, brand and political access 7, technology 6. Composite 7.6 — wide, but not unbreachable.

The key insight: the barrier is not "better technology" but "customers can no longer change course." That explains why growth reaches 93% — and why the customer count rises only 24%: new customers must rewrite their workflows, which is too slow.

Position in the value chain: not a software vendor but a process reengineer

On a client's procurement list, Palantir is never "a piece of software" — it is "a process overhaul." That is the most fundamental difference between it and Snowflake and Databricks: those two sell tools, and clients decide how to use them; Palantir sells outcomes, with engineers on site rewriting the process from scratch.

This positioning cuts both ways. The good: extremely high revenue per customer (average annual spend of USD 5.87 million per client), extremely high renewal rates, extremely strong pricing power. The bad: long sales cycles, slow customer growth (+24%), and gross margin dragged down by headcount (80% rather than 85%).

AIP is changing this positioning. The Bootcamp model lets clients build a prototype themselves in five days, cutting the sales cycle from nine months to five weeks — this is the mechanism behind US commercial growth of +149%. But it also means the company is shifting from "process overhaul firm" to "tool vendor" — a short-term boost to growth, a long-term erosion of the moat.

Moat breakdown: three hold up, two need a discount

Security clearance: 9. This is the hardest barrier of all. Stack classified-data access, deployment in air-gapped environments and a fifteen-year delivery record, and no more than five companies worldwide can match it. The barrier is qualification, not technology—and qualification barriers are the hardest to breach with technology.

Switching costs: 8; Ontology: 8. These are two sides of one thing: the ontology graph locks in the customer's business semantics, so switching vendors means redoing the past five years of process. Rebuild costs are measured in years. On the government side this is near-unbeatable; on the commercial side it is eroding, as cloud vendors begin offering "out-of-the-box ontology templates"—shallow, but sufficient.

Brand: 7; Technology: 6. On the government side the brand is an asset (one of the default options); on the commercial side it is a liability ("that company that built the system for ICE"). Technology is no barrier—its models are bought in and its engineering talent can be poached. The real technology asset is Apollo's deployment capability, and it is underrated.

The most dangerous rival: not Databricks, but Microsoft

Databricks is strong at the data entry point but lacks "business semantics": customer data sits with it, yet decisions still sit in Excel. Moving upmarket will take three to five years and require building a consulting team, which is not in its DNA.

Microsoft is different. It has three things Palantir does not: enterprise distribution (the default entry point of Office/Teams/Azure), the CFO's trust (existing contracts, existing security reviews), and the procurement convenience of "no new vendor sign-off." Fabric bundles data integration, governance and BI into Azure, so customers do not need to sign another Palantir.

This is not a product contest; it is a contest of procurement habits. A CFO would rather approve "adding a module to an existing cloud contract" than "signing a new vendor and going through a six-month security review." Palantir wins on credentials on the government side and loses on distribution on the commercial side — and the commercial side is the half that drives its fastest growth and most of its valuation.

Anduril deserves a separate mention. It binds hardware (drones, sentry towers) to software (Lattice), and its influence at the Department of Defense is rising fast. Its model is harder to displace because it sells "capability," not "software." If the DoD's procurement logic shifts from "buying software" to "buying capability," Anduril will be the biggest beneficiary and Palantir the biggest casualty.

Conclusion

Moat composite score 7.6: near-invincible on the government side (9 points), middling on the commercial side (6 points). This is not a technology leader; it collects rent because customers can no longer switch — a very real barrier, but one bounded by customer patience and the distribution power of the cloud vendors. Microsoft is the most dangerous rival, because the threat comes from procurement habits rather than product performance.
