Core view

(Publication-ready copy, about 1,000 words)

Palantir's Q2 2026 results are out: revenue USD 1.935 billion, up 93% year on year, GAAP operating margin 47%, free cash flow margin 63%. On any software company, those three numbers would be historic highs. But this quarterly report holds three truths, and misreading any one of them leads to the opposite conclusion.

Truth 1: The +93% growth comes almost entirely from existing customers

Total customers 1,049, up only 24% year on year, yet revenue rose 93%. There is only one explanation for this arithmetic: existing customers are flooding in with add-on orders. US commercial remaining performance obligations stand at USD 6.238 billion, up 124% year on year, more than three times current-period revenue.

The good news: this shows the product genuinely solves a problem — no company spends three times as much just to flatter a supplier. The bad news: it also shows its customer base is narrow — 1,049 customers support a USD 456 billion market cap, or USD 430 million per customer on average. Any shift in the budgets of a handful of large customers gets magnified by the market cap.

So when looking at this company, don't watch customer-count growth — watch "how much longer revenue per customer can keep rising." That figure is now about USD 5.87 million a year, dozens of times Salesforce's — there is room to rise, but the ceiling is not high.

Truth two: the 47% margin is real; the 55% net margin is not

GAAP operating margin 47.1%, after deducting USD 265 million in stock-based compensation — that is real performance. But the net margin of 54.9% is even higher — accounting offers only one explanation: interest income on USD 9.2 billion of cash, roughly USD 370 million a year, USD 90 million a quarter.

Interest is not earned by selling software. A one-point drop in rates cuts this profit by 25%. So to judge this company's earning power, look at operating profit of USD 912 million, not net profit of USD 1.062 billion.

Deduct one layer further: treat stock-based compensation as a real cost (it is, since share count grows 2% a year), and shareholder earnings are USD 955 million a quarter, a 49.3% margin.On that basis, annualizing Q2, its P/E is 119x; even on the TTM net profit basis in the working papers, it is 140x. Still extremely high, but at least real.

Truth #3: order growth trails revenue by 44 percentage points — the only crack

Total contract value USD 3.373 billion, +49% YoY; revenue +93% YoY. Contracts are the water source; revenue is the water drawn. The source is growing at only half the pace of drawdown, meaning current revenue is eating into the backlog.

Breaking it down, US commercial TCV rose 153%, extremely strong; government and international dragged on the total. Government contract signing follows the budget cycle, and there were no strategic-scale awards this quarter — 220 million-dollar contracts averaging USD 15.3 million each, all expansion deals rather than the kind of single deal worth several hundred million dollars.

This is not a crisis, but it is the thing to watch most closely over the next two quarters. The test is simple: the TCV / revenue ratio now stands at 1.74x; only a drop below 1.2 would be a real problem.

The final truth: there is no margin for error at this price

USD 456 billion market cap, 74x TTM price-to-sales. Run the math out to 2032: even if it reaches USD 45 billion in revenue at a 34% net margin and exits at 30x, its market cap six years from now is the same as today. Factor in 2% annual share count growth and it is 11% lower per share.

Bull case — USD 13 billion revenue in 2027, market keeps paying 36x sales — 3% upside. Base case USD 120, 37% downside. 3% up, 37% down. This payoff does not require you to be right; it only requires you not to play.

Palantir is a good company; there is no arguing that. But between a good company and a good investment sits a price. At today's price, you are betting the market will keep paying a premium for perfection — that is not investing, it is the greater fool theory. When price-to-sales returns to 30x (about USD 80), we can talk.

Conclusion

Conclusion: avoid. First-class business, real growth, top-tier margins, but a 74x price-to-sales ratio discounts six years of flawless execution in one go. Optimistic 3%, neutral −37%, pessimistic −63% — under this risk-reward structure, the only correct move is not to participate. Conditions to revisit: price-to-sales below 30x (about USD 80), or TCV growth back above revenue growth with non-US revenue accelerating.
