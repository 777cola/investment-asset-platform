Core thesis

Duan Yongping reads financial reports differently from others. He ignores guidance, adjusted figures and sell-side models, and looks at only three things: whether revenue was actually collected in cash, whether customers will buy again next year, and whether the moat around the business has narrowed. Read Palantir's Q2 through these three lenses and the answers are all there.

Revenue of USD 1.935 billion, up 93% year on year; free cash flow of USD 1.220 billion, higher than net income — the cash was genuinely collected. Customer count rose only 24% while revenue rose 93% — existing customers are raising their orders, which means they will buy again next year. There is no evidence the moat has narrowed.

Article 1: Is the revenue real?

To judge whether revenue is real, Duan Yongping checks whether cash flow matches up. Palantir's Q2 operating cash flow was about 1.24 billion (rough calculation) and net profit 1.062 billion, a ratio of 117%. Cash received exceeds book profit — this test is passed.

The second check is accounts receivable. This company's customers are governments and large enterprises, where slow payment is the norm, but bad debt is minimal. The financials show no typical risk signal of "receivables growing far faster than revenue" — this test is also passed.

The third check is the revenue recognition basis. Its revenue splits into subscriptions and deployment projects; the project portion is recognized on a percentage-of-completion basis, which in theory leaves room for manipulation. But 93% growth cannot be manufactured through early recognition — early recognition can only shift one quarter, not produce four consecutive quarters of acceleration. So the revenue is real; there is no doubt on this point.

Clause 2: Will customers still buy next year?

This is the one Duan Yongping values most, and Palantir's strongest. Total customers 1,049, up only 24% year over year, while revenue rose 93%—all growth came from existing customers expanding orders. Existing customers choosing to expand shows the product genuinely solves problems.

Harder evidence is remaining contract value: US commercial RDV USD 6.238 billion, +124% year over year. This is signed but unrecognized revenue, more than three times current-period revenue. Duan Yongping would say: "This is next year's revenue, already locked into contracts."

If you want to find fault: customer concentration is high—1,049 customers support a USD 456 billion market cap, averaging USD 430 million each. If a few of the top twenty customers cut budgets, revenue would feel it immediately. This isn't "a bad business," it's "a business not diversified enough."

Article 3: Has the moat narrowed?

Look at three numbers: gross margin, renewal rate, revenue per customer. Gross margin is about 80%, flat year over year, with no price war. Revenue per customer is rising (revenue growth is four times customer growth). The company does not disclose the renewal rate separately, but RDV growth offers an indirect read: if customers were not renewing, RDV would not rise 124%.

So the moat has not narrowed. Duan Yongping would add: a moat that has not narrowed does not mean the price is reasonable. When he bought Apple, Apple's moat had not narrowed either, but he waited for 13x PE. A company with a wide moat and an expensive price is, in his framework, "understandable, don't buy," not "good company, buy."

Conclusion

Duan Yongping-style conclusion: The business is real, the growth is real, the moat is real, the price is not. He would read this earnings report as a "certificate of quality assets," then say one thing: "Too expensive, wait." His stated logic is "better to miss out than to do wrong" — missing Palantir won't cost him a single dollar; buying at 74x price-to-sales might.
