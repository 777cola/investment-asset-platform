Three most misunderstood numbers

Core view

Munger's method for reading financial reports is "find where others misread." In this Q2 report, three numbers will be misread by 90% of people: adjusted operating margin 62%, net margin 55%, contract value growth 49%. All three are real numbers, and all three will lead to wrong conclusions.

His famous line is "invert, always invert." So this close read does not cover how good it is — only how it will fool you.

Misreading 1: the 62% adjusted operating margin

The company reported a GAAP operating margin of 47.1% and an adjusted margin of 61.7%. The most common line in sell-side reports is "operating margin of 62%, first in software." That statement is not wrong, but it describes a world in which employees are assumed to forgo stock compensation.

Of the RMB 283 million difference, RMB 265 million is equity-based compensation. This company's share count grows 2% a year, and that is the cost of this money. Munger would say: "If you have to give employees 2% of the company every year to keep them, then that 2% is a cost, not an adjustment."

The correct reading: 47% is the true margin, 62% is the margin "assuming no dilution," and the two differ by 15 percentage points. Which one to use depends on the question you are answering — use 47% to judge business quality, and use 62% to judge cash flow but deduct for dilution.

Misreading 2: the 55% net margin

Net profit of RMB 1.062 billion, net margin 54.9%, higher than the operating margin. At first glance it looks like "this company makes more money the further down you go," but in reality "its non-operating income exceeds its tax." Net profit above operating profit is an abnormal signal in any industry.

Breaking it down: operating profit of RMB 912 million, plus interest income of about RMB 90 million, minus tax of about RMB 40 million (low effective tax rate), gives RMB 1.06 billion. That is, 8% of net profit is deposit interest, and part is from the low tax rate. Neither of these grows with the business.

Munger would ask one question: if rates return to 1%, what is net profit? About RMB 930 million, a net margin of 48%. That is, this company's "55% net margin" becomes 48% after rate normalization. For a 140x PE, that is a 15% valuation gap.

Misreading 3: 49% growth in contract value

TCV up 49%, revenue up 93%. Many will see these numbers and say "orders can't keep up, trouble ahead"; others will say "TCV is a lagging indicator, ignore it." Both readings are too hasty.

The right approach is to break down the structure. US commercial TCV +153%, RDV +124% — this leg is flying; government and international dragged down the total. TCV is "total value of new contracts signed," and it is heavily skewed by single large deals — a USD 500 million government deal signed last quarter makes this quarter's year-over-year look ugly. So the right metric is not TCV growth, but the trend in the TCV / revenue ratio.

On that basis: Q2 TCV 3.373 billion ÷ revenue 1.935 billion = 1.74x. A ratio above 1.5 is healthy, meaning new signings still cover current-period recognition. What to watch is whether it falls below 1.2 — that is when the real crack appears.

Munger-style fourth question: what would kill it

It is not competition, not technology, but the combination of government budgets + high multiples. Government budgets set the floor on its revenue; high multiples set the floor on its share price. When both move down at once, the decline is multiplicative.

He would also flag one more thing: the better this company's financials look, the lower the market's tolerance for error. A company at 20x PE falls 10% on a single quarterly miss; a company at 74x price-to-sales falls 30% on a single quarterly miss. For the same operating fluctuation, the higher the valuation, the stronger the convexity of the share price — to the downside.

Conclusion

Munger-style conclusion: all three numbers mislead, but they mislead in the same direction—each makes the company look better than it is. The 47% is the true margin, 8% of the 55% is interest, and the 49% TCV growth is distorted by the timing of large deals. The ratio that actually matters is TCV/revenue = 1.74x, and that remains healthy. As for the price, he has no interest in discussing it—"buying a software company at 74x price-to-sales is a bet on human nature, not a calculation of the business."
