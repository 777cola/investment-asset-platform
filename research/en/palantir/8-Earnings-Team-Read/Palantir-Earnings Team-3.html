Three most misunderstood numbers

Core view

Munger's approach to reading financial reports is to "find where others misread." In this Q2 report, three numbers will be misread by 90% of people: adjusted operating margin 62%, net margin 55%, contract value growth 49%. All three are real, and all three will lead to the wrong conclusion.

His famous line is "invert, always invert." So this close read is not about how good it is — only about how it will fool you.

Misreading 1: the 62% adjusted operating margin

The company reported a GAAP operating margin of 47.1% and an adjusted margin of 61.7%. The line that recurs most often in sell-side reports is "operating margin of 62%, first in software." That statement is not wrong, but it describes a world in which employees are assumed to forgo stock compensation.

Of the RMB 283 million difference, RMB 265 million is equity-based compensation. The company's share count grows 2% a year, and that is the cost of this money. Munger would say: "If you have to give employees 2% of the company every year to keep them, then that 2% is a cost, not an adjustment."

The correct reading: 47% is the true margin, 62% is the margin "assuming no dilution," and the two differ by 15 percentage points. Which one to use depends on the question you are answering — use 47% to judge business quality, and use 62% to judge cash flow, but deduct for dilution.

Misreading 2: the 55% net margin

Net profit of RMB 1.062 billion and a net margin of 54.9%, above the operating margin. At first glance the company appears to earn more the further down the P&L you go, but in reality non-operating income exceeds its tax charge. Net profit above operating profit is an abnormal signal in any industry.

Breaking it down: operating profit of RMB 912 million, plus interest income of about RMB 90 million, less tax of about RMB 40 million (a low effective tax rate), gives RMB 1.06 billion. In other words, 8% of net profit is deposit interest and part comes from the low tax rate. Neither grows with the business.

Munger would ask one question: if rates return to 1%, what is net profit? About RMB 930 million, a net margin of 48%. In other words, the company's 55% net margin becomes 48% after rate normalisation. On a 140x P/E, that is a 15% valuation gap.

Misreading 3: 49% growth in contract value

TCV +49%, revenue +93%. Many will look at these numbers and conclude "orders can't keep up — trouble ahead"; others will dismiss TCV as a lagging indicator. Both readings are too hasty.

The right approach is to break down the structure. US commercial TCV +153% and RDV +124% — this leg is flying, while government and international dragged the total down. TCV measures the total value of new contracts signed and is heavily skewed by single large deals: a USD 500 million government deal signed last quarter makes this quarter's YoY comparison look ugly. The right metric, then, is not TCV growth but the trend in the TCV/revenue ratio.

On that basis: Q2 TCV 3.373 billion ÷ revenue 1.935 billion = 1.74x. A ratio above 1.5 is healthy, meaning new signings still cover current-period recognition. Watch for a fall below 1.2 — that is when the real crack appears.

Munger-style fourth question: what would kill it

The risk is not competition or technology but the combination of government budgets + high multiples. Government budgets set the floor under revenue; high multiples set the floor under the share price. When both fall together, the decline is multiplicative.

One further point: the better this company's financials look, the lower the market's tolerance for error. A company at 20x P/E falls 10% on a single quarterly miss; a company at 74x price-to-sales falls 30% on a single quarterly miss. For the same operating fluctuation, the higher the valuation, the greater the convexity of the share price — to the downside.

Conclusion

Munger-style conclusion: all three numbers mislead, and all in the same direction—each flatters the company. The 47% is the true margin, 8pp of the 55% is interest, and the 49% TCV growth is distorted by the timing of large deals. The ratio that actually matters is TCV/revenue at 1.74x, and that remains healthy. As for valuation, he has no interest in discussing it: "buying a software company at 74x price-to-sales is a bet on human nature, not a calculation of the business."
