Earnings Deep Dive (Li Lu) — Palantir

What will its revenue look like in ten years?

Core thesis

Li Lu does not read quarterly reports; he reads the "revenue mix ten years out." His question: what will this company earn money from in ten years, and will that business be stronger or weaker than it is today?

Through that lens, the key point in Q2 is not the +93%; it is that 81% of revenue comes from the US and 42% from the US government. How that mix shifts over the next decade determines whether this is "infrastructure" or "a contractor."

How will today's revenue mix change in ten years?

Three scenarios. First, commercial revenue climbs from 39.5% to 60% while government drops below 30% — Palantir is then an enterprise software company and should be valued as SaaS (10–15x P/S). Second, government holds above 40% and commercial penetration stays slow — it is then a "government IT platform", worth a contractor multiple plus a premium (8–12x). Third, both scale and revenue reaches USD 40 billion — today's price is then exactly fair.

The evidence so far supports the first: US commercial +149%, far outpacing government at +90%, with commercial TCV +153%.Mind the base, though — commercial at 764 million against government at 809 million is a narrow gap; two years of 100% growth would be enough for commercial to overtake.

Li Lu would ask: among commercial customers, how many "bought once and never expanded"? The filings do not say. That is the information gap —customer count and RDV are disclosed, but not net retention by cohort, the biggest blind spot for investors judging the ten-year structure.

Second layer: how strong is this business's "civilisation-level" attribute

Li Lu invested in BYD. His framework: "civilization-scale technological change + companies that can occupy a structural position within that change." As AI moves from models to production systems, it needs an intermediate layer connecting data, permissions and decisions—a layer that genuinely exists and is genuinely valuable.

But "this layer is valuable" does not mean "a company in this layer is valuable." He studied railways: they transformed the US economy, yet half of the railroad companies went bankrupt in the 1890s. The value of infrastructure accrues to society; excess profit accrues to the few companies occupying the bottleneck position.

Is Palantir that bottleneck? On the government side, yes (security clearances + history); on the commercial side, no (cloud vendors can replicate it). Its "civilization-scale" attribute is therefore only half present—it is a bottleneck for the US government, not for global commerce.

Layer three: what will the valuation be in ten years?

Assume that in ten years it reaches USD 40 billion in revenue (+17% CAGR), a 35% net margin and a 25x P/E, for a market cap of USD 350 billion—23% below today's level. This is Li Lu's deepest concern: the company grows up, yet the stock falls, because today's price has already discounted a decade of success.

Conversely, if it reaches USD 80 billion in revenue (+26% CAGR), a 40% net margin and a 30x multiple, the market cap is USD 960 billion, an annualized return of 7.7%. That is the return in the "highly successful" scenario, and it is still less than two points above the S&P's long-term average.

He would say: this is not an investment where the odds are in my favor. Every position in his portfolio must return more than threefold in the "company succeeds" scenario. Palantir does not even double in the best case, so it is not his kind of target, no matter how good the company is.

Conclusion

Li Lu-style conclusion: The trend is right, the position is half right, the price is completely wrong. On a ten-year view, the story is "a bottleneck for the US government plus a challenger in the commercial market" — the former solid, the latter fragile. Best case annualised return 7.7%; most likely case, negative. This is not the odds structure he would buy — he would file it in the "watch for ten years" folder and wait for a crisis.
