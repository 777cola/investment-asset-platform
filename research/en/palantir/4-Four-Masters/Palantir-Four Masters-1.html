Four Masters Agree: A Good Company, Not a Good Investment

Duan Yongping asks, "Do you understand it?" I do — but not at this price. Buffett asks, "Where is the margin of safety?" There is none. Munger flips the question: "What would make it fall 70%?" No black swan needed; ordinary mean reversion would do. Li Lu asks, "Will it still be here in ten years?" Most likely yes, but today's price has already prepaid the excess profit of ten years from now.

Four men, four methods, one conclusion: a USD 456 billion market cap and a 74x price-to-sales ratio price perfection, not risk. Upside of 3% in the optimistic case, −37% in the neutral case, −63% in the pessimistic case — this payoff structure does not require being right or wrong, only staying out.

Duan Yongping: I understand the business, not the price

Duan Yongping buys only three things: a business he understands, the right corporate culture, and a suitable price. Palantir passes the first two. The business essentially turns data into decisions, charged by subscription, and customers cannot easily switch away; Karp is a founder with genuine conviction who has done one thing for more than twenty years and has never made a single opportunistic acquisition for the sake of the financial statements. Companies like this are rare in both the A-share and US markets.

It fails the third. Duan Yongping anchors his view of price on "discounted future cash flow," and he has never in his life bought anything above 40x price-to-earnings, let alone 74x price-to-sales. He would use Apple as the comparison: when Apple traded at 30x P/E, you could see where every dollar of profit came from; in Palantir's market value, ninety percent rests on the assumption that it must remain the only winner.

His stated logic is "better to miss than to do wrong." Missing a good company costs you opportunity cost; buying at the wrong price costs you principal. So Palantir falls into his "understandable but won't buy" column—not because he looks down on it, but because the price never comes. He has said something to this effect himself: when a good company is expensive, just pretend you never saw it.

Buffett: The moat is real, the margin of safety is zero

Buffett would start with the business. A 47% GAAP operating margin, a 63% free cash flow margin, migration costs customers cannot escape, almost no debt and USD 9.2 billion of cash—structurally this is a business model he would like, close to See's Candies in 1972: pricing power rests on customers being unable to leave, not on cost leadership.

Then he would ask about price. He bought Coca-Cola at 15x P/E in 1988, Apple at 13x in 2016, BYD at just over 20x in 2020 and BYD at 10x in 2008. In his entire life he has never bought a 140x P/E target, not once. That is not conservatism but methodology: the margin of safety comes from the gap between price and value, not from how good the company is.

He would also be wary of one thing: the political nature of government contracts. Four-tenths of revenue comes from the US government, with budgets and priorities set by elections and Congress. That is not a moat but an exposure. A moat should make customers unable to leave you, not make you unable to leave one customer. He exited airline stocks in 2020 on exactly this logic—not because the companies were bad, but because they depended on a variable they could not control.

Munger: Invert — what would make it fall 70%

Munger's approach is to invert the question: under what circumstances does Palantir fall 70%? He would probably list three. ① Federal budgets tighten, or the government standardises AI procurement into framework agreements, shifting bargaining power back to the buyer; ② Microsoft and Databricks build the "ontology layer" into default cloud platform packages, turning it from the only option into one of many; ③ Growth drops from 93% to 40%, and the market cuts growth and multiple at the same time—a valuation double-kill.

The first two take time; the third does not.A 93% growth rate is mathematically impossible to sustain—the base grows, the denominator grows; this is arithmetic, not judgment.Yet a 74x price-to-sales multiple assumes it persists. Munger said "risk comes from not knowing what you don't know"—apt here: everyone knows it will decelerate, but no one is willing to price the deceleration.

He would add a statistical point: a company de-rating from 74x sales to 24x sales sees its stock fall 68%, while the fundamentals may only reflect "growth going from 93% to 45%"—still a good company. That is the terrifying thing about multiple risk:it does not require you to be wrong about the company, only wrong about the price.

Li Lu: The trend can be right, but the winner may not be it

Li Lu would acknowledge this is a civilisation-level trend. As AI moves from models to production systems, it needs an infrastructure layer in between that connects data, permissions and decisions — and that layer is genuinely valuable. He has studied US railroads, oil and semiconductors, and in every technology revolution the winners at the infrastructure layer captured the most profit.

But he would flag two things. First, a correct trend does not mean this company can capture the terminal excess profit. The moat at the application layer is thinner than at the model layer and the cloud layer: Microsoft has distribution channels and enterprise contracts, Databricks is entrenched at the data entry point, and open-source solutions are closing in fast. Second, his method is "ten-year certainty," and the current price has already cashed in that certainty in advance — even if it does become the standard in ten years, today's buyer still only earns 8% annualised, because most of the return has been prepaid by a 74x price-to-sales ratio.

Li Lu often says to look for moments when "price is far below value," and such moments usually appear in two places: misunderstood good companies, and strong companies at the bottom of a cycle. Palantir is now neither — it is fully understood by the market and sits at a sentiment high, just 2.5% below its 52-week high.

Odds: 3% upside, 63% downside — no need to do the math

All three scenarios anchor on 2027 revenue multiplied by price-to-sales. The base case assumes USD 11.8 billion in revenue and a 24x P/S, implying a USD 289 billion market cap and USD 120 per share, 37% below the current price. This is not a bearish call on the company; it is a bearish call on this price. A 24x P/S is neither cheap nor harsh for a software company growing 45%.

The bull case requires USD 13 billion in 2027 revenue (2026 guidance +60%) and the market still willing to pay 36x P/S, yielding USD 195, or 3% upside. In other words: have the company beat its targets and have the market keep paying a high multiple, both at once, and you make 3%.

The bear case assumes USD 10.5 billion in revenue and 16x P/S, yielding USD 70, or 63% downside. What does 16x P/S represent? It actually traded at that level in 2022. This is not a tail scenario; it happened within the past four years.

Four people would say the same thing: this is a good company, not a good investment. The only reason to buy it is "someone will take it off your hands at a higher price" — that is not an investment judgment, it is the greater fool theory. Duan Yongping calls it "passing the parcel"; Buffett calls it the "greater fool theory." Different names, same meaning.

Conclusion

Four votes, unanimous: Avoid. This is not a rejection of the company—business quality is top-tier on every dimension. What is rejected is the price: at 74x P/S, even perfect execution delivers a zero return, and any deceleration turns into a decline of more than 40%. Conditions to revisit: P/S back below 30x (about USD 80), or TCV growth re-accelerating above revenue growth alongside accelerating non-US revenue. Until then, it is the first line on the watchlist, not any line in the portfolio.
