Duan Yongping's lens: six questions, one answer

Ask the same questions about this company the way Duan Yongping would: Is this a good business? Will it still be here in ten years? Do I understand it? At what price do I buy? What do I fear most? Can it be shorted?

The answers to all six are simple: good business, still here in ten years, understandable, price not right, fear government budgets most, no shorting. Together they add up to one line — good company, wait for the price.

Q: Is this a good business?

Good. Why good? Because once customers use it, they cannot switch away. Look at the numbers: revenue up 93%, customer count up only 24% — all the growth came from existing customers ordering more. What kind of business gets existing customers to spend twice as much in a year? It must genuinely solve a problem.

Another number I particularly like: free cash flow is higher than net profit. That means it earns real money, not accounting profit. Many companies show handsome profits but cannot collect the cash — I don't touch that kind of business.

But it is not a perfect business. Its customers are governments and mega-enterprises, just over a thousand of them — too concentrated. And part of its revenue comes from on-site engineers, which is a manpower business, not a software business. So I give it 8 out of 10, not 10.

Q: Will it still be around in ten years?

Yes. What this company does — connecting data so people can make decisions — will still be needed in ten years. And its position in the US government wasn't won on technology; it was won over fifteen years and security clearances, the kind of thing no one can take away.

But "still here" and "still this profitable" are two different things. In ten years it may still be a major US government supplier, but by then its growth will be roughly 15%, and the market will value it at roughly 8x price-to-sales.The company survives, the valuation doesn't, and the stock still halves.

I often say:Buying a stock means buying the discounted value of future cash flows, not buying a company's fame.However famous, at the wrong price it is still a losing trade.

Q: Can you understand it?

I understand what it sells; I cannot figure out what it is worth.

What it sells is clear: it helps governments and large enterprises turn data into decisions, charging annual fees. That I understand. But the "what it is worth" layer I cannot calculate — because more than half of its price rests on "everyone believes it will remain the sole winner." That belief I cannot verify with numbers.

I understand Apple, because I know how many phones it will sell next year, how much it will earn, how many shares it will buy back. I understand Moutai, because I know how much liquor it produces a year and at what price it sells. With Palantir I understand the business, but I cannot know what its revenue will be six years out. What I cannot understand, I will not bet heavily on. That is what the circle of competence means — not understanding everything, but knowing what you do not understand.

Q: At what price would you buy?

Below a 30x P/S ratio, roughly USD 80. At that point its market cap would be about USD 185 billion, which on 2027 revenue of USD 11.8 billion is 15x—at that price, even if growth drops to 20%, I still make money.

Why not buy some now? Because buying now means earning from "someone willing to pay a higher price," not from "the company getting better." I can't understand the former; I can understand the latter. I only earn from the part I understand.

Some ask, what if the wait never comes? Then I don't buy. The market offers new opportunities every year, and money in your own pocket doesn't run away. What I fear most isn't missing out, it's buying wrong. Miss once and you simply didn't earn; buy wrong once and it takes years to climb out.

Q: What is the biggest fear?

Two things worry me most.

First, government budgets change. Four tenths of its revenue comes from the US government—that is not a moat, that is putting your fate in someone else's hands. If budgets tighten or procurement rules change, its revenue will fall faster than anyone's. When I buy a company, I like businesses with diversified customers where everyone depends on everyone else.

Second, Microsoft turns it into something "given away for free." Think about it: if enterprises already use Microsoft cloud and Microsoft says "we include this feature," will customers sign with a new vendor? In business history, far more companies have been killed by "bundled and given away" than by being beaten head-on.

Neither of these things is certain to happen. But buying at 74 times price-to-sales, if either one happens, you lose more than half. I am not afraid of a company having problems; I am afraid the price leaves no room for error.

Q: So can you short it?

No. Three reasons.

First, you don't short a good company. Its business is real, its growth is real. You're betting that "the price is too high," not that "the company will collapse"—and those two judgments differ tenfold in difficulty.

Second, time is not on the short seller's side. It can stay overvalued for two years; your margin won't last that long. I've seen too many people get the direction right and die on timing.

Third, not shorting is a rule I set for myself, not a judgment on this company. I've never made money from shorting in my life, and I don't intend to start.

So the answer is simple: don't buy, don't short. Leave it there and look at it once a quarter. Revisit when the price is right; if it never is, forget it. In investing, the most comfortable state is "cash in hand, names in mind."

Conclusion

Six questions answered, one conclusion: good business, understandable, still here in ten years, price does not work, no shorting. The verdict is "too expensive, wait" — not a dismissal of the company, but no way to get that price. The buy condition is explicit: price-to-sales below 30x (about USD 80). Until then, cash in hand and a name on the list is enough.
