03 Moat anatomy: three real, two are stories — Palantir

Break the word "moat" into five things that can be scored

Core view

"Moat" is overused. A real moat has only one test: can a well-funded competitor catch up within three years? By that standard, three of Palantir's five moats are real.

Three real ones: government security clearances (9), switching costs (8), Ontology (8). Two discounted ones: brand (7), technology (6).

The real moat: security clearances cannot be bought

Handling classified data for the US government requires security clearances, personnel vetting, facility accreditation and a fifteen-year performance record. The first three can be bought with money and time; the fourth cannot — without a track record, you are not eligible to bid on certain contracts.

One number measures the width of this moat: Palantir's share of US Department of Defense AI-related contracts. It is now one of the default options rather than the only one, with Anduril and Booz Allen both competing. But "one of" is enough in this market — the government will not hand critical systems to a new company with no track record.

Durability: more than ten years. This moat will not disappear because of technological change, only weaken because of political change.

Points two and three are the substantive ones: switching costs and the entity itself

The two are two sides of the same coin: the ontology locks in the customer's business semantics, while migration costs deter switching. How strong that lock-in proves depends on how deeply the customer uses the product — a customer running a single use case faces low migration costs, whereas one running twenty faces costs so high that switching is unviable.

Evidence: customer count grew just 24% against 93% revenue growth, pointing to deepening within the existing base, and RDV +124% shows that deepening is still accelerating. Both moats are widening, not narrowing.

One factor cuts the other way: cloud vendors have begun offering out-of-the-box ontology templates. Microsoft Fabric's semantic layer and Databricks' Unity Catalog, though shallow, cover 80% of generic scenarios. That is sufficient for customers who want reporting only, but not for those running complex decision-making. The moat therefore holds firm at the high end and is eroding at the low end.

Two discounted: brand and technology

Brand 7.0. On the government side the brand is an asset: "the company that builds intelligence systems" reads as reliable. On the commercial side it is a liability: "the company that built ICE's deportation system" gives some customers and employees pause. One brand, pulling in two directions.

Technology 6.0. This is the most overrated item. Its models are licensed from outside (Anthropic, OpenAI, Google), its ontology is not irreproducible technology, and its engineering talent can be poached. The real technology asset is Apollo—the orchestration layer that can deploy in any environment, including offline and on customer premises. The market badly undervalues this, but its value lies in lower delivery cost, not in blocking competition.

So technology gets 6.0: not a moat, but an efficiency advantage. And in software, efficiency advantages get caught up over time.

Moats and valuation: a wide moat does not justify a rich price

Many people conflate two things: "its moat is so wide that paying a little more is worth it." That is wrong. A moat explains how long it can keep earning money; valuation asks what those future earnings are worth discounted to today.

In numbers: assume its moat lets it reach USD 45 billion in revenue by 2032 at a 34% net margin; those future profits discounted to today are worth USD 289.2 billion (8% discount rate). Today's market cap is USD 456 billion. In other words, the market is paying a 58% premium for the "moat"—not a premium for the moat, but a premium for "the moat will never be breached."

However wide a moat is, it has a price. Companies with wide moats deserve a premium, not an unlimited premium.

Conclusion

Moat conclusion: composite score 7.6 — wide, but not impregnable. Near-invincible in government, moderate in commercial. Three genuine moats (licences, switching costs, the entity itself) are widening; two discounted ones (brand, technology) do not constitute barriers. But a moat does not settle valuation — it answers only "how long can it earn," not "how much is it worth."
