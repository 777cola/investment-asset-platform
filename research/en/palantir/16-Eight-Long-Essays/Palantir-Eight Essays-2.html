02 Business essence: it does not sell software, it sells "the power to define" — Palantir

Whoever defines semantics collects the rent

Core thesis

Every software business solves one problem: turning chaos into order. But the way they solve it differs, and so does the way they make money. Some sell tools (Excel), some sell pipes (databases), some sell conclusions (Palantir). Selling conclusions is the most profitable, because it takes on the "judgment" on the customer's behalf.

This piece lays it out clearly: what Palantir is actually selling, why customers are willing to pay USD 5.87 million a year, and where the boundaries of this business lie.

Ontology: translating the business world into graphs

A manufacturer runs five systems that do not talk to each other: ERP, MES, SCADA, Excel reports and email. The question "should this production line be shut down for maintenance today" has its answer scattered across five places. What Palantir does is define concepts such as "production line", "equipment", "work order" and "downtime" as objects, define their relationships as edges, and then run rules and models on that graph.

This process is called "ontology modeling". Its value lies not in the technology but in forcing the client to articulate what counts as an anomaly. Most enterprises cannot articulate it — this is not an IT problem but a management problem. Half of what Palantir's on-site engineers do is technical; the other half is helping clients standardize the language of management.

Once this graph is built, the client cannot leave it. Every upper-layer application (reports, alerts, AI agents) has to read this graph. Switching vendors is not switching software; it is redefining the company's business semantics from scratch. That is the source of its 62% adjusted operating margin.

Subscription: why revenue is so steady

Its contract structure is "platform subscription + use-case expansion." In year one the customer buys one platform license plus two use cases, adds to ten use cases by year three, and migrates the entire department onto it by year five. Each expansion is a price increase, but customers do not feel they are being charged more—because the new use cases deliver new value.

This model shows up in the financials as: slow customer growth (+24%), fast revenue-per-customer growth (revenue +93%). Remaining contract value of USD 6.238 billion (US commercial), up +124% year over year—revenue already signed but not yet recognized, a reservoir equivalent to the next three years.

The upside of the subscription model is high visibility, high renewal rates and strong cash flow (customers prepay). The downside is that it demands continuous proof of value—once a customer feels "this year's use cases brought no new value," next year's expansion stops. So its growth is not linear but stepwise: every step up requires a new success story.

Onsite: the part that most resembles consulting and is most easily misunderstood

Palantir has several thousand Forward Deployed Engineers (FDEs) stationed on customer sites. This is where it differs most from every SaaS company — SaaS companies sell and leave; Palantir sells and moves in.

The upside of this model is extremely high customer stickiness and deep process embedding. The downside is high cost, slow expansion and dependence on people. This is why its gross margin is 80% rather than 90%, and why its customer count grew only 24% — every new customer requires a team to move in for six months.

AIP's bootcamp model is changing this: a prototype in five days, customers add use cases themselves, and FDEs appear only at key junctures. This is the key step in its migration from "consulting" to "software", and the real reason US commercial grew 149% in 2026. If this step succeeds, its gross margin, customer growth rate and valuation logic will all be rewritten.

Boundary: this business has a limited ceiling

Three boundaries. First, the customer boundary — roughly 5,000 to 8,000 institutions worldwide can afford to spend over USD 1 million a year on data integration, and it has 1,049 of them today. Second, the industry boundary — manufacturing, energy, healthcare and government sell best; retail, consumer and internet are weak. Third, the geographic boundary — the US accounts for 81%, while overseas lags by an order of magnitude.

Together, these three boundaries imply an addressable market of about USD 300 billion (6,000 customers × USD 5 million average annual spend), against current revenue of USD 8.15 billion. Penetration is 2.7%. That number shows both the size of the opportunity and that the ceiling lies not in demand but in delivery capacity.

Conclusion

Conclusion on the nature of the business: It sells "the power to define" — locking a customer's business semantics onto its own ontology, then collecting rent annually. This business carries extremely high margins, very strong stickiness and excellent cash flow, but expansion is constrained by delivery capacity. AIP's bootcamp is its attempt to break through that constraint; whether it succeeds determines whether this is an USD 8 billion company or an USD 40 billion company.
