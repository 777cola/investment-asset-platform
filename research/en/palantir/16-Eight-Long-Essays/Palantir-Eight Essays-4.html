04 Financial reality: three margins, only one is real — Palantir

47%, 55%, 63% — three numbers, three bases, a threefold valuation gap
Research date 2026/10/05  ·  Price $189.40 (+0.34%)  ·  Market cap About USD 456 billion  ·  PS(TTM) Approximately 74x
← Back to the hub

Core view

For the same earnings report, a company will give you three margins: a GAAP operating margin of 47.1%, a GAAP net margin of 54.9%, and a free cash flow margin of 63.1%. All three numbers are real, but they answer three different questions. Use the wrong one and your valuation will be off by 30%.

This piece breaks the three numbers apart and tells you which to use and which to ignore.

47%: true operating capability, the one to use

GAAP operating income USD 912 million, margin 47.1%. This figure already deducts stock-based compensation (265 million) and all expenses, with only interest and taxes excluded. The question it answers is: is this business itself profitable? The answer is extremely profitable.

Cross-sectional comparison: Salesforce about 20%, Adobe about 35%, Microsoft about 45%. Palantir's 47% ranks in the top three among large software companies. Longitudinal comparison: 26.8% in the same period last year (back-calculated), a 20 percentage point improvement in one year. This improvement comes from the expense ratio being compressed from 46% to 33%, the result of a model shift (consulting to software), not cost cutting.

55%: false; 8% is deposit interest

Net profit of RMB 1.062 billion, net margin 54.9%, 7.8 percentage points above the operating margin. On first look the number confuses—what company gets more profitable the further down the income statement you go?

The answer lies below the operating line: interest on USD 9.2 billion of cash, roughly 370 million annualized (at a rough 4%), or 90 million a quarter; plus an effective tax rate below the statutory rate (historical loss carryforwards). Together the two lift net profit to RMB 1.062 billion.

Neither source is sustainable. A 100bp rate cut removes 90 million annualized; once the tax credits run out and the rate returns to 21%, net profit falls a further 40 million. So the "55% net margin" becomes roughly 48% after normalization. Valuing on 55% overstates value by 15%.

63%: real cash, but equity incentives must be deducted

Free cash flow was RMB 1.22 billion, a 63.1% margin, 16 percentage points above the operating margin. This figure comes closest to "the cash shareholders actually receive," but one adjustment is needed: RMB 265 million of share-based compensation is a non-cash expense that was added back.

After deducting it, the figure is RMB 955 million, a 49.3% margin. This is the true owner earnings. It is 2 percentage points above the GAAP operating margin of 47.1% — showing that the company converts cash extremely well, with almost no accounts-receivable padding in its profit.

Valuation on this basis: annualizing Q2 gives RMB 3.82 billion, or 119x the RMB 456 billion market cap; annualized net income on the same basis is RMB 4.25 billion, or 107x. Both multiples say the same thing: the price has already discounted more than twenty years of these profits.

TCV: the only metric flashing red

The income statement is all good news; the crack is in orders. Total contract value USD 3.373 billion, +49% YoY; revenue +93%. Contract growth is only half of revenue growth, meaning current revenue is consuming the existing contract backlog.

Breaking it down, US commercial TCV +153% (extremely strong), dragged by government and international (no strategic-scale mega deals). 220 million-dollar contracts, averaging USD 15.3 million each — all expansion deals, not strategic deals.

This crack is not fatal, but it is the only metric that needs verification over the next two quarters. Judgment criterion: TCV/revenue is currently 1.74x; only a drop below 1.2 would be a real problem.

Conclusion

Financial truth: 47% is about the business, 63% is about cash flow, 55% should be ignored, and 49% is true shareholder earnings. All four numbers point to "this is an excellent business," and all four point to "this price is extremely expensive" — a 119x shareholder-earnings P/E is not the price a company that can still double should command.
