06 Management: genuine conviction, but governance falls short on shareholder alignment — Palantir

Capability 9, alignment of interests 3
Research date 2026/10/05  ·  Price $189.40 (+0.34%)  ·  Market cap About USD 456 billion  ·  PS(TTM) Approximately 74x
← Back to the hub

Core view

Judge management on two counts: whether he can get things done, and whether he is willing to share the spoils with you. Karp scores near full marks on the first and only 3 points on the second.

That is an investment judgment, not a moral one: where management is highly capable but indifferent to shareholder returns, owning the stock means betting on his judgment rather than sharing in the company's growth.

Capability: 23 years devoted to one thing

Karp founded Palantir with Thiel and Cohen in 2003 — twenty-three years ago. In that time the company has done one thing: turn data into decisions. It never chased hype (blockchain, the metaverse, Web3 — none of it), never made opportunistic acquisitions, never cut R&D to prop up short-term EPS.

AIP is the clearest illustration.Launched in 2023, US commercial revenue grew 149% YoY in 2026 — in two years, from concept to USD 3 billion in annualized revenue. That is not luck; it stems from a bet he began placing in 2020 that LLMs would make the ontology layer essential.

He speaks bluntly in public, takes sharp positions and often offends people. But he is highly consistent — he does what he says and does not play both sides. Silicon Valley does not have many CEOs like that.

Alignment of interests: 13.7%, 2% and locked-in voting rights

Three numbers tell the story. 13.7% — stock-based compensation as a share of revenue, annualising to over USD 1 billion. 2% — annual share count growth, so shareholders are diluted 2% a year. 50% — the Class F super-voting rights lock in nearly half the votes for the three founders, leaving outside shareholders unable to change anything at the ballot.

Together they mean you put up the money, he makes the decisions, you have no say, and you get diluted every year. That is not unusual in founder-led companies, but combined with a 74x price-to-sales ratio it amounts to paying the highest premium for a team you cannot constrain.

Karp's 2024 pay package caps out at USD 1.1 billion, vesting over ten years against share-price milestones. The design logic — he earns only when shareholders earn — sounds perfect. But the milestones are set on share price, not revenue or ROIC, so his incentive is tied to market cap, not to value creation.

Capital allocation: cash accumulates on the balance sheet, with no buybacks and no dividend

USD 9.2 billion of cash, no dividend, no meaningful buyback (USD 1 billion authorised in 2023, barely executed) and no major M&A. Management's explanation: "keeping it for strategic opportunities."

That cuts two ways. The bull read: capital found uses with returns above buybacks (R&D, sales), evidenced by 93% growth. The bear read: management does not see its own stock as cheap, so it does not buy back.

I lean toward the latter. At 74x price-to-sales, the company is right not to buy back—a buyback would mean buying its own shares at a high price. But the absence of even a token buyback shows management's view of the share price diverges from the sell side's. That says more than any disclosure of share sales.

Successor: no number two Karp

The division of labour is "Karp external, Sankar internal, Cohen sales", and the three have run the firm that way for a decade. External narrative, government relations and employee mobilisation, however, are all heavily concentrated in Karp alone.

His departure would not sink the company—product strength, qualification barriers and customer dependence all remain. But the "why Palantir" story would lose a piece, and much of the 74x price-to-sales multiple rests on that story. That is the leverage of key-person risk at high multiples: a 20% discount on the story means a 20% discount on market cap.

Conclusion

Management conclusion: capability 9 points, alignment of interests 3 points, overall 6 points. He built the company from zero to a USD 456 billion market cap, and no one can dispute that. But shareholders have almost no say in this company, and they are diluted 2% every year. Buying its stock means handing your money to a team you cannot constrain—that demands a very large discount, and the current price is a premium.
