Key takeaways from the earnings deep dive

The Q3 2026 (July–September) report reads as an across-the-board decline on the surface: operating profit of RMB 12.705 billion, -5.8%, GAAP net income of RMB 22.112 billion, -28.2%, and underwriting profit of RMB 1.352 billion, -43%.

Break it down and the picture changes: insurance fell 26.4% while the industrial side rose 12.6%, the two offsetting to produce -5.8%. The decline stems from the auto insurance cycle and short-term bond yields, not from any loss of operating control; the growth comes from rail volumes and manufacturing restocking, and is real. The quality of this report is better than the headline numbers suggest.

Tier 1 revenue structure: half premiums, half real economy

Of the five segments, the two insurance businesses combined posted RMB 4.086 billion pre-tax, 35.6% of the segment total; the three physical businesses posted RMB 7.383 billion, 64.4%. This is where Berkshire least resembles an "insurance company" — two-thirds of its profit comes from railroads, power and manufacturing.

That structure saved it in Q3 2026. Insurance alone would have shown a 26.4% profit decline — an ugly quarter; adding the physical businesses' +12.6% narrowed it to -5.8%.

The structure carries risk too: all three physical businesses are asset-heavy, regulated or bound by industrial cycles, with a growth ceiling of 5%–10%. Getting overall growth back to double digits requires M&A, and M&A has not closed a big deal in three years.

Tier 2 — Profit quality: underwriting margin 8.6%, still profitable

Underwriting profit was USD 1.352 billion; grossing back from a 91.4% combined ratio implies premium volume of about USD 15.7 billion. For every USD 100 of premium collected, USD 91.4 goes out in losses and expenses, leaving USD 8.6 of profit. A year ago that figure was about USD 15 on the same rough basis.

The underwriting margin falling from 15% to 8.6% is the most substantive deterioration in this report, and GEICO is the cause: profit of USD 982 million, -45%. Auto claims costs (repair labour hours, used-car residual values, medical) kept rising through 2025–2026, while premiums began to soften after fully repricing in 2024; the two moved in opposite directions and compressed the margin.

Margins at the operating businesses are improving: BNSF +19.3% as volume recovery diluted fixed costs, BHE +8.3% on rate base expansion, Manufacturing +11.8% on restocking. Together the three rose +12.6%, the only bright spot this quarter.

Tier 3 Costs and investment income: short-term bond yields are a hidden drag

Insurance investment income of RMB 2.734 billion, -14.1%, is a line many analyses skip. It comes mainly from interest on USD 377.4 billion of cash and short-term bonds — short-term bond yields slid from a 2024 high of 5.25% to 4.4%–4.6%, so the same principal earns 15% less. This is the inevitable result of falling rates, unrelated to operating capability.

Another angle: 377.4 billion × 4.5% ÷ 4 ≈ USD 4.2 billion per quarter of interest income (rough calculation, including taxable versus tax-exempt structural differences); the gap versus the actual 2.734 billion comes from part of the funds being railroad/energy working capital and from the duration structure of the bond portfolio. The order of magnitude checks out.

The RMB 9.4 billion gap between GAAP net profit of RMB 22.112 billion and operating profit of RMB 12.705 billion is entirely investment gains and losses (mark-to-market on equities). The sign of this item can reverse every quarter and should not be used to judge operations.

Layer 4 Cash flow and balance sheet: cash is building, the asset base is getting heavier

Cash plus short-term debt reached RMB 377.4 billion, up again from the second quarter, while buybacks over the same period totalled RMB 9.647 billion and M&A spend RMB 16.2 billion (first three quarters). Spending heavily while cash still rises means operating cash flow far exceeds the sum of these two items.

The arithmetic is proof of an "asset shortage": money compounds faster than it can be deployed. Berkshire is not unwilling to buy; there are simply no targets large enough, or prices are not right.

The asset side is getting heavier: total assets USD 1.304 trillion, equity USD 761.4 billion, leverage 1.7x. For an insurer this leverage is abnormally low — it has deliberately switched off "insurer leverage" in exchange for the ability to never be forced to sell assets. The cost is an ROE of just 10.4%.

Book value per Class A share USD 536,590, up 10.5% YoY — the cleanest line of all: even with profit declining, net assets still compound at a double-digit annual rate.

Conclusion

Read this earnings report as "don't stop at the first line." The -5.8% decline in operating profit is real, but the drivers are clear: the auto insurance cycle and falling short-term bond yields — neither reflects a capability problem. Meanwhile, the three physical segments grew 12.6%, book value rose 10.5% and cash hit a new high — that trio is the long-term value.

Only one number matters: the combined ratio. 91.4% sits above the safety line, 95% is the warning line and 100% is the turning point. Until it breaks below 95%, a profit decline just means "earning less" and does not change the business.
