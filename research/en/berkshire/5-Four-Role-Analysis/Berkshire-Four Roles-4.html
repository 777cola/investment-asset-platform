Risk Assessment (Li Lu's perspective) — Berkshire

Five paths that could knock 20% off, and one that would be truly fatal

Core view

Risk operates on two levels: what makes the stock fall, and what makes the business deteriorate. The first is abundant, the second rare. Berkshire's problem is that the first has already played out—the stock has just hit an all-time high.

Layer one: share-price risk, all three on valuation

① Safe-haven flows return. The stock outperformed the S&P by 19.2 percentage points over the past month, a gap exceeded only in 2008 over the past 30 years. In mid-November, single-day swings in tech stocks routinely exceeded 5%, a sign that panic arrived in pulses. The premium was borrowed and must be repaid.

② Buybacks cannot support the valuation. Q3 buybacks totalled RMB 9.647 billion against a market cap of RMB 1.26 trillion—just 0.8%, more symbolic than real support.

③ Rates rebound. The RMB 377.4 billion of short-term debt is a "rate asset": a drag when rates fall (declining reinvestment income is already reflected in the -14.1%), a beneficiary when rates rise. But the share price reacts inversely to rates—as the discount rate rises, 1.66x book cannot hold.

Second layer: business risk — only two merit serious attention

① Wildfires and catastrophes. BHE's transmission equipment in several western states has been alleged to have ignited wildfires, and payouts in such litigation can reach the tens of USD billions. Berkshire can absorb the hit, but it would erode profits for years. This is the only risk that could change the "book value growth rate."

② Second-order succession risk. Abel inherits the CEO role, not "Buffett." Buffett's value is not just stock picking; it is being the person others call first in a crisis. That reputation cannot be inherited, only re-earned. If the first major crisis is mishandled, Berkshire's "buyer of last resort" premium will decline permanently.

The rest are slow variables: the curse of size (USD 1.26 trillion market cap; doubling requires earning another Berkshire), and indexation (the top five holdings are 68.5% of the 13F, increasingly resembling an active version of the S&P 500).

The truly fatal flaw is not any single one of these, but that "it turns into a bond"

Weighing all the risks, the worst outcome is not bankruptcy but a slow transformation into a quasi-bond yielding 4%–5%: cash keeps rising as a share of the total, returns on operating assets are capped by regulation, and growth depends on M&A that is getting harder to execute.

That outcome requires nothing bad to happen—only that "nothing happens." Yet the market prices it at 1.66x book, as if "something will happen."

The Li Lu-style question: looking back ten years from now, is the bigger risk having paid too much, or having missed it? At the current price, overpaying is more likely—because if you miss it, you can still earn 4.5% on short-term bonds.

Conclusion

The risk list is short, but every item points the same way: the 18% downside is valuation risk, not operating risk. Operationally the company is safer than 99% of its peers; on valuation it is more expensive than 90% of them.

Both hold at once, which leaves waiting as the only conclusion.
