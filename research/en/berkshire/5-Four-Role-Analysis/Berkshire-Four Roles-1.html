Business analysis (Duan Yongping's perspective) — Berkshire

What the business actually earns: float, pricing power and the decade-ahead picture

Core view

Berkshire earns money three ways: the underwriting spread, investment income on float, and cash flow from operating businesses. The second is the most valuable of the three, because it requires no capital and no negotiation — only time.

It does not sell insurance. It sells other people's willingness to leave their money with it.

GEICO sells cheap auto insurance, a product customers price-check once a year and feel little loyalty towards. Berkshire, by contrast, sells something else: a piece of paper that says "this company will not welch on its obligations." Companies buy its large-limit liability and reinsurance not because it is cheap, but because when something big goes wrong, it will certainly be able to pay. The pricing power of that trust is far higher than that of auto insurance.

The USD 178.5 billion float is that trust, quantified. A combined ratio of 91.4% means customers effectively pay Berkshire 8.6% for the privilege of handing it their money—unique in the financial industry. Banks pay interest on deposits; funds pay distribution fees for capital; Berkshire gets paid instead.

Pricing power over real assets: yes, but capped by regulation

BNSF reported pre-tax profit of USD 1.729 billion, up 19.3%, driven mainly by a rebound in intermodal volume. Its rail pricing power rests on having "no second route": from the Port of Los Angeles to Chicago, trucking costs three times as much as rail. Yet it cannot raise prices at will—shippers will sue, and regulators will come asking questions. Rail is a business with a moat but no freedom to raise prices.

BHE is even clearer: returns on regulated utilities are set by state commissions—the more capex, the more allowed profit, but per-unit returns are capped. Pre-tax profit of USD 1.612 billion, up 8.3%, with growth coming from rate base expansion rather than price increases.

Manufacturing, services and retail posted USD 4.042 billion, up 11.8%, making it the best segment this quarter and the one that most resembles an ordinary company: Precision Castparts' aerospace forgings, Lubrizol's additives and Clayton's homes each have their own cycle, with no unified pricing-power story.

Will it still be around in ten years? Yes, but perhaps not this machine

Berkshire will certainly still be around in ten years, and its float will only be larger. What is uncertain is what it will look like: if Abel puts the USD 377.4 billion to work, it will resemble an operating holding company; if he keeps buying short-term Treasuries, it will gradually become a giant cash-management company, with returns converging toward Treasuries.

The real bet here is not "can the business persist" but "will this money be put to use."Whether the ten-year annualized return is 8% or 4% hinges entirely on this one question.

Conclusion

This is a good business—good enough that it does not need annual proof. But it is a "slow business": 8%–10% annualised, and it will not surprise you. Buy in at USD 592 and what you are buying is that certainty, not that growth. Paying a 66% book premium for certainty is a personal choice, not an investment judgment.
