# Berkshire（BRK.B）— Duan Yongping Supplement

## Question 1: Is this a good business?

Good — but what makes it good? It uses money that others pay it to hold. Float stands at USD 178.5 billion and the combined ratio is 91.4%: customers hand it premiums, it invests them, and after claims are paid 8.6% is left over. Insurance float, not a bank loan, is the cheapest money in the world, and this is the world's largest holder of it.

That said, it is not a "phenomenal business." A phenomenal business is one like Moutai, where raising prices needs no explanation and capacity is never a concern. Berkshire has to build railroads, construct power plants and deal with regulators; it earns hard money, at 8%–10% annualised. Among first-rate companies it is the "steady" type, not the "windfall" type.

## Question 2: Will it still be around in ten years?

It will still be there, and in all likelihood be larger than it is now. The reason is not the name "Buffett" but its structure: no debt maturity dates, no single customer, no technology-substitution risk. Any one of those three can kill 90% of companies; it is exposed to none of them.

The only thing likely to change in ten years is its "character." If the RMB 377.4 billion keeps buying short-term debt, it will gradually turn into a quasi-bond yielding 4.5%; if Abel puts it to work, it will remain an 8%–10% compounding machine. Neither outcome counts as "not there," but the returns differ by a factor of two.

## Question 3: Do you understand it?

Eighty percent is understandable; the other twenty percent is not. The understandable part—how premiums are collected, how claims are paid, how railroads make money, how electricity tariffs are set—sits in the financial statements and is a matter of arithmetic.

The part I cannot understand comes down to two things. First, the intrinsic value of the equity portfolio. Apple, American Express, Coca-Cola, Alphabet and Bank of America account for 68.5% of the 13F; what they are worth depends on the next ten years of the US stock market, and that I cannot judge. Second, the price discipline on buybacks. Buying back RMB 9.6 billion at 1.66 times book—management sees something different from what I see, and I do not know what they see.

What I cannot understand is not fatal, but it warrants a discount. My approach: I pay for the "ununderstandable" only when the price offers a sufficient margin of safety. At the current price, I am being asked to pay a premium for the parts I cannot understand—that will not do.

## Question 4: At what price to buy?

Start looking below USD 500; start buying below USD 460. USD 500 equates to 1.4x book, the midpoint of the stock's valuation range over the past decade. Buying a good company at the midpoint of its range captures book value growth over the long run, with no valuation loss.

Some will ask why not wait for 400. Because it never comes. 1.2x book has appeared only in 2011, March 2020 and 2022, each time accompanied by the narrative that "this company is finished." Those who wait for such prices are usually too afraid to buy at the bottom.

Scaling in is right: buy a little at 500, add a little at 460, buy a little more at 430. No need for precision, just the right direction.

## Question 5: What worries you most?

The biggest fear isn't a loss — it's "it looks like it won't lose". Berkshire feels like a "buy it and forget it" stock, and that feeling is the most dangerous, because it gets you buying at 1.66x and holding off at 1.2x.

Three specific fears. ① Abel does a large, expensive deal, deploying the USD 377.4 billion into a mediocre asset — the 2016 PCP write-down of USD 9.8 billion is the precedent. ② Progressive slowly erodes auto insurance share, with underwriting profit stuck below USD 1.5 billion a quarter long term. ③ Wildfire litigation becomes a second "asbestos" — decades of payouts, tens of billions in damages.

None of the three would sink the company, but any one would turn "8%–10%" into "5%–6%". For a stock that earns only from compounding, a two-point drop in growth should mean a 30% drop in valuation.

## Question 6: If you could own only one, would you buy it or short-duration bonds?

At this price, buy short-term bonds. A 4.5% risk-free yield with no 18% downside to absorb, no need to watch the market every night and no need to judge whether Abel is up to the job. Berkshire's expected return is now 6%, but the risk is equity risk.

At USD 500, the answer flips: expected return of 12%–15% against 4.5% on short-term bonds, with the extra 8% as compensation for bearing volatility.

That comparison is everything. Investing need not be complicated; it only requires choosing the better of two things, then waiting.
