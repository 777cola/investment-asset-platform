# Berkshire（BRK.B）— Duan Yongping Supplement

Figures are taken from the 2026Q3 financial report: float of RMB 178.5 billion, combined ratio of 91.4%, PB of 1.66x, and the top five 13F holdings at 68.5%. Historical PB lows (2011, 2020/03, 2022) are derived from public share price and book value data.

## Question 1: Is this a good business?

Good. What makes it good? **It uses money that others pay it to hold.** USD 178.5 billion in float, with a combined ratio of 91.4%, meaning customers hand it premiums, it invests them, and after paying claims 8.6% is left over. The cheapest money in the world is not a bank loan but insurance float, and this is the world's largest float holder.

But it is not a "phenomenal business." A phenomenal business is one like Moutai — raising prices needs no explanation, and capacity is never a concern. Berkshire has to build railroads, construct power plants, and deal with regulators; it earns hard money, at 8%–10% annualized. **It is the "steady" type among first-rate companies, not the "windfall" type.**

## Question 2: Will it still be around in ten years?

It will still be there, and in all likelihood be larger than it is now. The reason is not the name "Buffett" but its structure: **no debt maturity dates, no single customer, no technology-substitution risk.** Those three things can kill 90% of companies; it is exposed to none of them.

The only thing likely to change in ten years is its "character." If the RMB 377.4 billion keeps buying short-term debt, it will gradually turn into a quasi-bond yielding 4.5%; if Abel puts it to work, it will remain an 8%–10% compounding machine. **Neither outcome counts as "not there," but the returns differ by a factor of two.**

## Question 3: Do you understand it?

Eighty percent is understandable; twenty percent is not. What is understandable: how premiums are collected, how claims are paid, how railroads make money, how electricity tariffs are set—all of this is in the financial statements, a matter of arithmetic.

What is not understandable comes down to two things. **First, the intrinsic value of the equity portfolio.** Apple, American Express, Coca-Cola, Alphabet and Bank of America account for 68.5% of the 13F; what they are worth depends on the next ten years of the US stock market, and that I cannot judge. **Second, the price discipline on buybacks.** Buying back RMB 9.6 billion at 1.66 times book—management sees something different from what I see, and I do not know what they see.

The parts I cannot understand are not fatal, but they warrant a discount. **My approach: I pay for the "ununderstandable" only when the price gives me a sufficient margin of safety.** At the current price, I am being asked to pay a premium for the parts I cannot understand—that will not do.

## Question 4: At what price to buy?

Start looking below USD 500, start buying below USD 460. **USD 500 corresponds to 1.4x book, the midpoint of its valuation range over the past decade.** Buying a good company at the midpoint earns you the growth in book value over the long run, with no valuation loss.

Some will ask why not wait for 400. Because it never comes. 1.2x book has appeared only in 2011, March 2020 and 2022, each time accompanied by the narrative that "this company is finished." Those who wait for such prices are usually too afraid to buy at the bottom.

Scaling in is right: buy a little at 500, add a little at 460, buy a little more at 430. **No need for precision, just the right direction.**

## Question 5: What worries you most?

The biggest fear is not a loss, but **"it looks like it won't lose"**. Berkshire gives people the feeling that "you buy it and forget about it" — and that feeling is the most dangerous, because it makes you buy at 1.66x and shy away from adding at 1.2x.

Three specific fears. ① Abel makes a big, expensive acquisition, spending the USD 377.4 billion on a mediocre asset — the 2016 PCP write-down of USD 9.8 billion is precedent. ② Auto insurance share is slowly eaten away by Progressive, with underwriting profit stuck below USD 1.5 billion per quarter long term. ③ Wildfire litigation becomes a second "asbestos" — paying out for decades, tens of billions in damages.

None of these three would sink the company, but all would turn "8%–10%" into "5%–6%". **For a stock that earns only from compounding, a two-point drop in growth should mean a 30% drop in valuation.**

## Question 6: If you could buy only one, would you buy it or short-duration bonds?

At this price, buy short-term bonds. A 4.5% risk-free yield, with no 18% downside to absorb, no need to watch the market every night, no need to judge whether Abel is up to the job. **Berkshire's expected return is now 6%, but the risk is equity risk.**

But if it falls to USD 500, the answer flips. Then the expected return is 12%–15%, short-term bonds still pay 4.5%, and the extra 8% is the compensation for bearing volatility.

That comparison is everything. Investing does not need to be complicated; it only requires choosing the better of two things, then waiting.
