Portfolio recommendation

Current recommended position: 0%. Not because the company is poor, but because 1.66x book with 6% upside versus 18% downside is not a payoff worth consuming portfolio risk budget.

Build the position in three tranches: USD 500 (1.4x book) 3%, USD 460 (1.3x) 5%, USD 430 (1.2x) 8%. Cap at 8%, not 15%—it is a defensive asset, not an offensive one. Its role is to reduce portfolio volatility, not to raise returns.

Why is the cap only 8%?

Berkshire offers an expected annualised return of 8%–10% with volatility one-third lower than the S&P.Its role is to "reduce the portfolio's maximum drawdown," not to "raise the portfolio's return."A 15% position therefore amounts to funding a defensive asset out of the allocation meant for offensive assets.

What 8% means: within a 10–12 stock portfolio, this is a "ballast"-level allocation, not a core holding. Core holdings should be businesses capable of annualised returns above 15%.

The exception: if the portfolio as a whole shifts into defensive mode — for example, you expect a recession over the next 12 months — the weight can be temporarily raised to 12%–15%. That, however, is a macro call, not a call on this stock.

Position-sizing discipline: price and thesis as two triggers

Adding: add on price triggers only, never on "good news". Three tranches at 500 / 460 / 430, each 8%–9% apart; don't try to pick the bottom. Trimming: two conditions — ① P/B >1.85 (about USD 730) with no substantive improvement in the thesis; ② a single position exceeds 10%.

Stop-loss: no price stop, a thesis stop. A 30% drop in this name is often the best entry point; a price stop would force you to sell at the low. The time to actually exit is when the business deteriorates: the combined ratio breaches 100%, float declines, or it makes an acquisition that wrecks the balance sheet.

The cautionary tale for this discipline is March 2020: Berkshire fell 30%, many investors stopped out, then watched it climb back in 2021.

Correlation with other holdings: the beta of negative beta

Berkshire's correlation with the S&P 500 runs at about 0.6–0.7 over the long run, given its large US equity book. But in panic periods that correlation drops below 0.3 or even turns negative — November 2026 was one example: the S&P fell while Berkshire rose 13.2%.

Correlation with short-term bonds is near 0 (the cash sleeve behaves like short-term bonds, but the share price fluctuates); near 0 with gold (neither generates cash flow, though the mechanisms differ); about 0.5 with the utilities sector (XLU), given BHE's similar valuation logic; and negatively correlated with AI hardware stocks during risk-off periods — its most valuable correlation this year.

Portfolio implication: if you are already heavily weighted in the AI supply chain, Berkshire is a hedge; if your portfolio is already defensive (utilities, consumer staples, short-term bonds), its marginal value is very low — even 8% is too much.

Conclusion

Start at 0. Three tiers of conditional orders: 500 / 460 / 430, corresponding to 3% / 5% / 8%. Cap at 8% — this is ballast, not an engine.

Use the thesis for stop-loss, not the price. No leverage, no options, no chasing highs out of a desire to "avoid risk" — these are the three easiest mistakes to make with this stock, because its story is too well suited to telling panicked people what they want to hear.
