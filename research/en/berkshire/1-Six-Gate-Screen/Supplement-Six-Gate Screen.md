## Gate one: circle of competence 9/10 — the financials need no guesswork

Berkshire's business model in one sentence: take insurance float, buy good businesses that generate their own cash flow, and park the excess in short-term Treasuries when no targets can be found. The financials report insurance underwriting, insurance investment, BNSF, BHE and manufacturing/services/retailing separately, so each piece can be valued on its own, with no "other" line hiding profit.

This test rarely earns a 9 in US equities. Most conglomerates fail on opaque segment data, leaving investors to guess from consolidated statements. Berkshire is the opposite: even float cost, catastrophe losses and the railroad operating ratio are broken out. You do not need to believe management's story; you only need to be able to add.

## Gate two: good business 8/10 — negative-cost funding plus quasi-monopoly assets

The mark of a good business is that it is "hard for others to take away, and able to raise prices without continuous investment." Berkshire is very strong on the first count, average on the second.

In insurance, USD 178.5 billion of float and a 91.4% combined ratio mean policyholders pay Berkshire to hold their money — the cheapest funding in the world, cheaper than debt issuance and cheaper than equity issuance. In the operating businesses, BNSF and BHE are regulated near-monopolies; pre-tax profit was USD 1.729 billion and USD 1.612 billion this quarter, up 19.3% and 8.3% YoY. Manufacturing, services and retail: USD 4.042 billion, up 11.8%.

The deduction is on return on capital: shareholders' equity of USD 761.4 billion against annualised net profit of about USD 77.2 billion gives an ROE of only 10.4% (rough calculation). Not a poor business — equity is simply too thick and leverage barely used.

## Gate 3: Moat 9/10 — four in total, the last one impossible to replicate

GEICO's direct-sales cost advantage: no agent commissions, so the same policy costs 10%–15% less. BNSF's network: the western US has only two transcontinental lines — the other is Union Pacific — and no third can be built. BHE's regulated franchises: returns on regulated utilities are capped, but competitors are locked out.

The fourth is Berkshire itself: USD 377.4 billion in cash plus the "Buffett's company" name. In 2008 Goldman Sachs and General Electric queued up for its preferred stock; in 2020 it did not act, yet everyone was waiting for it to. This moat was not bought; it was compounded over sixty years — replicating it takes not money but time and reputation.

## Level four: Management 7/10 — Abel's first report card

The timeline first: Abel becomes CEO on January 1, 2026; Buffett hands over the chairman role to Howard on September 18; and this third-quarter report lands on November 7. It is the new team's first quarterly report with no Buffett signature on it at all — part results, part exam.

The answer is decent. Underwriting profit falls 43%, yet the combined ratio holds at 91.4% — earning less, not losing money. Buybacks rise from RMB 4.527 billion in the second quarter to RMB 9.647 billion, a clear willingness to return capital. Jain continues to run insurance, with no personnel changes.

The 3-point deduction reflects insufficient sample, not doubt about ability: he has yet to steer the company through a real crisis or complete a USD 30 billion-scale acquisition. Those 3 points can only be earned back in the next round of panic.

## Gate 5: Margin of safety 3/10 — this gate vetoes the idea

Book value per share (A shares) USD 536,590, equivalent to USD 357.7 per B share. Share price USD 592.85, P/B 1.66x. Historically, Berkshire's most comfortable buying zone was 1.2–1.4x; above 1.5x it starts to look expensive, and above 1.8x you are in 1998 and 2007 territory.

A different yardstick tells the same story: operating profit of USD 12.705 billion this quarter, USD 50.8 billion annualized, against a market cap of USD 1.26 trillion, or 24.8x. For a conglomerate growing 5%–8% a year, 25x operating profit offers no margin of safety.

The only part deserving a high score is the balance sheet: USD 377.4 billion in cash and short-term Treasuries, equal to 30% of market cap. But that is not a margin of safety, it is the business model — the 1.66x you pay already includes that pile of cash.

## Level six: Discipline 7/10 — self-restraint in not chasing hot themes, with one lapse

The hardest call of 2026: in a year when Nvidia's FY2027Q3 revenue reached USD 115.8 billion, up 103% YoY, Berkshire chased not a single AI theme, preferring to leave USD 377.4 billion parked in short-duration Treasuries yielding 4.4%–4.6%. That discipline has saved it many times over the past decade.

Buybacks are the soft spot. Berkshire repurchased USD 14.4 billion in the first three quarters, USD 9.647 billion of it in Q3 alone — the most aggressive quarter since the new buyback policy began in 2018, and struck at a 66% premium. Buffett's original formulation was "repurchase only below intrinsic value"; buybacks at this price can only be explained as "management believes intrinsic value is far above book," or "management wants to prop up relative performance." The two explanations mean entirely different things for shareholders.

## Veto items and items to be verified

Price is the only veto. At the company level there is no second reason to reject it: debt is manageable, cash is abundant, the culture is intact and succession is smooth. At 1.66x book, this is the one factor that turns the investment into negative expectancy — USD 626 in the neutral case (+6%), USD 488 in the bear case (-18%).

Three things to verify: ① where Abel's buyback price ceiling is written, and whether the annual report discloses the range-weighted average price; ② underwriting profit has fallen for two consecutive quarters — will the 2027 combined ratio breach 95%; ③ what the USD 377.4 billion ultimately goes into — continuing to buy short-dated Treasuries is "no ideas", deploying it is "capable".

Until these three questions are answered, any purchase is a bet that "others will panic more".
