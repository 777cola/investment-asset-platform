Six-Gate Screen — Berkshire

Berkshire (BRK.B) | 01 · Six-Gate Screen
Research date 2026/11/16  ·  Price $592.85 (-0.02%)  ·  Market cap About USD 1.26 trillion  ·  PE(TTM) Approximately 16.6x

Core thesis

Five of six gates passed, 43 points. Business, moat and culture all sit in the first-tier range; price is the only sticking point: USD 592.85 corresponds to 1.66x book value and about 25x annualized operating earnings. This is not a company that needs nitpicking; it is a stock that needs a price to wait for.

Conclusion first: do not buy, but keep watching. The triggers are specific — the share price returns below USD 500 (1.4x book), or a single acquisition of more than USD 30 billion appears, or underwriting profit returns to USD 2 billion per quarter.

Gate one: Circle of competence 9/10 — its financials need no guesswork

Berkshire's business model in one sentence: take insurance float, buy good businesses that generate their own cash flow, and buy short-term Treasuries when no targets can be found. The financial statements list insurance underwriting, insurance investment, BNSF, BHE and manufacturing/services/retailing separately, and each piece can be valued on its own, with no "other" line hiding profit.

This test rarely earns a 9 in US equities. The problem with most conglomerates is opaque segment data, leaving investors to guess from consolidated statements. Berkshire is the opposite: even float cost, catastrophe losses and railroad operating ratio are broken out. You do not need to believe management's story; you only need to be able to add.

Gate two: good business 8/10 — negative-cost funding + quasi-monopoly assets

The test of a good business is "hard for others to take away, and able to raise prices without continuous investment." Berkshire is very strong on the first half, average on the second.

On the insurance side, USD 178.5 billion of float and a combined ratio of 91.4% mean policyholders pay it to hold their money — the cheapest funding in the world, cheaper than issuing debt, cheaper than equity issuance. On the operating side, BNSF and BHE are regulated near-monopolies; pre-tax profit was USD 1.729 billion and USD 1.612 billion this quarter, up 19.3% and 8.3% year on year. Manufacturing, services and retail: USD 4.042 billion, up 11.8%.

The deduction is on return on capital: shareholders' equity of USD 761.4 billion against annualized net profit of about USD 77.2 billion gives an ROE of only 10.4% (rough calculation). Not a poor business — equity is simply too thick and leverage barely used.

Gate 3: Moat 9/10 — four in total, the last one impossible to replicate

GEICO's direct-sales cost advantage: no agent commissions, so the same policy costs 10%–15% less. BNSF's network: the western US has only two transcontinental lines, the other being Union Pacific, and no third can be built. BHE's regulated franchises: returns on regulated utilities are capped, but competitors are locked out.

The fourth is Berkshire itself: USD 377.4 billion in cash plus the "Buffett's company" name. In 2008 Goldman Sachs and General Electric queued up for its preferred stock; in 2020 it did not act, but everyone was waiting for it to act. This moat was not bought; it was compounded over sixty years — replicating it takes not money but time and reputation.

Level four: Management 7/10 — Abel's first report card

The timeline needs to be laid out first: on January 1, 2026, Abel takes over as CEO; on September 18, Buffett hands over the chairman role and Howard succeeds him; on November 7, this third-quarter report is released. This is the new team's first quarterly report with no Buffett signature at all, so it is both results and an exam.

The answer is decent. Underwriting profit falls 43%, yet the combined ratio remains at 91.4% — earning less, not losing money; buybacks rise from RMB 4.527 billion in the second quarter to RMB 9.647 billion, showing willingness to return capital. Jain continues to run insurance; no personnel changes.

Deducting 3 points is not doubt about ability but insufficient sample: he has not been through a real crisis deployment, nor made a USD 30 billion-scale acquisition. Those 3 points must wait for the next round of panic to be restored.

Gate 5: Margin of safety 3/10 — this gate vetoes the idea

Book value per share (A shares) USD 536,590, equivalent to USD 357.7 per B share. Share price USD 592.85, P/B 1.66x. Historically, Berkshire's most comfortable buying zone was 1.2–1.4x; above 1.5x it starts to get expensive, and above 1.8x you are in 1998 and 2007 territory.

A different yardstick tells the same story: operating profit of USD 12.705 billion this quarter, USD 50.8 billion annualized, against a market cap of USD 1.26 trillion, or 24.8x. For a conglomerate growing 5%–8% a year, 25x operating profit offers no margin of safety.

The only part deserving a high score is the balance sheet: USD 377.4 billion in cash and short-term Treasuries, equal to 30% of market cap. But that is not a margin of safety, it is the business model — the 1.66x you pay already includes that pile of cash.

Level six: Discipline 7/10 — self-restraint in not chasing hot themes, and one lapse

The hardest thing to do in 2026: in a year when Nvidia's FY2027Q3 revenue hit USD 115.8 billion, up 103% year on year, Berkshire chased not a single AI theme, preferring to leave USD 377.4 billion sitting in short-duration Treasuries yielding 4.4%–4.6%. That discipline has saved it many times over the past decade.

The soft spot is buybacks. It repurchased USD 14.4 billion in the first three quarters, USD 9.647 billion of it in Q3 alone — the most aggressive quarter since the new buyback policy began in 2018, and it came at a 66% premium. Buffett's original formulation was "repurchase only below intrinsic value"; buybacks at this price can only be explained as "management believes intrinsic value is far above book," or "management wants to prop up relative performance." The two explanations mean entirely different things for shareholders.

Veto items and items to be verified

There is only one veto: price. No second reason to reject the company exists at the company level — debt is manageable, cash is abundant, the culture is intact, succession is smooth. 1.66x book is the only thing that turns this investment into "negative expectancy": USD 626 in the neutral case (+6%), USD 488 in the bear case (-18%).

Three things to verify: ① Where Abel's buyback price ceiling is written, and whether the annual report discloses the range-weighted average price; ② underwriting profit has fallen for two consecutive quarters — will the 2027 combined ratio breach 95%; ③ what the USD 377.4 billion ultimately goes into — continuing to buy short-dated Treasuries is "no ideas", deploying it is "capable".

Until these three questions are answered, any purchase is a bet that "others will panic more".

Conclusion

Score 43, five gates passed. No buy, for one reason only: price. USD 592.85 corresponds to 1.66x book and 24.8x annualized operating profit, with 6% upside and 18% downside — the risk-reward does not hold.

But it is the only name in this portfolio where "when it falls, you must act." Entry points in three tiers: start building below USD 500 (1.4x book), add to standard weight at USD 460 (1.3x), go heavy below USD 430 (1.2x). Don't wait for it to drop to USD 400 before researching — by then you most likely won't dare to buy.
