Industry-chain positioning

Berkshire does not occupy a single link in the "conglomerate holding" value chain; it is the chain's capital provider. Upstream it collects insurance liabilities (float of USD 178.5 billion), midstream it allocates capital, and downstream it holds railroads, power, manufacturing and retail.

That position determines its pricing power: it has pricing power over customers (policyholders), none over regulators (rates), and limited power over shippers (rail freight rates). It earns money from "negative cost of funds + long asset duration," not from "product price increases." That also caps its growth at 8%–10%.

Upstream: it sells "certainty", not insurance

At the top of the insurance value chain, what matters is not product design but claims-paying capacity. In a USD 50 billion catastrophe, no more than five companies worldwide can pay out in one go without affecting their ratings, and Berkshire is one of them. That gives it absolute pricing power in categories others dare not underwrite: large liability, terrorism and stadium cover.

Auto insurance sits at the other end. GEICO sells a standardized product, and customers compare price and claims speed with no loyalty. Here margins are being continuously compressed by Progressive's data-driven pricing, and this quarter's -45% is the evidence.

The "upstream" therefore has to be viewed in two halves: commercial insurance is the moat, auto insurance is the battlefield. The former delivers stable but limited scale; the latter delivers large scale but thin profit.

Midstream: capital allocation is its real "core business"

The most accurate way to view Berkshire is as "a capital allocation company": its product is "converting low-cost liabilities into high-return assets," its customers are shareholders and its suppliers are policyholders.

The key to this business is the purchase price, not operational improvement. Buying Precision Castparts for RMB 32.1 billion in 2016 was the wrong price (roughly RMB 9.8 billion written down in 2020); buying Japan's five major trading houses in 2020 was the right price (dividend yield 5%+, financing cost 0.5%). Same company, same method — the difference lies in price.

The problem now is no price: RMB 377.4 billion in cash, three years without a single deployment above USD 30 billion. The middle-machine is idling, and this is the biggest risk to the valuation.

Downstream: three regulated cash-flow machines

BNSF is one of two oligopolists in the western United States, with a rail network that cannot be replicated, but its freight rates are constrained by truck competition and the Surface Transportation Board. Its value lies not in growth but in "replacement cost far above book value"—building a transcontinental railroad today would cost several hundred billion dollars.

BHE is the most capital-rich player among regulated utilities, and data-center electricity demand is turning the industry from a "bond substitute" into a "utility with volume." Wildfire litigation, however, is a long-term tail risk; several cases combined could reach USD 10 billion or more.

Manufacturing, services and retail is a grab bag: aerospace forgings (PCP), lubricant additives (Lubrizol), prefabricated housing (Clayton), food distribution (McLane). There is no synergy, but each piece generates cash flow, totaling RMB 4.04 billion per quarter, +11.8%.

Cycle position: four sub-sectors, four different clocks

Insurance: hard market peaking, drifting toward mid-cycle. Combined ratio 91.4% versus the industry average of 96%–98%, with 2–3 more quarters of decline but no collapse. Rail: recovering off the bottom. Intermodal volumes pick up with restocking and rates are flat — "volume up, price flat." Power: early stage of a demand upcycle. Data-centre electricity use pushes US power demand growth from 0.5% to 2%–3%; the capex cycle has only just begun. Manufacturing: tracking the industrial cycle, mild expansion.

The four clocks do not point in the same direction at once; combined, they produce a mild -5.8% decline. That lack of synchronisation is both the value of a conglomerate and the reason it will never run fast.

Conclusion

It sits on the funding side of the chain rather than the production side, a position that confers the strongest bargaining power (negative-cost funding, pricing power in a crisis) but the lowest growth (8%–10%).

The four sub-sectors are out of sync on the cycle, with a mild overall downturn. What really determines the next three years is not any single sub-sector but when the RMB 377.4 billion of cash converts into assets. Until then, it is simply a slow machine that does not break down.
