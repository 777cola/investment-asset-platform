## Real signal one: safe-haven flows have turned "certainty" into a crowded trade

Berkshire did not cause the AI trade collapse in mid-November, but it was the biggest beneficiary. The logic chain is clear: tech valuations rest on "discounting future cash flows," and once the discount rate or growth assumptions waver, capital flees to assets whose "cash flows are already certain." Two-thirds of Berkshire's profit comes from railroads, utilities and manufacturing — none of which has anything to do with AI.

On the numbers, the stock rose 13.2% in a month and outperformed the S&P by 19.2 percentage points, the largest since 2010. That move is not "fundamentals improved 13%" — it is "the valuation multiple re-rated from 1.45x book to 1.66x."

That is also the most fragile link: safe-haven money arrives fast and leaves faster. It does not need the company to deteriorate — only for the panic to end.

## Real signal two: management lifted the buyback from RMB 4.5 billion to RMB 9.6 billion — a statement of intent

Q3 buybacks were USD 9.647 billion, Q2 USD 4.527 billion, and Q1 only about USD 230 million (derived by subtracting from the RMB 14.4 billion nine-month total). Over three quarters, buybacks grew 40-fold. The acceleration came as the stock rose from 540 to 590 — the opposite direction. The normal logic is to buy less as the price rises.

Two explanations: ① management believes intrinsic value is far above book value (replacement cost of the railroads and utilities, unrealized gains on Apple and Amex); ② the new leadership wants to use buybacks to prop up relative performance. Either way, it points to one thing: the USD 377.4 billion will not sit in short-term Treasuries forever.

## Real signal three: earnings held up, and that matters more than "good"

Operating profit fell 5.8% and the market did not react. That is anomalous in 2026: over the same period CATL's Q3 net profit rose 14.5% yet its A-shares fell 12%, and Cambricon dropped 6% on a growth slowdown. Berkshire's profit fell 5.8% while its stock hit a new high — the buyers are not buying profit.

They are buying two things: a combined ratio of 91.4% (still profitable, no collapse) and book value +10.5% (net assets still growing). Those two keep the "defensive" label intact.

## Noise: four things that look eventful but carry no real information

① 13F position weights. Top five at 68.5%, Alphabet at 12% — a quarterly snapshot that reflects passive drift from stock-price moves, not active decisions.② Down 0.02% in a single day. 592.85 versus 592.94, a 9-cent gap; reading anything into it is overfitting.

③ The "asset shortage" narrative. The media casts "lots of cash" as "no skill" and "lots of buybacks" as "confidence"; both can be true at once, which is precisely why it is not analysis.④ The "symbolism" of the chairman handover. Announced a year ahead of the September 18 event; the market had already priced it.

The test is simple: if a development cannot be explained by cash flow, discount rate or competitive landscape, it is noise.

## The tradable part: only one

Of the three genuine signals, only the first (safe-haven flows) is tradeable, and the direction is "wait for the tide to recede", not "chase it". This kind of extreme relative performance has occurred four times in history, and each time it gave back more than half within 1–3 months.

The second (buybacks) and third (earnings didn't collapse) are "reasons to hold", not "reasons to buy". They tell you the company is fine; they don't tell you the price is right.
