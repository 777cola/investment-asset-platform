Management's assessment

2026 is the first year in six decades that Berkshire has no Buffett making the final call. Abel became CEO on January 1, Buffett handed the chairman role to Howard on September 18, and on November 7 the first quarterly report signed solely by the new leadership landed: underwriting profit -43%, buybacks raised to RMB 9.647 billion.

Assessment: the handover itself has been smooth — no management turmoil, no change in culture, shareholder letters still written. Abel's capital allocation record, however, is a blank: no USD 30 billion-scale acquisition, no cash deployed in a crisis. Only time can score this item.

Abel's first test: a pass, but not a high score

Three things stand out from this quarterly report. First, the business judgment was sound: the decline in underwriting profit reflects the industry cycle, and he did not dress it up with accounting maneuvers, and he explained the rebound in GEICO's claims costs. Second, capital returns were more aggressive: Q3 buybacks of 9.647 billion were more than double Q2's 4.527 billion, the most aggressive quarter since 2018. Third, M&A remains small deals: Taylor Morrison at 6.8 billion, plus another transaction for a combined 16.2 billion, neither large enough to change the company.

Together these three things form a portrait: a steady operator, a cautious acquirer. Not far from Buffett's portrait; the difference is that Buffett dared to swing hard when others did not (PCP for 32.1 billion in 2016, Goldman preferred for 5 billion in 2008), and Abel has yet to prove he would.

Governance structure: chairman and CEO roles are separate, a positive

From September 18, Howard Buffett will serve as chairman and Abel as CEO, separating the two roles. That is healthier than combining both titles in one person: the chairman oversees supervision and succession, the CEO runs operations, and when problems arise two people reconcile the accounts rather than one person self-auditing.

Howard's positioning is clear — non-executive, not involved in operations, with the primary duty of ensuring "the culture is not diluted." His background in agriculture and philanthropy means he will not intervene in BNSF's operating decisions. This is a deliberate design.

The risk lies at the other end: Buffett remains the largest shareholder (about 14% economic interest, about 30% voting power), and his views still carry decisive influence. The so-called "handover" is currently "handing over operating control, not influence." This ensures stability, but it also means Abel's independence can only be verified once Buffett fully exits.

Track record: one blemish, six decades clean

Berkshire's integrity record sits broadly in the top tier of the S&P 500: six decades of shareholder letters, no stock options, no lavish executive severance, a CEO salary of USD 100,000. The one real blemish came in 2011 — David Sokol bought Lubrizol shares himself while recommending that Berkshire acquire the company, pocketing about USD 3 million; Buffett learned of it only after the affair surfaced, and Sokol ultimately resigned.

The value of the episode lies not in the gossip but in what it proves: ① Buffett's information-isolation mechanism had a hole; ② the aftermath was handled thoroughly — no one retained, no excuses, full public disclosure. The second matters more than the first.

The 2026 watch item is buyback disclosure. With USD 9.6 billion repurchased at 1.66 times book, shareholders cannot judge whether the money was well spent unless the annual report discloses the weighted average price of the range.

Succession: the real test comes in the next crisis

Abel is now in his early 60s, with a normal tenure of 10–15 years. The situation he inherits is entirely different from Buffett's: when Buffett took over, the company had a market cap of USD 20 million and could buy anything; when Abel takes over, the market cap is USD 1.26 trillion, any acquisition capable of changing the company's scale must be USD 30 billion or more, and there are only a few dozen such targets worldwide.

His test, then, is not "stock picking" but "picking the right one from an extremely small opportunity set," and "acting when everyone else is afraid to move." Neither can be trained in advance.

The reasonable expectation: his first three years will be mainly about "defense"—maintaining underwriting discipline, gradually increasing buybacks, and making small acquisitions below USD 10 billion. The real test comes in the next major market downturn, when he will either become the second Buffett or be proven to be merely an excellent operator.

Compensation and incentives: no options is the biggest advantage

Berkshire does not grant executives stock options; bonuses are tied to "the capital return of the business they run," not to KPIs management sets for itself. The consequence of this design: management will not act for the sake of the short-term stock price, nor buy back stock or dress up quarterly reports to serve an exercise price.

That is especially scarce in 2026. Over the same period, tech companies have used RSUs to turn employees into people who "can only get paid if the stock price rises," whereas Berkshire's management relies on cash salary and long-term shareholding.

The drawback is insufficient incentive: Abel's pay is low relative to the CEO of a USD 1.26 trillion company, so in theory he risks being poached. Given his shareholding and age, however, that risk is small.

Conclusion

Management test: 7 out of 10. Smooth handover, clean culture, right incentives — but a blank capital-allocation record. Four successive buyback step-ups (Q1 RMB 230 million -> Q2 RMB 4.527 billion -> Q3 RMB 9.647 billion) show the new chief is willing to return capital, though doing so at 1.66x book sets a loose bar.

Abel's watchlist has only three items: the first acquisition above USD 30 billion, deployment in the next crisis, and whether the annual report discloses the weighted average buyback price. Only when all three are answered does a 9 become discussable.
