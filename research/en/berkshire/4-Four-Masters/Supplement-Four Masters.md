## Duan Yongping: You understand this business — can you live with this price?

Duan Yongping asks only three things when buying a company: is the business good, is the culture right, and is the price reasonable. On the first two, Berkshire scores full marks. Float is the cheapest money in the world — USD 178.5 billion at a combined ratio of 91.4%, with policyholders paying it to hold their money; on culture, "figure out how much you can lose before you figure out how much you can make" is an institution at Berkshire, not a slogan.

The third test fails. At USD 592.85 and 1.66 times book, the annualized return over the next decade roughly equals book value growth of 8%–10%, plus a small negative contribution from valuation reversion. Duan Yongping would say: "This is a business I'd be willing to own, but at this price I'd rather wait." He waited for 12 times PE before buying Apple, and he waited until after the plasticizer scandal before buying Moutai.

He would add a harsher point: if all you want is a 4.5% return, buy short-term bonds and skip the equity volatility. Berkshire's expected return now is about the same as short-term bonds, but the risk is far greater.

## Buffett: This is a company you'd want to fall, but right now it's rising

Buffett has a much-quoted line: Berkshire is the kind of stock "you want the price to go down" because the company keeps buying back shares. In Q3 2026 that line acquired an awkward new version — the company repurchased USD 9.647 billion at 1.66x book, the largest single quarter since 2018.

His own standard is "buy back only below intrinsic value." Either he believes intrinsic value sits far above book (plausible: unrealized gains on Apple, Amex and Coca-Cola are carried at cost and never hit book; BNSF and BHE also carry well below replacement cost), or the standard has loosened. Either way, investors should downgrade "buyback" from a value signal to capital return.

He would stress the other side too: USD 377.4 billion of cash is worth something when others panic. But when everyone knows it is worth something and will pay 1.66x book for it, that option is priced rich. The real opportunity was 2022, when book was 1.2x and nobody wanted it.

## Munger: Invert — what would make it fall 20%

Munger's approach is to invert the question: don't ask why it will rise, ask what would make it fall 20%. Three paths, ranked by probability.

First, an AI trade rebound.Over the past month Berkshire has outperformed the S&P 500 by 19.2 percentage points, its largest rolling monthly relative gain since 2010 and the biggest in 30 years after 2008. Every such extreme divergence stems from the market repricing "certainty." In mid-November, single-day swings in tech stocks already reached over 5%; once the panic ends, the premium gives way. This is the most realistic path.

Second, deterioration in the underwriting cycle.A price war in auto insurance plus one major catastrophe could push the combined ratio from 91.4% above 100%.Third, the new management makes a large, expensive acquisition.Abel has yet to do a USD 30 billion deal, and the market will discount his first big M&A.

Munger would add: none of these three requires the company to get worse, only for "the people who bought it to change their minds."

## Li Lu: A call option on the dollar system, but the option is expensive

Li Lu would place Berkshire on a longer timeline: it is essentially a USD-denominated, diversified cash-flow portfolio levered with float, while also holding USD 377.4 billion of short-term debt. In an era when dollar credit is questioned and geopolitical risk is rising, this structure is itself a scarce asset—it collects cash flow from USD assets while retaining the ability to convert debt claims into real assets at any time.

The holdings in Japan's five major trading houses are the other side of the same logic: funding low-interest JPY liabilities to buy JPY assets, earning the spread and asset appreciation. This is not a financial trick but a judgment on "which currency is depreciating."

But he would point out the boundary condition: this scarcity can only be cashed in when "others run into trouble." If no cheap assets are available to buy over the next three years, the USD 377.4 billion yields only 4.4%. Buying Berkshire is essentially a bet that "there will be a major fire sale"—and that bet now costs a 66% book premium.

## Odds assessment: positive expected value, but not worth risking 18% downside for 2.4%

Weight the three scenarios by probability: 0.3×(-18%) + 0.5×(+6%) + 0.2×(+24%) = +2.4%. Mathematically this is a positive expectation, but the structure is "win 6% or lose 18%" — payoff ratio 1:3, win rate 50%.

The problem is the subjectivity of the probability assignments. If the pessimistic probability rises from 30% to 40% (AI trades have already rebounded, so this assumption is not aggressive), the expectation becomes -1.2%, turning negative outright. In other words, the expected value of this investment is highly sensitive to a single variable — market sentiment — rather than to the company's operations.

That is why all four of us reached the same conclusion: the company is not the problem; the margin for error at this price is too thin. At 1.4x book (about USD 500), the neutral scenario becomes +18% and the pessimistic scenario loses only 6%; under the same set of probabilities the expectation immediately doubles. Same company, same analysis — only the price differs, and the conclusion shifts from "watch" to "buy".
