## Title and lead-in

Stock at an all-time high while profit fell 5.8%: the USD 377.4 billion question this Berkshire report leaves unanswered

Bottom line first: good company, wrong price. Would-be buyers should wait for a pullback to USD 500.

Berkshire reported its first full quarter under Abel on November 7. The market barely blinked — the stock kept climbing to USD 592.85 by November 16, just 2 cents shy of its 52-week high.

## I. First, the numbers: what fell fell, and what rose rose

Operating profit USD 12.705 billion, -5.8% YoY; net profit USD 22.112 billion, -28.2% YoY. Insurance underwriting profit USD 1.352 billion, -43% YoY, of which GEICO USD 982 million, -45%.

On that reading the story ends here: auto insurance is broken.

But the other half of the same statement tells a different story: BNSF Railway USD 1.729 billion, +19.3%; Berkshire Hathaway Energy USD 1.612 billion, +8.3%; manufacturing, services and retail USD 4.042 billion, +11.8%. Together these three total USD 7.383 billion, +12.6% YoY, filling most of the insurance hole.

The -5.8% headline is therefore the net of insurance down 26% and the real businesses up 12.6%: not a broad collapse, but half retreating and half advancing.

## 2. Underwriting profit fell 43% — why that is not bad news

It comes down to one number: the combined ratio of 91.4%.

For every USD 100 of premiums collected, USD 91.4 goes out in claims and expenses, leaving USD 8.6 of profit. Last year that number was about USD 15. This is a return from windfall to normal, not a loss.

The driver is the auto insurance cycle. From 2023 to 2025, auto insurance pricing rose faster than claims and the whole industry booked three years of excess profit. In 2026, claims costs (repairs, used cars, medical) catch up and pricing starts to soften. This is not Berkshire's problem alone; Progressive and Allstate face the same.

The real warning line is 100%. It is still 8.6 points away.

## III. Why the stock keeps rising: panicked capital has found a landing spot

The AI trade swung violently in mid-November. CoreWeave fell more than 70% from its high, and single-day swings in tech stocks ran to double digits.

Where did the money go? Into an asset with no position in any AI theme, two-thirds of its profit drawn from railroads, power and manufacturing, and USD 377.4 billion of short-term Treasuries on the balance sheet.

Over the past month, Berkshire outperformed the S&P 500 by 19.2 percentage points, its largest rolling monthly relative gain since 2010 and second only to the 2008 financial crisis in 30 years.

This is not earnings-driven; it is flow-driven. It does not require the company to improve, only for others to deteriorate.

## IV. The real question: where will the USD 377.4 billion go

Cash of USD 40.7 billion plus short-term Treasuries of USD 336.7 billion totals USD 377.4 billion, equal to 30% of market cap.

That money earns 4.4%–4.6% a year, or over USD 10 billion. It does not lose value, but it does not generate excess returns either.

Meanwhile, Q3 buybacks totaled USD 9.647 billion, more than double Q2 and the most aggressive quarter since 2018. First three quarters: USD 14.4 billion of buybacks and USD 16.2 billion of M&A spend (including USD 6.8 billion for Taylor Morrison). USD 30.6 billion spent, and cash still rose — the arithmetic proof that the money cannot be spent fast enough.

Three destinations, three outcomes: keep buying short-term debt and the company becomes a 4.5% quasi-bond; large-scale M&A and earnings per share step up immediately; buybacks at high prices amount to paying USD 1.66 for USD 1. The valuation the market assigns today already prices in the second outcome.

## V. Abel's first report card: 7 points

Took over as CEO on January 1; Buffett handed him the chairman role on September 18. This is the first quarterly report in sixty years without Buffett's signature.

He got three things right: he did not gloss over bad news, he raised buybacks from USD 4.5 billion to USD 9.6 billion, and he kept making small-scale acquisitions.But none of it proves he would act boldly in a crisis.

Buffett's legend rests not on the 19.9% annualised return but on his record as a buyer in all four crises of 1973, 1987, 2008 and 2020. Abel has yet to live through one. Those 3 points can only be earned in the next panic.

## VI. Valuation: 6% upside, 18% downside

Book value per share is USD 536,590 for A shares and USD 357.7 for B shares. At a share price of 592.85, P/B is 1.66x against a historical range of 1.2–1.9x and a midpoint of 1.4x.

On a different yardstick, annualised operating profit this quarter was USD 50.8 billion against a market cap of USD 1.26 trillion, or 24.8x. For a company growing 8%–10% a year, that multiple is not cheap.

Three scenarios: bear case USD 488 (-18%), base case USD 626 (+6%), bull case USD 734 (+24%). Weighted at 30/50/20 probabilities, expected return is +2.4% — barely above short-term bonds, yet carrying 18% downside.

## Seven. Conclusion: a good company, waiting for the price

The entry point is specific: start building below USD 500 (1.4x book), add to 5% at USD 460 and to 8% at USD 430. Cap at 8%: this is ballast, not an engine.

Any one of three triggers justifies acting early: a single acquisition above USD 30 billion, underwriting profit back to USD 2 billion/quarter, or the stock falling below 500.

One honest caveat to close: Berkshire is most dangerous precisely when it looks safest. Today everyone knows it is a safe haven, and that perception is already priced into the 1.66x book premium.

This company is worth waiting for. It is not worth chasing.
