## Position in the value chain: it is the buyer's buyer

An ordinary company sits in the middle of the "supplier—itself—customer" chain, and its bargaining power depends on how many substitutes exist. Berkshire sits not on this chain but above it: it buys a slice of the entire chain (railroads, electricity, manufacturing) while providing insurance and capital to the entire chain.

That confers an advantage others lack: it can use money from insurance liabilities to buy physical assets, at a negative cost of capital. A railroad issuing bonds to build track pays 5% interest; Berkshire builds with float at a cost of -1%. On the same asset, that is a 6-point difference in return.

The disadvantage is that it must manage two kinds of business at once: underwriting discipline (no price cuts for the sake of scale) and capital discipline (no reckless buying for the sake of growth). Historically, its mistakes have fallen into both categories.

## The Thinnest Link: Switching Costs of Just 5 Cents

Auto insurance is Berkshire's least sticky business. At each annual renewal, customers shop comparison sites and will switch for USD 50. GEICO retains policyholders through price and claims experience, not contracts. Competition will therefore always compress its profitability back to the industry average.

Railroads are somewhat stickier: dedicated lines, multi-year agreements and integrated dispatch systems mean switching suppliers takes months. But bulk shippers (coal, grain) are highly sensitive to freight rates and use trucks to push prices down.

Stickiness in manufacturing depends on the company: PCP's aerospace forging certification cycle runs 3–5 years, so stickiness is strong; Clayton's prefabricated-home buyers are price-driven, so stickiness is weak. An overall score of 5, the weakest link in this portfolio and one reason its ROE is only 10.4%.

## The most dangerous competitor isn't another insurer — it's Progressive plus 4.5% Treasuries

Layer one: Progressive. In auto insurance, Progressive uses usage-based insurance (UBI) to keep its loss ratio 3–5 points below GEICO's while taking share through advertising and direct sales. GEICO's profit fell 45% this quarter — partly cyclical, partly a deliberate cession of share. Three consecutive quarters of declining in-force policy counts at GEICO would signal structural defeat.

Layer two: 4.5% US short-term Treasuries. This is the truly lethal competitor. With the risk-free rate at 4.5%, an equity yielding 8%–10% annualised with 15% volatility does not win on a risk-adjusted basis. Berkshire itself holds 377.4 billion in short-term Treasuries — a de facto admission of high opportunity cost.

The two competitors differ in nature: Progressive is taking profit, short-term Treasuries are taking capital. The latter is harder to fight because it is macro.
