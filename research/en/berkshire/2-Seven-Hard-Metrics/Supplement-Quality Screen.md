## ROE of 10.4% does not, on its own, make this a bad business

The 15% ROE threshold assumes asset-light companies that compound earnings. Berkshire carries USD 761.4 billion of equity, of which 377.4 billion sits in cash and short-term bonds—by definition that pile can only earn 4.4%, and it mathematically caps ROE.

Do the division: assume the entire cash balance is put into 4.5% Treasuries, and the return on net assets for that portion is 4.5%. The remaining 384.0 billion of operating assets generate 50.8 billion of annualised operating profit, a return of about 13.2%. The return on the physical assets is adequate; cash drags it down to 10.4%.

Failing this test therefore means "slower compounding," not "bad business." The valuation implication is direct: book value rises 8%–10% a year, and that number plus valuation swings is the ceiling on long-term stock returns.

## The second caveat: underwriting margin halved

Underwriting profit was USD 1.352 billion, down 43% YoY, on a combined ratio of 91.4%. Every dollar of premium still earned 8.6 cents, versus about 15 cents last year (roughly calculated on a comparable premium basis). GEICO was the main drag, with profit of USD 982 million, down 45%.

The cause is the auto insurance cycle. From 2023 to 2025, auto insurance pricing rose faster than claims and the industry booked three years of excess profit; in 2026, claims costs (repairs, used cars, medical) caught up and pricing began to soften. This is not a problem unique to Berkshire; Progressive has the same issue.

The test is simple: a combined ratio below 95% is healthy; only above 100% is a real problem. At 91.4% now, there are still 8.6 points to the red line.

## Of the five criteria passed, liabilities and share capital are the toughest

Liabilities: Interest-bearing debt stands at roughly USD 130 billion, all of it backed by specific assets—BNSF's locomotives and rail network, BHE's power plants, Clayton's mortgage portfolio. The parent carries almost no net debt, the precondition for acting as buyer of last resort.

Share capital: Buybacks totalled USD 9.647 billion in Q3 and USD 14.4 billion for the first three quarters, shrinking the share count. That matters especially in 2026, when many tech companies are issuing equity at high prices, issuing convertible bonds and granting stock to employees.

Cash flow: Insurance "collects first, pays claims later," so operating cash flow naturally exceeds net income. This quarter's net profit of USD 22.112 billion includes large unrealised investment gains and is not cash, but operating profit of USD 12.705 billion is real.

## Is it fatal? No, but expectations should be lowered.

Neither issue is fatal, and both point to the same conclusion: this is an 8%–10% compounder, not a 15% growth stock.

The negative screen exists to filter out structurally bad companies—high leverage, share dilution, fabricated cash flow, ROE propped up by leverage. Berkshire touches none of these. Its problem is different: good company + high price = mediocre returns.

So the verdict at this gate is not "reject" but "passes, but don't expect surprises." The real decision belongs at the fifth gate: valuation.
