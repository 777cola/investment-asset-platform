Synthesis of Views — Berkshire

The consensus of four people, one real contradiction, and one actionable line

Core view

Four people read the same report and reached broadly the same conclusion: operations are fine, the price is the problem. What is really worth writing down is one contradiction—buybacks.

Consensus: three

① The profit decline is cyclical, not a quality problem. All four mention the 91.4% combined ratio and view it as "earning less." ② The three physical businesses are the valuation floor. RMB 7.383 billion pre-tax, +12.6%, about RMB 29.5 billion annualized — the only profit not dependent on market sentiment. ③ Where the cash goes is the core variable. All four list the RMB 377.4 billion as the top item to watch, more important than quarterly swings in underwriting profit.

Contradiction: the RMB 9.6 billion buyback — rational, or a loosening of discipline

Duan Yongping said "acceptable, but not a signal"; Buffett said "I can't bring myself to buy"; Munger said "not very Munger"; Li Lu said "price-sensitive". None of the four saw it as a plus, but none saw it as a minus either.

Break it down and the contradiction resolves: the rationale for buybacks depends on the ratio of intrinsic value to book value. If true intrinsic value is 1.4x book, buying back at 1.66x is only slightly expensive; if it is 1.2x, the money was 30% misallocated. And outside investors can never pin down that ratio — precisely the difficulty in valuing Berkshire.

Valuation: why four people arrived at three different views of "expensive"

Duan Yongping uses "ten-year annualized return": book value grows 8% a year, entry premium 66%, ten-year return diluted to about 6%, not much better than short-term bonds — expensive. Buffett uses "intrinsic value/book value": if after revaluing the railroad, the power business and unrealized stock gains intrinsic value is 1.4x book, then 1.66x is only slightly expensive — mildly expensive. Li Lu uses "the time value of cash": RMB 377.4 billion undeployed for three years, the option is decaying, the fair PB should be revised down — more expensive.

The difference between the three answers is not the arithmetic, it is the denominator. Book value, revalued value, discounted cash — three numbers. This explains why some people always find Berkshire's stock cheap and others always find it expensive — it has no single correct valuation anchor.

Our approach: use the most conservative book-value basis for buy decisions (1.66x → expensive), and allow the revalued basis when judging management behavior (buybacks are not reckless spending). Use the two bases separately, never mix them to talk ourselves into a position.

Conclusion

The one-line takeaway: this earnings report tells you the company isn't broken; it doesn't tell you the stock is cheap. Two decisions must be made separately — if you want to own the business, you can wait patiently; if you want to buy the stock, wait for USD 500.
