Synthesis of Views — Berkshire

Four views, one genuine contradiction and one actionable line
Research date 2026/11/16  ·  Price $592.85 (-0.02%)  ·  Market cap About USD 1.26 trillion  ·  PE(TTM) Approximately 16.6x
← Back to the hub

Core view

Four readers of the same report reached broadly the same conclusion: operations are fine, the price is the problem. What is really worth flagging is a single contradiction—buybacks.

Consensus: three

① The profit decline is cyclical, not a quality problem. All four flag the 91.4% combined ratio and read it as "earning less." ② The three physical businesses are the valuation floor. RMB 7.383 billion pre-tax, +12.6%, or roughly RMB 29.5 billion annualized — the only profit stream not hostage to market sentiment. ③ Where the cash goes is the core variable. All four put the RMB 377.4 billion at the top of their watch list, ahead of quarterly swings in underwriting profit.

Contradiction: the RMB 9.6 billion buyback — rational, or a loosening of discipline

Duan Yongping called it "acceptable, but not a signal"; Buffett said "I can't bring myself to buy"; Munger said "not very Munger"; Li Lu called it "price-sensitive". None of the four treated it as a plus, but none treated it as a minus either.

The contradiction resolves once you break it down: the case for buybacks rests on the ratio of intrinsic value to book value. If true intrinsic value is 1.4x book, buying back at 1.66x is only slightly expensive; if it is 1.2x, 30% of the money was misallocated. Outside investors can never pin down that ratio — which is precisely what makes Berkshire so hard to value.

Valuation: why four people reached three different views of "expensive"

Duan Yongping anchors on "ten-year annualised return": book value compounding at 8% a year, a 66% entry premium, and the ten-year return dilutes to roughly 6% — not much better than short-term bonds. Expensive. Buffett anchors on "intrinsic value/book value": revalue the railroad, the power business and unrealised stock gains, and if intrinsic value comes out at 1.4x book, then 1.66x is only slightly expensive — mildly expensive. Li Lu anchors on "the time value of cash": RMB 377.4 billion left undeployed for three years is a decaying option, so the fair PB should be revised down — more expensive.

The gap between the three answers is not arithmetic, it is the denominator: book value, revalued value, discounted cash — three different numbers. That is why some investors always find Berkshire cheap and others always find it expensive: the stock has no single correct valuation anchor.

Our approach: use the most conservative book-value basis for buy decisions (1.66x → expensive), and allow the revalued basis when judging management behaviour (buybacks are not reckless spending). Keep the two bases separate — never mix them to talk ourselves into a position.

Conclusion

The one-line takeaway: this earnings report tells you the company isn't broken; it doesn't tell you the stock is cheap. Two decisions have to be made separately — if you want to own the business, you can wait patiently; if you want to buy the stock, wait for USD 500.
