Where the RMB 377.4 billion went matters a hundred times more than this quarter's profit

Core view

The only real news in this quarterly report is that cash rose again. Falling profit is old news; the cash pile is new—and it will determine returns over the next decade.

Three possible destinations for the cash

① Keep buying short-term debt. At 4.5% annualised, the company becomes a money-market fund: ROE settles around 10% and the share price tracks book value.② Large acquisitions. Only a few dozen targets globally exceed USD 30 billion, and most are regulated utilities or industrial assets—available, but rarely cheap.③ Buybacks. Repurchasing at 1.66x book means paying USD 1.66 for USD 1, unless intrinsic value is genuinely higher.

Of the three paths, ② offers the most upside for shareholders and is the hardest to execute. ① is the safest and the most mediocre. ③ is the compromise, but price-sensitive.

Its position in the dollar system is its most undervalued asset

Viewing Berkshire as merely "a US conglomerate" underestimates it. It is also a reservoir of USD liquidity: USD 377.4 billion of short-term debt can be converted into any currency or any asset within 24 hours. With dollar credit now in question and geopolitical conflict frequent, that capability is itself a scarce asset.

The Japanese trading house holdings rehearse the same logic: borrow yen, buy yen assets, capture the spread. This is not arbitrage; it is a judgment on the monetary order.

But this kind of value is only realized in a "crisis." In peacetime, it is just 4.5%. So buyers are essentially buying a ticket that says "I have priority purchase rights in the next crisis." The problem is that ticket now costs a 66% premium.

Conclusion

Ten years out, the number that matters in this quarterly report is not -5.8% but RMB 377.4 billion. Deploy that capital and Berkshire is worth USD 1.5 trillion; sit on it and it is worth USD 1.1 trillion.

The current price already assumes the money gets invested.
