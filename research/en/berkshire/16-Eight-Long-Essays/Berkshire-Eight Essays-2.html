2) Business model: a machine that uses other people's money to buy assets — Berkshire

Core view

Berkshire's business model can be summed up in one sentence: fund positive-return assets with negative-cost liabilities, then spend the rest of the time doing nothing. Three things, none of them difficult; what is difficult is doing them without interruption for sixty years.

First: negative-cost liabilities

The essence of float is "collect first, pay later": customers pay premiums up front, while claims are paid out slowly over the coming years or even decades. Until claims are settled, the money belongs to the company and can be invested. If underwriting is also profitable, the cost of this money is negative.

Berkshire's float of USD 178.5 billion, combined with a combined ratio of 91.4% this quarter, means it not only uses this money for free but earns an extra 8.6%. On that math, the "interest rate" on the float is roughly -1% to -2% — below the deposit cost at any bank and 6 percentage points below Treasuries.

What makes this advantage scarce: it requires a AAA-rated balance sheet as backing. Customers dare to hand Berkshire massive liability insurance because they trust it will still be around in 20 years. New companies cannot replicate this, lacking a sixty-year track record.

Second: buy assets with positive returns

With cheap money in hand, the next question is what to buy. Berkshire's selection criteria have not changed in decades: understandable, moat-protected, reasonably priced, trustworthy management. All four are required.

The result is three asset classes: ① wholly owned subsidiaries (BNSF, BHE, PCP, Clayton, Lubrizol, McLane); ② large minority stakes (Apple, Amex, Coca-Cola, Bank of America, Alphabet, with the top five accounting for 68.5% of the 13F); ③ short-term Treasuries (RMB 336.7 billion).

Each generates a different return stream: subsidiaries deliver stable cash flow (about RMB 29.5 billion pre-tax annualized), minority stakes deliver market-value volatility plus dividends, and short-term debt yields 4.5%. The current portfolio is "one-third operating businesses, one-third equities, one-third cash" — a ratio that is itself a judgment on the market: too many assets look expensive.

The third point: do nothing

This is the hardest part to grasp and the most widely misunderstood. From 2023 to 2026 Berkshire was a net seller of equities, stockpiling cash to 377.4 billion — a stance the media labelled an "asset shortage." Yet not buying is itself a decision: it is equivalent to saying "I do not accept the current price."

The opportunity cost is quantifiable. Had that 377.4 billion gone into the S&P 500 at a 10% annualised return, it would have earned roughly 110 billion more over three years; in reality it earned 4.5%, or about 60 billion less. The maths looks poor, but it is intrinsic to Buffett's system: better to earn less than to lose big.

The essence of this business, then, is not "strong stock-picking ability" but "the ability to refrain from acting when others must act." That freedom stems from having no maturity dates on the liability side and no leverage on the asset side.

Conclusion

Negative-cost liabilities, positive-return assets and the freedom not to act — combine all three and you have Berkshire. Take any one away and the model breaks: without float it is just an ordinary holding company; without the assets, float is a liability; without the freedom to "choose not to act," it would be forced to buy into bubbles.
