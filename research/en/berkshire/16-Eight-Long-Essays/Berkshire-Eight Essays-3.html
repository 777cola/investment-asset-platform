③ Moat anatomy: four wide but shallow walls — Berkshire

Core view

Moats come in two kinds: one lets you raise prices (Moutai, Apple), the other keeps you alive (Berkshire). Berkshire is the latter — and the most extreme version of it.

Wall 1: GEICO's cost structure (8/10)

Direct sales eliminate agent commissions, making the same policy 10%–15% cheaper. That moat was decisive when Berkshire acquired GEICO in 1995, but by 2026 it has eroded: Progressive is using UBI (usage-based insurance) to drive its loss ratio lower, and GEICO's advantage has shifted from structural to operational.

GEICO's underwriting profit of 982 million this quarter, down -45%, is the reading that shows this wall thinning. It is still there, but it is no longer "others cannot break in"—it is "others need time to break in."

Wall two: BNSF's rail network (8/10); wall three: regulatory licences (9/10)

Only two transcontinental railroads run through the western United States: BNSF and Union Pacific. A third cannot be built under today's economics — environmental review, land acquisition, capital expenditure. This is a physical monopoly, harder than a brand.

It cannot, however, raise prices at will: shippers can switch to trucks, and the Surface Transportation Board reviews rates. Profit growth therefore comes from "volume + efficiency," not "price hikes." This quarter's +19.3% reflects volume recovery.

BHE's moat is the franchise: a state permits only one company to build the power grid. Returns are regulated at a fixed percentage of capital expenditure (typically 9%–11%), but competition is shut out entirely. This is a "low return, zero risk" business, best held with a bond mindset.

Wall 4: Balance sheet and reputation (10/10) — the one asset that cannot be replicated

USD 377.4 billion in cash and short-term bonds, plus the "Buffett's company" nameplate. That wall counts for little in normal times but everything in a crisis: in 2008 Goldman Sachs and General Electric queued up for its preferred stock (10% dividend); in 2020 the market waited for it to act (it didn't, but everyone was waiting).

This wall took sixty years to build; replicating it takes not money but time and reputation.Any new company that put up USD 377.4 billion in cash still wouldn't get the first phone call at such moments—because trust cannot be bought with money.

But it has one fatal weakness: it depends on a "person."How much of Buffett's judgment, network and reputation Abel has inherited, no one knows today. Of the four walls, this is the only one that "may thin over time."

The Thinnest Link: A Switching Cost of Just 5 Cents

All four walls "prevent entry"; none "prevent churn." Auto insurance customers comparison-shop every year and switch over a USD 50 price gap; rail shippers can play trucking against rail to force rates down; manufacturing has no binding contracts. The most counterintuitive thing about Berkshire's moat: the walls are high, but the door is unlocked.

The consequence is quantifiable. It must spend heavily every year (advertising, claims, customer service) just to retain customers, and those costs eat into what could otherwise be higher profit. That is why its ROE is only 10.4%, while a company with genuine switching costs (Apple's ecosystem, Microsoft's Office) can exceed 30%.

One number suffices to judge whether this wall is thinning: GEICO's in-force policies. If the number is rising, it is winning the price war; if falling, the wall is collapsing. Profit lags; policy count leads.

Conclusion

Four walls, weighted score 7.6 (brand 9, cost 8, network 7, switching cost 5, licence 9). They guarantee Berkshire "won't die", not that it "runs fast".

That is why it works as ballast, not as an engine: at Berkshire, the width of the moat and the speed of growth pull in opposite directions.
