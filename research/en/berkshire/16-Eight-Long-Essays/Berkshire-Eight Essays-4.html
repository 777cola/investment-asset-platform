④ Financial reality: 16.6x PE is a trap — Berkshire

Deep-dive series 4/8 · Three measures, three answers
Research date 2026/11/16  ·  Price $592.85 (-0.02%)  ·  Market cap About USD 1.26 trillion  ·  PE(TTM) Approximately 16.6x
← Back to the hub

Core view

Every argument that Berkshire is "cheap" rests on a single number: a P/E of 16.6x, below the S&P 500's 20x. That number is wrong — or rather, it measures the wrong thing.

Basis 1: 16.6x GAAP net profit — the least meaningful of the three

TTM GAAP net profit is about RMB 76 billion against a RMB 1.26 trillion market cap, implying 16.6x P/E. That looks cheap, but the net profit line includes tens of billions of unrealised investment gains: GAAP net profit this quarter was RMB 22.112 billion versus operating profit of just RMB 12.705 billion, and the RMB 9.4 billion gap is entirely mark-to-market gains and losses on equities.

Pricing off a P/E built on unrealised gains is tantamount to valuing the company on how much the market rose this year. In 2022 the S&P fell 19%, Berkshire's GAAP net profit turned negative and its P/E went to infinity — yet the underlying business had not changed at all.

Basis two: 24.8x operating profit — the real price

Operating profit of RMB 12.705 billion, or RMB 50.8 billion annualised, against a market cap of RMB 1.26 trillion — 24.8x. That is what you actually pay for the business.

Is 24.8x expensive? The S&P 500 trades at about 20x, utilities at about 18x and quality industrials at 18–22x. Berkshire's growth of 8%–10% is a touch faster than utilities and slower than quality industrials. A 25x multiple means paying a 25%–35% premium for "certainty."

That 25%–35% is the safe-haven premium. The market bid it up in November 2026; the company did not earn it.

Basis 3: 1.66x book value — the most stable anchor

B-share equivalent book value per share is USD 357.7 against a share price of 592.85, a P/B of 1.66. This is the most reliable anchor: it is unaffected by quarterly swings in investment gains or by changes in tax rates.

The historical range runs from 1.2x (2011, March 2020) to 1.9x (1998), with a midpoint around 1.4x. At 1.66x the stock sits in the 70th percentile — not extremely expensive, but certainly not cheap.

One counterintuitive point: Berkshire's book value understates intrinsic value. Rail and utility assets are carried at historical cost, and replacement cost may be 1.5–2x book; unrealised gains on Apple and Amex are not fully reflected on the balance sheet either. On that basis, "true P/B" may be 1.3–1.4x.

For a buy decision, however, you cannot value the stock off revalued figures — that is management's math for buybacks, not the investor's.

Conclusion

Three metrics, three answers: 16.6x (cheap), 24.8x (expensive), 1.66x (on the pricey side). The right approach: use GAAP P/E to rule out buying the wrong kind of stock, the operating profit multiple to judge whether it is expensive, and P/B to set the entry point.

Investors who rely on the first number alone rush in at every tech-stock selloff; those who rely on the second never find an entry point.
