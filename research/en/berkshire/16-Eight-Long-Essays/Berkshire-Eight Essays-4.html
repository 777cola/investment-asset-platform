④ Financial reality: 16.6x PE is a trap — Berkshire

Deep-dive series 4/8 · Three measures, three answers

Core view

Every discussion of Berkshire being "cheap" starts with one number: a PE of 16.6x, cheaper than the S&P 500's 20x. That number is wrong — or rather, it measures the wrong thing.

Basis 1: 16.6x GAAP net profit — the least meaningful of the three

TTM GAAP net profit is about RMB 76 billion, market cap RMB 1.26 trillion, PE 16.6x. Sounds cheap. But that net profit includes tens of billions in unrealized investment gains — GAAP net profit this quarter was RMB 22.112 billion, operating profit only RMB 12.705 billion, and the RMB 9.4 billion gap is entirely mark-to-market gains and losses on equities.

Calculating PE on profit that includes unrealized gains is equivalent to pricing the company off how much the market rose this year. In 2022 the S&P fell 19% and Berkshire's GAAP net profit was negative, making its PE infinite — yet the business had not changed at all.

Basis two: 24.8x operating profit — the real price

Operating profit of RMB 12.705 billion, annualized RMB 50.8 billion, market cap RMB 1.26 trillion, 24.8x. This is what you actually pay for the business.

Is 24.8x expensive? For comparison: the S&P 500 trades at about 20x, utilities at about 18x, quality industrials at 18–22x. Berkshire's growth is 8%–10%, a bit faster than utilities, slower than quality industrials. A 25x multiple means paying a 25%–35% premium for "certainty."

That 25%–35% is the "safe-haven premium." The market bid it up in November 2026; the company did not earn it.

Basis 3: 1.66x book value — the most stable anchor

B-share equivalent book value per share is USD 357.7, the stock trades at 592.85, PB 1.66. This is the most reliable number because it is unaffected by quarterly swings in investment gains and by changes in tax rates.

Historical range: 1.2x (2011, March 2020) to 1.9x (1998). Midpoint around 1.4x. At 1.66x it sits in the 70th percentile — not extremely expensive, but certainly not cheap.

One counterintuitive point: Berkshire's book value understates intrinsic value. Rail and utility assets are carried at historical cost, and replacement cost may be 1.5–2x book; unrealized gains on Apple and Amex are not fully on the balance sheet either. So "true PB" may be 1.3–1.4x.

But for a buy decision, you cannot discount the stock using revalued figures — that is management's math for buybacks, not the investor's math.

Conclusion

Three measures, three answers: 16.6x (cheap), 24.8x (expensive), 1.66x (on the pricey side). The right approach: use GAAP PE to rule out buying the wrong kind of stock, use the operating profit multiple to judge whether it is expensive, and use PB to set the entry point.

Those who use only the first number rush in at every tech-stock crash; those who use only the second never find an entry point.
