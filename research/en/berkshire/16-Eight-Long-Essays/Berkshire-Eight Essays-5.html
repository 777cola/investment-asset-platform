⑤ Industry and competition: four clocks, one direction — Berkshire

Core view

Berkshire has no single "industry" — it has four. Judging its future means judging four cycles at once: insurance, rail, power and manufacturing. As of November 2026, the four clocks read: downturn, recovery, upswing, moderate.

Insurance: hard market gives way to soft, with 2–3 quarters to run

2023–2025 were the golden three years for global P&C insurance: auto rates rose more than 40%, commercial property premiums surged on frequent catastrophes, and industry-wide underwriting profit hit a record. Berkshire was one of the biggest beneficiaries—underwriting profit for the same period in 2025 was 2.369 billion.

The turning point came in 2026: claims inflation caught up (repairs, medical, used cars) and commercial insurance prices fell as reinsurance capital flooded in.Underwriting profit this quarter was 1.352 billion, -43%, with a combined ratio of 91.4%—a return from "windfall" to "normal."

Industry position: not yet at a loss, but the downturn has another 2–3 quarters to run. The basis for this call is that peers (Progressive, Chubb) are seeing underwriting margins decline in tandem, pointing to a cycle rather than a company-specific problem.

Railways, power, manufacturing: three unsynchronized clocks

Rail is recovering. BNSF USD 1.729 billion, +19.3%, with intermodal volumes recovering on manufacturing restocking. Pricing, however, is flat: excess truck capacity weighs on rates and rail dare not raise prices. The result is volume up, price flat, with profit growth coming from efficiency.

Power is in the early upcycle. BHE USD 1.612 billion, +8.3%, looks unremarkable, but demand is shifting: AI data centres are pushing US power demand growth from 0.5% to 2%–3%, something unseen in two decades. The capex cycle has only just begun; over the next five years BHE's rate base will keep expanding.

Manufacturing is expanding moderately. USD 4.042 billion, +11.8%, with aerospace components (PCP) recovering on flight hours, housing (Clayton) improving as rates peak and chemicals (Lubrizol) tracking industrial utilisation.

The four engines are out of sync, and together they come to -5.8%. That out-of-sync quality is the value of a conglomerate: they don't all collapse at once, and they don't all boom at once.

Competition: the real rivals are Progressive and the 4.5% Treasury

Progressive has pushed risk-based pricing in auto insurance to its logical extreme with UBI: good drivers pay less, bad drivers pay more, and its loss ratio runs 3–5 points below the industry. That model is steadily eating into GEICO's share. If GEICO's in-force policy count falls for three consecutive quarters, that is structural loss of ground, not a cycle.

Competition also comes from capital markets: with a 4.5% risk-free yield, equities offering 8%–10% annualised returns with 15% volatility look less attractive. This is not one company stealing business; it is the entire equity asset class being repriced. Berkshire itself holding USD 377.4 billion in short-term Treasuries is an admission of this in action.

Conclusion

Four industries, four cycles: insurance down, rail recovering, power up, manufacturing mild. Together they form a portfolio that will neither collapse nor accelerate.

Only M&A can change that. Which is why where the RMB 377.4 billion goes matters more than any single quarter's underwriting profit.
