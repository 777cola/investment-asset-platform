Core view

Risks split two ways: those that cost you money and those that degrade the business. Berkshire carries seven of the first and only one of the second.

Category 1: those that can make the business worse (1 item)

① Underwriting discipline breaks down. If the combined ratio exceeds 100% for two consecutive years, float flips from "negative-cost capital" to a "positive-cost liability" and the foundation of the entire business model collapses. This is the only risk that can truly destroy Berkshire.

Probability: low. Historically it approached 100% only in some years around 2016, and each time the company actively shrank its book. But this is the only risk that calls for "liquidating" rather than "adding."

Category two: those that would push the share price down (7 items)

② Flight of safe-haven capital. High probability, -15% to -20% impact. The stock has outperformed the S&P by 19.2 percentage points over the past month; divergence of this magnitude has occurred only four times in 30 years, and each time it reversed within 1–3 months.

③ Auto insurance share loss. Medium probability, -5% to -8% impact. GEICO profit -45%; if policy count also starts to decline, this becomes a structural problem. ④ Wildfire litigation. Medium probability, -3% to -5% impact (one-off). Liability across several western states could reach USD 10 billion+. ⑤ Large, expensive acquisitions. Low probability, -8% to -15% impact. The 2016 PCP writedown of USD 9.8 billion is precedent.

⑥ Rising interest rates. Medium probability, -5% impact. Higher discount rates; 1.66x book value won't hold. ⑦ Buffett reduces stake or dies. Low probability (he has moved to chairman emeritus), -5% impact (sentiment, not fundamentals). ⑧ Curse of scale. Probability 100%, ongoing impact. At a market cap of USD 1.26 trillion, annualized returns will stay below the S&P long term.

How to use this checklist

The seven "stock-price risks" need pricing, not hedging—together they justify a valuation discount of roughly 15%, which is why the entry point should be USD 500 rather than 592.

One "business risk" needs monitoring, not forecasting—it has only four observable indicators: the combined ratio, float size, GEICO policy count, and whether any large acquisition is made at more than 2x book value. Checking these four indicators once a quarter is more useful than reading a hundred research reports.

One overlooked point: it no longer has the "small and beautiful" fallback

Every analysis assumes Berkshire can "get better slowly." But a USD 1.26 trillion market cap means it must earn over USD 100 billion a year just to lift the stock 8% — more than the annual profit of 490 companies in the S&P 500.

That creates a structural risk: it can only buy mega-assets, and mega-assets are rarely cheap. In the 1980s it could buy a USD 500 million company at a 30% return; today the smallest target it will "look at" is USD 10 billion, and with sovereign funds and PE competing in that pool, prices have long since been bid flat.

This is not management's fault; it is the price of success. But it means: over the next decade, returns will converge toward "the average return of large-cap US blue chips," not toward "Buffett's historical record."

Conclusion

Eight risks: one 1.0 fatal (underwriting discipline), seven skin-deep (valuation and sentiment). The fatal risk is low probability; the skin-deep ones are high probability.

The right response is therefore not "don't buy" but "buy only when cheap": let price absorb the 15% skin-deep risk, and use quarterly metrics to guard against the 1.0 fatal risk.
