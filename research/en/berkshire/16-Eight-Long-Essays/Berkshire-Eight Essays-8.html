Core view

The previous seven pieces were analysis; this one is execution. Analysis can be complex, but execution must be simple—three disciplines, one price table, one watch calendar.

Discipline one: tier by price, not by sentiment

USD 592.85 = 1.66x book = 0 position. USD 500 = 1.4x = 3%. USD 460 = 1.3x = 5%. USD 430 = 1.2x = 8%. Cap at 8%, because it is ballast, not an engine.

The 8%–9% spacing between these three tiers is deliberate: Berkshire's annualized volatility is about 15%, so one tier equals half a standard deviation, meaning a high probability of triggering at least one tier within a year. Set tiers USD 10 apart and you will be buying and selling constantly; set them USD 100 apart and you may never get filled.

Note that book value rises 8%–10% a year, so this table must shift up once a year. USD 500 is 1.4x this year and 1.3x next year.

Principle two: exit on thesis invalidation, not on price stops

A 30% drop in this company has often been the best buying opportunity (2011, March 2020, 2022). Use a price stop-loss and you will sell at the bottom, then watch it climb back.

The real time to exit is when the business deteriorates. Four signals: ① combined ratio above 100% for two consecutive quarters; ② float declining year on year; ③ a large acquisition at more than 2x book value; ④ continued large buybacks above 1.8x book value.

Of these four signals, the first two mean "the foundation has collapsed"; the last two mean "management has changed". If any one appears, the rating drops straight from "Watch" to "Avoid", regardless of price.

Discipline three: put a time limit on waiting

The biggest risk of "waiting for a pullback" is waiting indefinitely — until the company changes and you forget why you were waiting. So set a deadline for the wait: 18 months.

If the share price has not fallen back below USD 500 before May 2028, and none of the three trigger conditions is met (a single acquisition of USD 30 billion, underwriting profit back to USD 2 billion per quarter, PB back to 1.4x), then the conclusion must change to "abandon this stock" and put the money elsewhere.

The reason is simple: if it trades above 1.6x for three years, the market's pricing of "certainty" has permanently shifted higher, which means the assumption of "waiting for 1.4x" is itself wrong. A wrong assumption should be abandoned, not clung to.

Three things we avoid: leverage, options, and chasing safe havens

No leverage. Berkshire already runs 1.7x leverage (liabilities/equity); buying it on margin stacks insurance-liability risk on top of your own. A 20% drawdown is normal for it, and leverage knocks you out during normal times.

No options. Its volatility is low and options are cheaply priced, but direction is hard to call. Buying calls to bet on safe-haven flows persisting is a bet on market sentiment, not on the company. Don't chase the rally on "safe haven." The most common mistake with this stock is being persuaded by its story during a panic, then getting trapped when the panic ends — November 2026 is exactly that kind of moment.

Do none of these three things, and what's left is waiting. Waiting is the hardest action, because it looks like doing nothing.

Conclusion

Three rules: price tiers (0/3/5/8%), thesis stop-loss (four signals), waiting period (18 months). The first two govern risk; the last governs yourself.

The conclusion is unchanged: watch, wait for a pullback. USD 592.85 is not a buy point; USD 500 is. This company is worth waiting for, but not worth chasing.
