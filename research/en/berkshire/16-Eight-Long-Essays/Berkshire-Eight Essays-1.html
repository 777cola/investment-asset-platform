Core view

Most people know Berkshire through three lines: an insurance company, Buffett's company, and a steady 10% a year. None of the three is wrong, but each will lead you to the wrong call in November 2026.

Myth one: it is an insurance company

Open the segment report: insurance underwriting RMB 1.352 billion, insurance investment RMB 2.734 billion, totaling RMB 4.086 billion; BNSF RMB 1.729 billion, BHE RMB 1.612 billion, manufacturing, services and retailing RMB 4.042 billion, totaling RMB 7.383 billion. Two-thirds of profit comes from railroads, power and manufacturing.

Insurance is not a "source of profit" for it, but a "source of funds." USD 178.5 billion of float at a negative cost (combined ratio 91.4%) — that is insurance's real position: the fuel tank of this machine, not the engine.

That distinction determines how you value it. Value it as an insurer (PB 1.2–1.5x, watching the underwriting cycle) and you get "expensive"; value it as a conglomerate holding company (segment cash flow, capital allocation) and you get "fair enough." Both methods hold up, so its share price never reaches consensus.

Myth two: this is Buffett's company

On January 1, 2026, Abel takes over as CEO. On September 18, Buffett hands over the chairmanship to Howard and becomes chairman emeritus. Structurally, this is no longer Buffett's company.

But the market still prices in the "Buffett premium." That premium comes from three things: a sixty-year track record, judgment in a crisis, and the standing that "whoever he calls has to pick up." The first two can be passed on. The third cannot.

So the real question is not "can Abel do the job" but "when will the market stop paying for Buffett." That moment could be a misstep in a crisis, a mediocre acquisition, or simply three years from now, when people notice "nothing is different" and gradually forget. That is the crux of the cognitive reset: you are buying a company that has already handed over the reins, but the price still carries the pre-succession premium.

Myth three: it rises a steady 10% a year

Over the long run, annualized returns from 1965 to today are about 19.9%, the best record in the history of human investing. But over the past decade, annualized returns are roughly 9%–11%, trailing the S&P 500 (12%–13% including dividends).

The reason is not diminished skill; it is size. With a market cap of USD 1.26 trillion, doubling requires earning another Berkshire; and worldwide there are only a few dozen targets that can absorb USD 300 billion. Size is the enemy of compounding, and that holds as true for Buffett as for Abel.

So the expectation of "steady 10% gains" must become "steady 8%–10% gains, with lower volatility than the index." This is not bad news; it is a different asset class — what you want is low volatility, not high returns. Viewed as a bond substitute, it is good; viewed as a growth stock, it is poor.

Conclusion

Strip away three illusions and what remains is a plain judgment: a USD 1.26 trillion machine with insurance float as its fuel tank, railroads, power and manufacturing as its engine, and a new driver at the wheel—compounding 8%–10% annually with low volatility.

It is worth 1.3–1.4 times book. It now trades at 1.66 times. That is all.
