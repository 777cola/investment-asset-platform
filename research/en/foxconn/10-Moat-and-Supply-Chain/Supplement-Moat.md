## Position in the value chain: the buyer of materials is also the one being priced

Upstream sit Nvidia, the three HBM giants, TSMC's CoWoS, and Delta's and Lite-On's power and thermal solutions. Every link in that chain is raising prices in 2026, and all of it is customer-specified—Hon Hai has no pricing power at this layer, only procurement scale, which buys a little priority on lead times, not on price.

Downstream sit the five North American CSPs, Neoclouds, sovereign clouds, and brand vendors such as Dell and HP. Buyer concentration exceeds seller concentration: the five CSPs account for about 70% of full-rack procurement, falling to about 60% in 2026. Upstream prices rise, downstream squeezes, and Hon Hai sits in the middle—its only lever is to retain part of the processing fee through in-house components.

Then there is production location: tariff adjustments since 2025 have led customers to demand "multi-site backup," turning Hon Hai's capacity in Mexico, Vietnam, India and the US into a scarce resource. But location advantage is granted by policy, and policy can take it back—this is the only option-like item in its industry-chain position, and the one least suited to being treated as a permanent moat.

## Moat score: one true river, four shallow fords

Cost advantage: 8 points. This is the only item that stands up to scrutiny: gross margin is the highest of the six and by more than 1 percentage point; AI server share exceeds 40%; April 2026 monthly shipments led the runner-up by about 2,100 racks. The ceiling, though, is that the cost advantage cannot support price increases — it only means surviving better than others at the same price, not that the price itself improves.

Switching costs: 5 points. Rack certification cycles are long and yield ramp-up is hard, so customers do not switch plants lightly; that stickiness is real. But orders are awarded by project and reshuffled with each platform generation (GB300 to VR200 is one such reshuffle), and customers always retain second and third suppliers. Stickiness exists, but it is not lock-in.

Brand: 1 point; network effects: 3 points; licences: 2 points. Contract manufacturers have no consumer brand; the network effects of capacity footprint are weak; there are no licence barriers, only customer AVL lists and country-of-origin requirements. The three together total 6 points, which amounts to nothing.

Of the five, only the cost advantage can plausibly widen, via a rising share of in-house components — extending from connectors, mechanical parts and PCB into power modules, liquid-cooling CDUs and complete switches. Each 1 percentage point increase in the in-house ratio lifts gross margin by roughly 0.05–0.08 percentage points (a rough calculation assuming 20% gross margin on components and their share of BOM). To lift margin by 0.4 percentage points, the in-house ratio must rise by more than 5 percentage points, which takes three to five years. The moat is widening, but more slowly than the cycle is rolling over.

## The most dangerous rival: not Quanta, but upstream

Quanta is close behind with the highest AI server revenue share, but it suffers from the same problem: gross margin of 5.02%, down 2.03pct YoY, diluted harder than Hon Hai. The two are fighting over the same slice of processing fees, and neither can take pricing power from the other, because pricing power is not in their hands.

Two risks are genuinely dangerous. First, upstream price increases: 2026 DRAM contract prices +60%–70%, NAND +100%. These pass straight through the contract manufacturers' income statement — revenue grows, profit does not. Second, customers disintermediating rack integration and buying components directly: once cloud providers standardise rack integration, contract manufacturers fall back from "system integrators" to "assemblers," and even a 6% gross margin will not hold. The former is already happening; the latter is a 2027–2028 technology-roadmap issue.

A third competitor is easily overlooked: customers' second suppliers. Every CSP keeps two to three contract manufacturers in parallel, allocating orders 60/25/15 to preserve bargaining power. Hon Hai always gets the largest share, but never all of it — this structure means it cannot earn excess profit through "sole supply," and explains why it cannot raise prices despite a market share above 40%.
