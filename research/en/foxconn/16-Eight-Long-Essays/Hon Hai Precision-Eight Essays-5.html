⑤ Industry and competition: a prisoners' dilemma among the six players — Hon Hai Precision

No one dares to raise prices, and no one dares to cut output
Research date 2026/10/19  ·  Price NT$240 (-1.84%)  ·  Market cap About TWD 3.37 trillion  ·  PE(2026E) Approximately 12.6x
← Back to the hub

Core view

2026Q2 gross margins across the six Taiwanese ODMs: Hon Hai 6.12%, Wistron 5.66%, Quanta 5.02%, Compal 4.6%, Pegatron 4.43%, Inventec 4.23%. This is not a spread across six companies; it is the pricing outcome of a single industry. All six know that raising prices would lift profit, yet none dares move first — because the customer holds the other five.

Prisoner's dilemma: whoever moves first on price loses the orders

If one of the six raises prices by 3%, the customer's choice is simple: move the order to the other five. Switching costs three months of qualification and ramp-up, but a 3% price gap is hundreds of millions of dollars of savings a year. So the rational choice is for none to raise prices, and for all to grind it out in a 4%–6% gross margin.

There is only one solution to this game: differentiate the product until customers cannot switch. TSMC did it (exclusive process nodes); Hon Hai did not (seven or eight suppliers can build a full rack). So the six players' gross margin will stay in the 4%–6% range for a long time, and none of them can lift the industry to double digits.

Four toll booths: profit distribution along the value chain

NVIDIA gross margin 70%+, the three HBM players 40%–50%, power and thermal 25%–30%, advanced packaging 30%–50%, foundry and assembly 4%–6%. Along this chain, gross margin rises with proximity to "only one company can do it."

The 2026 price increases widened the gap further: DRAM contract prices +60%–70%, NAND +100%, with these costs passing straight through to the foundries. When upstream prices rise, foundry revenue grows and gross margin shrinks; when upstream prices fall, revenue shrinks and gross margin grows—the foundry is always adjusting passively, never setting prices on its own.

Marginal shifts in the competitive landscape

Three changes stand out. First, Wistron's gross margin rose 1.22pct YoY to 5.66%, the only one of the six to post a recovery in both margins, pointing to room for repair once the new-product learning curve is complete. Second, Quanta's gross margin fell 2.03pct YoY to 5.02%, the most heavily diluted given its highest AI mix. Third, Wiwynn reached 9.3% via white-box direct supply, proving that skipping the brand and selling directly to CSPs is a route to higher gross margin.

Together the three show: gross-margin differences stem mainly from model — customer structure and in-house production ratio — not from management efficiency. They also mean Hon Hai's 6.12% risks being caught: Wistron is already closing in.

Cycle position: year four, the most aggressive year

AI capex began in 2023, making 2026 its fourth year. The four major North American cloud providers have sharply raised capex, and Hon Hai's Q3 revenue rose 47.1%, with September surpassing the trillion mark. TrendForce expects NVL72 output value to grow another 214% in 2027, driven by a doubling of ASP and upstream price increases. Demand visibility extends to 2027; the issue is the 2027 YoY base.

A single quarter of 3.03 trillion in 2026Q3 means 2027Q3 must grow 30% to reach 3.94 trillion. If cloud capex growth falls from 40% to 10%, Hon Hai's revenue growth will drop to single digits—and with a 6% gross margin there is no buffer; EPS will fall far more than revenue.

Competitive conclusion: winner takes all, yet the winner gets no prize

Foxconn wins this cycle: over 40% market share, the most complete capacity and the most stable yields. But winning here means winning the most orders, not earning the highest margin. Industry structure dictates it: the winner's prize is more revenue, not more profit.

For investors the implication is direct: don't pay a premium for the "winner" at the industry peak; pay a discount for the "winner that won't die" when sentiment on the industry is pessimistic. The former is RMB 239; the latter is RMB 160.

If cloud providers begin building their own production lines

One development in 2026 is worth flagging: some cloud providers have started building full-rack assembly lines inside their data centre campuses, bringing "last-mile" integration back in-house. The motive is not cost savings but control over lead times and yield — a rack arriving a quarter late delays the entire campus's ramp.

For contract manufacturers the implications cut two ways. Near term, cloud providers' in-house lines have limited capacity and still need to buy externally; long term, once assembly becomes "a standard process inside the campus," the contract manufacturer's value reduces to parts manufacturing.Hon Hai's response is to go deeper on self-made components — parts can be sold to others, assembly cannot. If this transformation succeeds, its identity shifts from "assembler" to "component maker," and gross margin may actually rise.

Conclusion

Industry view: Liu Ge's prisoner's dilemma has no near-term fix; gross margin stays in the 4%–6% range over the long run; all four toll booths along the value chain sit above contract manufacturing; the cycle is in its fourth year, and the 2027 base will look ugly. Hon Hai wins this cycle, but what it wins is orders, not margin. Which is why the "AI server leader" label is of limited help to investors: leadership guarantees it builds the most racks, not that it keeps an extra cent from each one.
