③ Moat anatomy: a river wide but not deep — Hon Hai Precision

Blocks new entrants, not customers
Research date 2026/10/19  ·  Price NT$240 (-1.84%)  ·  Market cap About TWD 3.37 trillion  ·  PE(2026E) Approximately 12.6x
← Back to the hub

Core view

There is only one test of a moat: can it raise prices. Measured against that test, Hon Hai's answer is a 6.12% gross margin, down 0.21pct year on year — it cannot raise prices. But it does have a river: global capacity plus vertical integration keep new players out. Wide but not deep is the most accurate description of that river.

First moat: global capacity, three lines in three months

Hon Hai is the only manufacturer that can deliver AI racks simultaneously in Mexico, Vietnam, India and the US. In April 2026 alone, it shipped roughly 2,100 racks more than Quanta and roughly 1,300–1,400 more than Wistron. This capability comes from thirty years of accumulation and cannot be bought with money—you can buy factories, but you cannot buy yield rates and supply-chain relationships.

But its value has limits: capacity can only be monetized when customers require multi-location backups. If tariffs ease and customers do not require backups, this advantage is discounted. So it is a policy option, not a permanent moat.

Second moat: vertical integration, capturing every segment of the midstream

Foxconn makes connectors, mechanical parts, PCBs, power supplies, thermal solutions and racks in-house. In-house components carry a 15%–25% gross margin, and once assembled into a full rack they lift overall gross margin by more than 1 percentage point — that is the real source of its 1.1pct premium over Quanta and 1.5pct premium over Compal. It is not that Foxconn negotiates better; it converts others' profit into its own.

But that 1 percentage point is an efficiency gap, not a moat. Efficiency gaps get closed: Quanta is also building in-house components, and Wistron's gross margin has already reached 5.66%, up 1.22pct year on year. Once peers learn vertical integration, that 1 percentage point disappears.

Paragraph 3: Switching costs, a five-point stickiness

Cabinet certification cycles are long and yield ramp-up is difficult, so customers do not switch suppliers lightly—this is real stickiness. But orders are awarded project by project, and a platform generational change reshuffles everything—when GB300 gives way to VR200, the supplier list must go through certification all over again. Stickiness lasts for the life of one platform generation, not for a decade.

More critically, customers always cultivate a second and third supplier, allocating orders 60/25/15. Foxconn takes the largest share, but never all of it. This structure keeps it alive, and also ensures it cannot earn excess profit.

Can the river widen: a slow path

The only direction in which the moat can widen is a continued rise in the in-house production ratio: extending from connectors, mechanical parts and PCBs into power modules, liquid-cooling CDUs and complete switches. Each 1 percentage point increase in the in-house ratio lifts gross margin by roughly 0.05–0.08pct (a rough calculation assuming 20% gross margin on components and their share of BOM). Moving it up 0.4pct takes three to five years.

On this slow path, time is the opponent: a cyclical downturn may take only two quarters. The moat is widening, but more slowly than the cycle—the most awkward combination in investing.

The competitor is not a peer, but the customer's procurement system

The moat's standard opponent is a peer, but Hon Hai's real constraint comes from its customers' procurement systems: every CSP simultaneously runs two to three suppliers, allocates orders on a 60/25/15 split, and renegotiates prices annually. The purpose of this system is precisely "to prevent any single supplier from gaining pricing power."

Under this system, the largest share Hon Hai can win is 60%, and the best gross margin it can achieve is "1 percentage point above the runner-up." It cannot break through this ceiling by performing better, because the ceiling is set by the system. So to assess Hon Hai's moat, asking "how much stronger is it than Quanta" is useless; ask "how much stronger do its customers allow it to be."

Conclusion

Moat conclusion: cost advantage 8, switching costs 5, network 3, licence 2, brand 1. That is enough for Hon Hai to survive a price war, not enough to earn excess returns. Paying a premium for this moat buys "won't die", not "will improve". One line to remember: this moat keeps out those who want in, but cannot stop those already inside from walking out — the customer's second-supplier system is the door that stays open.
