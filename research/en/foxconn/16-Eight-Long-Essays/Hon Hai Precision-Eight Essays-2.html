② Business model: trading balance sheet for revenue — Hon Hai Precision

The math of processing fees, and why they stay low

Core view

Hon Hai's business in one sentence: it uses its own balance sheet to absorb component procurement, capacity build-out and delivery risk on behalf of customers, in exchange for a fixed processing fee. Every phrase in that sentence shapes the margin. "On behalf of customers" means a heavy asset base; "fixed processing fee" means no pricing power; "delivery risk" means plants in four locations.

The math behind the processing fee: how 2.4% is derived

2026Q2: revenue RMB 2,525.9 billion → gross profit RMB 154.5 billion (6.12%) → operating expenses RMB 59.7 billion → operating income RMB 94.8 billion (3.75%) → taxes and minority interests RMB 34.8 billion → net income RMB 59.97 billion (2.37%). A four-stage funnel, each stage shaving off a slice, leaving 2.4% at the end.

The first stage cuts deepest: purchased materials account for 94% of revenue, a ratio set by product mix — the more full-rack assemblies, the higher the materials share and the lower the gross margin. The second stage is expenses, already compressed to 2.36% with no room left. The third is taxes and minority interests: Foxconn Industrial Internet is not wholly owned, so profit must be shared out. Of the four stages, only the first can improve, and that is up to the customer.

Turnover: how it actually makes money

How does a company survive on a 2.37% net margin? Turnover. Assets turn 1.4 times a year, the equity multiplier is 2.4x, and only then does ROE reach 8%–12%. In other words, it earns not "profit per transaction" but "how many rounds it completes in a year."

The fatal flaw of a turnover business: the moment turnover slows, ROE collapses. When revenue growth falls to single digits while receivables and inventory stay on the books, asset turnover declines and ROE can drop from 12% to 8% in just two quarters. This is why such companies look cheapest at the top of the cycle and most expensive at the bottom—the denominator of P/E moves in reverse.

Capital consumption: growth costs you twice

The first payment is in working capital: revenue rose 39%, receivables and inventory rose with it, and cash went out first. The second is capex: capacity expansion across four locations, with plant and equipment built first and orders arriving later. After both, free cash flow for the first half was negative.

TSMC faces the same two payments, but its gross margin is 50%+, so plenty is left after paying. Hon Hai's gross margin is 6%, and nothing is left. Both are capital-intensive, but only one has a buffer—the difference is not how much capex is spent, but how thick the gross margin is.

Ceiling: why it cannot grow into TSMC

A common question: Hon Hai has been in contract manufacturing for decades, so why can't it lift gross margin the way TSMC did? The answer lies in customer structure. TSMC's customers are fabless design houses with nowhere else to go; Hon Hai's are brands and cloud providers who can always find a second or third supplier. Pricing power does not depend on how well you execute; it depends on whether the customer has alternatives.

Hon Hai's ceiling is therefore structural, not technological. It leaves only two levers: raise the share of in-house components (capturing several midstream segments) and spread capacity to wherever customers must go (tariffs, production locations). It has pulled both, and gross margin went from 5.9% to 6.12% — 0.2 percentage points in six years. That is the speed of the ceiling.

Counterexample: why Wiwynn can achieve 9.3%

Within the same business, Wiwynn's 2026Q2 gross margin was 9.3%, the only double-digit figure among the six peers. The gap reflects model, not effort: Wiwynn runs white-label direct supply, selling straight to CSPs and stripping out the two layers of markup taken by brand and channel, and it quotes on a "system plus services" basis rather than collecting a processing fee per rack.It sells "contract manufacturing" as a "solution", so the price is naturally different.

Why doesn't Foxconn do the same? Because its big customers want exactly that "own brand" label. Once Foxconn goes white-label, it is competing with its customers for the end market, and the orders vanish immediately.This is the paradox of contract manufacturing: the closer you are to the customer, the less you can become the customer. What Wiwynn can do, Foxconn cannot — a difference in model, not in capability.

Keep this in mind and everything that follows — reading financials, sizing up the industry, judging management — becomes much smoother: what you are studying is not a company's ceiling on capability, but the ceiling on profit it is permitted.

Conclusion

Business model conclusion: This is a business that trades assets for revenue, turns inventory into ROE, and trades scale for orders. Its ceiling is set by customers' freedom to choose, not by its own effort. Grasp this and you will not harbour unrealistic hopes for "gross margin improvement." So do not ask of Hon Hai "can it do better"; ask "how much will its customers allow it to earn." The former is a management question, the latter a structural one, and only the latter determines valuation.
