⑥ Management: six years of Liu Young-way, new track but same margin — Hon Hai Precision

Top-tier execution, middling capital allocation

Core view

When Liu Young-way took over in July 2019, Hon Hai's revenue was over 5 trillion, seven tenths of it from Apple, and gross margin was just above 5%. Six years on, cloud and networking account for more than 40% of revenue, AI server share exceeds 40%, and a single month — September — broke the trillion mark. The mix shifted; the margin did not: 6.12%, up just 0.2pp in six years. That is no mark against him — it is the structure of the business — but investors must keep the two apart.

Three things done right

First, he built cloud networking into the largest division, completing capacity and customer positioning ahead of the AI demand surge. Second, he used Foxconn Industrial Internet to house the AI business in a standalone platform, listed on the A-share market in 2018 and now the valuation anchor for this rally. Third, he spread capacity across Mexico, Vietnam, India and the US, winning "multi-site backup" orders amid tariff and geopolitical pressure.

The common thread: he correctly judged where growth would come from, and acted two years early. Contract manufacturers rarely get this right—most are pulled along by customers. This deserves high marks.

One thing not achieved: profit margin

On taking office he pledged to lift gross margin. Six years on it stands at 6.12%, 0.2pct above the 5.9% of 2019 but still short of the 2022 peak. Not for want of effort — the structure will not allow it: customers pay processing fees per cabinet, upstream sells materials at market prices, and the contract manufacturer in between is left with no room to raise prices.

That points to one conclusion: pinning hopes for gross margin on a change of management is wrong. Whoever takes over, the outcome will be the same unless the customers or the model change.

Capital allocation: two big checks written badly

Sharp: in 2016 it paid JPY 388.8 billion for 66%, gaining panels, brand and appliance channels; losses ran for years until plant closures and layoffs turned it positive after 2020. Strategically necessary, financially mediocre. Lordstown: in 2022 it paid USD 230 million for GM's old plant, betting on EV contract manufacturing; in 2023 customer cooperation contracted and capacity sat idle. Outright misjudgment.

Together the two show a company that excels at building what others have designed, not at defining a product from scratch. So after 2024 it has bet all its capital back on AI racks, and the direction is right — a return to what it does best.

Governance: the nine-member committee marks substantive progress

Since 2019, major capex decisions have no longer rested with a single individual but with a nine-member operating committee. The point is to cut the probability of "one-man error": the decisions that cost Foxconn dearly in its history (Wisconsin, Lordstown) all bore a strong imprint of individual will.

Terry Gou remains the largest individual shareholder with roughly a 10% stake; he carries weight on major investments but is not involved in day-to-day operations. No asset stripping, no related-party transaction scandals, no financial restatements — governance is one less thing to worry about.

Succession: the mechanism exists, the candidate does not

Young Liu was born in 1956 and turns 70 in 2026; the company has no public succession timetable. The mechanism is already in place — a committee plus a business-group general manager responsibility system — which in theory does not depend on any one person. But the AI rack business is effectively run by Cheng Hung-meng of Foxconn Industrial Internet, and if the next chairman lacks experience in this business, strategic continuity will suffer.

Implication for investors: continuity risk is lower than at a typical family business, but so is strategic flexibility — a committee system will not make big mistakes, nor will it make particularly bold correct decisions. Put it in the "watch" bucket; governance is a plus, not a reason to buy.

Terry Gou's legacy: a one-man company

Under Terry Gou, Hon Hai was "a one-man company": fast decisions, strong execution, a willingness to place heavy bets, and some big mistakes. The USD 10 billion Wisconsin investment pledge and the Lordstown EV bet both bore the stamp of one man's will—announce first, do the math later.

The biggest change under Young Liu is not the business but the decision-making mechanism: a nine-member committee has turned "one man decides" into "collective decision-making." The cost is a little less speed; the benefit is a much lower error rate. For a company with revenue of TWD 8 trillion, that is a worthwhile trade. For investors, though, it also means the company will no longer make the "bet the company" calls that turn out right—the kind that usually come from one person.

Conclusion

Management verdict: Execution 8, capital allocation 6, governance 7, succession 6. Liu Young-way has steered the company into the mainstream of AI infrastructure — a real achievement. But he cannot change the 6% gross margin fate, nor should he be asked to. The rating is not raised on management. Judging management means separating two questions: can he take the company to the right place (yes), and can he change the margin structure of this business (no, and that should not fall to him).
