## Liu Young-way's six years: a new track, the same margin

When Liu Young-way took over in July 2019, Foxconn's problems were plain: revenue of over TWD 5 trillion, 70% dependence on Apple and a gross margin barely above 5%. He got three things right—building cloud networking into the largest division, ring-fencing the AI business in a separate platform via FII, and spreading capacity across Mexico and Vietnam to hedge tariffs. By 2026Q2 cloud networking accounts for more than 40% of revenue and AI server market share exceeds 40%—that is not luck.

What he did not achieve also bears stating plainly: gross margin has gone from 5.9% in 2019 to 6.12% now, a move of just 0.2pp in six years. Changing lanes answered "where growth comes from," not "where profit comes from." That is a function of business structure rather than a mark against him, but investors should not mistake a successful transformation for improved profitability.

## Capital allocation: two big cheques poorly spent

The Sharp deal is the easiest to size: JPY 388.8 billion for 66%, buying panels, brand and appliance channels. Impairments and losses ran every year from 2016 to 2019; only plant closures, layoffs and asset sales turned it positive after 2020. A mediocre financial return, but strategically necessary—without Sharp, Hon Hai could not have entered branding and panels.

Lordstown was a pure misjudgment: USD 230 million for GM's old plant, a bet on EV contract manufacturing; customer cooperation contracted in 2023 and capacity sat idle. The EV line, from MIH to Lordstown, produced no scale revenue in six years. Together the two deals show one thing: this company excels at building what others have designed, and fails at defining a product from scratch. Its decision to push all capital back into AI server racks after 2024 is therefore right.

## Shareholder returns: payout ratio 55%, dividend yield below 3%

Hon Hai's dividend policy is stable rather than generous: the payout ratio has run at roughly 55% in recent years, and at 239.5 the yield is below 3% on our rough calculation. Capacity, meanwhile, has been expanded for four consecutive years, with capex taking priority over dividends and buybacks. For a company with a net margin of 2.37%, the choice is understandable—without adding capacity it loses orders—but for shareholders it means holding-period returns come almost entirely from the share price.

Against peers, Quanta and Wistron also pay out in the 60%–70% range, so Hon Hai is not the worst; but its ROE is only 9%–12%, and the return on reinvested retained earnings sits below the opportunity range set by its cost of capital. That is the hidden cost of the "expansion first" strategy.

## Integrity and governance: a record to discount

In 2017 it announced a USD 10 billion investment in Wisconsin that would create 13,000 jobs; actual investment and hiring fell far short, and the state subsidies were renegotiated. This is not fraud; it is the classic pattern of promising the best case externally while running the business on cash flow internally. Apply the same discount rate when reading its capacity plans and revenue targets.

On governance, the nine-member operating committee is real progress: major capex is no longer decided by one person, and since 2019 there has been no repeat of "the chairman changes the company's direction with one word." Terry Gou remains the largest individual shareholder at roughly 10%, influential on major investments but not involved in day-to-day operations. No asset stripping, no related-party scandals, no financial restatements — this item is safe.

## Succession: the mechanism exists, the candidate does not

Liu Young-way was born in 1956 and turns 70 in 2026. No succession timetable has been published, but the mechanism is in place: a nine-member operating committee plus a business-group general manager responsibility system that in theory does not depend on any one person. The real succession test is not who moves up, but who holds the AI rack business — currently run in practice by Cheng Hung-meng of Foxconn Industrial Internet.

Investor takeaway: continuity risk is lower than at a typical family business, but strategic flexibility is also low — a committee system will not make big mistakes, and will not make particularly risky correct decisions either. In the "watch" bucket, this governance structure is a plus, not a reason to buy.
