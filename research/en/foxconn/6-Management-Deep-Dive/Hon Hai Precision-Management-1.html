Management — Hon Hai Precision

Core call: this management team has moved the company onto a different track, and its spending has not been especially disciplined

Liu Young-way took over in 2019 and in six years pulled off one big thing: rebuilding Hon Hai from an "Apple assembly plant" into an "AI infrastructure supplier," with cloud and networking products accounting for over 40% of revenue in Q2 2026 and AI servers holding more than 40% market share.On execution, this is top-tier for contract manufacturing. But capital allocation deserves only 6 points: neither the Sharp nor the Lordstown acquisition and investment made money, the payout ratio is about 55% with a yield below 3%, and simultaneous capacity expansion in four locations front-loads risk onto shareholders.

Governance has no fatal flaws: a nine-person management committee replaces one-man decision-making, and Terry Gou has stepped back from day-to-day operations but remains the largest shareholder. What really matters is the track record on past promises—the Wisconsin investment and job commitments shrank sharply, a sign that the company's external numbers should be discounted.

Liu Young-way's six years: he changed the track, not the margin

When he took over in July 2019, Foxconn's problems were clear: revenue of over TWD 5 trillion, 70% dependent on Apple, and a gross margin just above 5%. Liu Young-way did three things right—making cloud networking products the largest division, housing the AI business in a separate platform via FII, and spreading capacity to Mexico and Vietnam to hedge tariffs. In 2026Q2, cloud networking accounts for more than 40% of revenue and AI server market share exceeds 40%—that is not luck.

But what he did not achieve must also be stated plainly: gross margin went from 5.9% in 2019 to 6.12% now, moving just 0.2 percentage points in six years. Changing lanes solved "where growth comes from," not "where profit comes from." This is determined by the business structure and is not a mark against him, but investors should not mistake "successful transformation" for "improved profitability."

Capital allocation: two big cheques poorly spent

The Sharp deal is easiest to size up: JPY 388.8 billion for 66%, in exchange for panels, brand and appliance channels. Impairments and losses ran every year from 2016 to 2019; only plant closures, layoffs and asset sales turned it positive after 2020. Mediocre financial return, strategically necessary—without Sharp, Hon Hai could not have entered branding and panels.

Lordstown was a pure misjudgment: USD 230 million for GM's old plant, a bet on EV contract manufacturing; customer cooperation contracted in 2023 and capacity sat idle. The EV line, from MIH to Lordstown, produced no scale revenue in six years. Together the two deals show one thing: this company excels at building what others have designed, and fails at defining a product from scratch. So its decision to push all capital back into AI server racks after 2024 is right.

Shareholder returns: payout ratio 55%, dividend yield below 3%

Hon Hai's dividend policy is stable but not generous: the payout ratio in recent years is about 55%, and at 239.5 the yield is under 3% (rough calculation). At the same time, it has expanded capacity for four consecutive years, with capital expenditure taking priority over dividends and buybacks. For a company with a net margin of 2.37%, this choice is understandable—without adding capacity, it loses orders; but for shareholders, it means holding-period returns come almost entirely from the share price.

Compared with peers: Quanta and Wistron also have payout ratios in the 60%–70% range, so Hon Hai is not the worst; but its ROE is only 9%–12%, and the return on reinvested retained earnings is below the opportunity range of its cost of capital. This is the hidden cost of the "expansion first" strategy.

Integrity and governance: a record to discount

In 2017 it announced a USD 10 billion investment in Wisconsin creating 13,000 jobs; actual investment and hiring shrank sharply, and the state subsidies were renegotiated. This is not fraud; it is the classic pattern of promising the best case externally while running the business on cash flow internally. When reading its capacity plans and revenue targets, adjust for that same discount rate.

On governance, the nine-member operating committee is real progress: major capex is no longer decided by one person, and since 2019 there has been no repeat of "the chairman changes the company's direction with one word." Terry Gou remains the largest individual shareholder at roughly 10%, influential on major investments but not involved in day-to-day operations. No asset stripping, no related-party scandals, no financial restatements — this item is safe.

Succession: the mechanism exists, the candidate does not

Liu Young-way was born in 1956 and turns 70 in 2026. The company has not published a succession timetable, but the mechanism is in place: a nine-member operating committee plus a business-group general manager responsibility system, in theory not dependent on any one person. The real succession test is not who moves up, but who holds the AI rack business — currently the actual operator is Foxconn Industrial Internet's Cheng Hung-meng.

Implication for investors: the company's continuity risk is lower than at a typical family business, but its strategic flexibility is also low — a committee system means it will not make big mistakes, and will not make particularly risky correct decisions either. Placed in the "watch" bucket, this governance structure is a plus, not a reason to buy.

Conclusion

Management scores 7/10: top-tier execution, middling capital allocation, tight shareholder returns. Liu Young-way steered the company into the main current of AI infrastructure, but did not change the fate of a 6% gross margin; two failed investments show it excels at manufacturing, not at defining products. There are no hard governance flaws, and the nine-member committee is real progress. The rating is not upgraded on management — good management paired with a bad business structure still means "Watch".
