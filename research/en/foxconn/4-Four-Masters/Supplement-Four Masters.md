## Duan Yongping: Is this a good business?

Duan Yongping sets a high bar: a good business is one that "makes money without trying too hard." Hon Hai is the antithesis—it must keep building plants, stocking materials and hiring people for revenue to rise 47%, and still ends up with a 2.37% net margin, EPS climbing from RMB 12.09 to RMB 19.06 on scale alone. This is a classic asset-heavy, low-margin, highly cyclical business: the money it earns comes from management efficiency, not from the business model.

He would concede that this is one of the best management teams in the industry: global capacity layout, yield control, customer relationships—thirty years of accumulation that no one can replicate. But "the best contract manufacturer" and "a good business" are two different evaluation systems. He bought Apple because Apple can price, not because Foxconn can assemble.

He would press one more question: will this business still exist in ten years? Yes. Contract manufacturing will not disappear, and AI racks will not go unwanted tomorrow. But who it will manufacture for in ten years, and how many points it earns per rack, cannot be calculated today. A business whose terminal margin he cannot understand, Duan Yongping files under "can look, do not take a heavy position." If he had to buy, his condition would be a price low enough that the business's uncertainty need not be calculated—by his habit, that is below 8x P/E, around RMB 160.

## Buffett: Can a moat raise prices?

Buffett's first question would be whether the moat supports pricing power. Hon Hai's answer is no: revenue rose 41% while gross margin fell 0.21 percentage points, so the company cannot even pass on cost increases and must squeeze out profit through scale and expense leverage — operating income +68%, faster than revenue's +41%, reflects efficiency, not pricing.The moat rests on cost advantage: durable but not wide.

On margin of safety, 12.6x 2026E P/E, 10.6x 2027E P/E and 1.5x PB are not expensive in absolute terms. But contract manufacturing profits are peak-cycle profits: once cloud capex growth slows from 40% to 10% in 2027, EPS will fall far more than revenue, because a 6% gross margin offers no buffer.Applying 12x to peak EPS is not cheap — it treats the cycle as the norm.

He would also watch the accounting consequences of capex: the plants and equipment built across four sites in 2026 will be depreciated over 2027–2029. If orders are absent then, depreciation will press directly on the 6% gross margin — this year's capex is the fixed cost of three years from now. This is the most typical "bomb buried in the boom" of contract manufacturing, and Buffett has seen it many times.

## Munger: invert the question — what would make it halve?

Munger would invert the problem. Three triggers: ① North American cloud providers cut their 2027 capex guidance and AI rack orders go from "multiple growth" to "flat"; ② once HBM and advanced packaging supply bottlenecks ease, customers break whole racks back into components and buy them directly, and Hon Hai's integration premium disappears; ③ fixed costs from US capacity expansion (Texas, Wisconsin) arrive before orders do, pushing utilization below 70%.

If any one of these occurs, a 6.12% gross margin cannot support a 3.75% operating margin; EPS falls from 19 yuan to below 10 yuan, and a 12x PE instantly becomes 24x. The stock has fallen from 309 to 239, a 22.5% drawdown, and the market is already pricing this in advance—but only a third of it.

Munger would add one more observation on incentives: the KPI for contract manufacturers is yield, delivery time and capacity utilization, not return on capital. Management bonuses are tied to "how many racks we win," not "how much we earn per rack." Under such incentives, scale will always take priority over profit; the 6% gross margin is an institutional outcome, not an oversight. To change it, the KPI must change first—and the KPI is set by the customer.

## Li Lu: Positioning in a Civilizational Trend

Li Lu would take a longer view: AI infrastructure is the most certain capex cycle of this generation, Foxconn holds over 40% global share of AI servers, and it is the only manufacturer that can deliver simultaneously from Mexico, Vietnam, India and the US — an irreplaceable position. That much he would acknowledge.

But he would press on who captures the excess profit at the endgame. Pricing power clearly sits with Nvidia (chips), with the three memory giants (2026 DRAM contract prices up 60%–70%, NAND up over 100%), and with power and data centers (Nvidia providing residual-value guarantees for OpenAI's campuses, Google committing USD 44 billion to Anthropic's leases). The contract manufacturer takes on locked-in demand, not elevated bargaining power. Right trend, lowest position in the chain — the type of combination he is least willing to pay a premium for.

He would add one point from the China perspective: Foxconn is one of very few manufacturing platforms with substantial capacity on both the US and China sides, and this "amphibious" identity is worth something as geopolitics heats up and trades at a discount when tensions ease. It is not "one of us" to either side, so it cannot fully capture the policy dividends from both — that is the second discount its position confers.

## Where we differ: timing the entry, not the thesis

All four converge on the same conclusion and differ only on timing and price. Duan Yongping wants a "price that requires no calculation," roughly RMB 160; Buffett wants "normalized EPS times no more than 10x," implying RMB 150–180; Munger wants "after the bad news is out," which turns on sentiment rather than price; Li Lu's view is simply that a position of this kind does not belong in the portfolio unless the weight is very small.

All four price anchors sit below RMB 180, against a current price of RMB 239.5. That gap is itself the conclusion: not "wait and see," but "no one is willing to act at this price." The working paper's "watch, wait for the gross margin inflection" stance is slightly looser than what the four investors require, and points to another path: if gross margin demonstrably moves up, the denominator grows and RMB 239 can become cheap. Evidence for that path must wait for the 2026Q3 and Q4 results.

## Odds assessment: probability-weighted upside of only +3%

Weighting the three scenarios 40%/45%/15%: 160×0.4 + 280×0.45 + 380×0.15 = RMB 247, only +3.1% versus RMB 239.5. That is not "another 3% upside"; it is 33% downside risk in exchange for a 3% expected return. Not worth it.

Flipping the risk-reward requires one of two things: either the price falls below RMB 180 — the same RMB 247 expected value then implies +37%, while bear-case downside shrinks to just -11% — or gross margin shows an empirical inflection (two consecutive quarters above 6.5%, base-case EPS revised up to RMB 26, and RMB 280 becomes RMB 320). The first comes from the market, the second from the financials; neither is in our hands, so we can only wait.

Some will argue 12.6x already prices in the bear case. It does not. The bear case is EPS back to RMB 15 at 10x, or RMB 150; the current price of RMB 239.5 corresponds to "EPS RMB 22.5 at 10.6x," a base-case-to-optimistic combination. In other words, at the current price you are buying the base case but bearing the bear case. That is the entire meaning of an unfavorable risk-reward.
