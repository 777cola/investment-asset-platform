Financial analysis (Buffett's perspective) — Hon Hai Precision

Read all three statements together: the profit is real, the cash is not

Core view

The income statement looks good, the cash flow statement looks bad, and the balance sheet is ballooning. In 2026Q2 revenue rose 41%, operating profit 68% and net profit 35% — all three are up; but working capital tied up in the business rose along with revenue, and free cash flow for the first half was negative. This is the classic case of "growth eating cash".

Income statement: expense leverage is real

Revenue 2 trillion 5,259 billion (+41%), gross profit 154.5 billion (6.12%), operating expenses 59.7 billion, operating income 94.8 billion (3.75%, +0.6pct YoY), net income 59.97 billion (2.37%). Falling gross margin with rising operating margin admits only one explanation: capacity utilization is maxed out, and depreciation and administrative expenses are being diluted. This is genuine operating improvement, not accounting sleight of hand.

But expense leverage has a ceiling. Operating expenses of 59.7 billion are already just 2.36% of revenue; squeezing out another 0.3 percentage points yields only 7.6 billion more in pre-tax profit—equal to 12% of net income. In other words, the room left on the expense side cannot support a re-rating.

Cash flow: the quality of growth shows here

AI server racks carry high unit prices and long material lead times. Every extra dollar of revenue requires upfront spending on components, receivables and inventory. In the first half of 2026, revenue rose 39% year on year, working capital tied up expanded in tandem, and free cash flow was negative. For a company with a 2.37% net margin, this is no small problem—it means growth must be paid for by borrowing or selling assets.

Buffett would ask: if revenue stopped growing, would cash flow turn positive immediately? The answer is yes, because working capital would stop expanding. But that shows the current positive operating cash flow is masked by growth. "Growth means bleeding cash" is the norm for this kind of business, not an accident of this quarter.

Balance sheet: leverage is low, but assets are heavy

Net debt ratio is about 15% (rough calculation), interest coverage about 10x — the debt structure is not dangerous, and this is one of the two hard metrics out of seven that the company passes. But the asset side is rapidly getting heavier: plants and equipment for capacity expansion in four locations, and materials stocked for AI racks, are all low-turnover assets. ROE of only 9%–12% is precisely the result of three things stacking up: heavy assets, slow turnover, thin margins.

DuPont breakdown: net margin 2.37% × asset turnover of about 1.4x × equity multiplier of about 2.4x ≈ 8%. To raise ROE, the only lever is net margin, and net margin is decided by customers. This is the core reason the financial seat votes "watch."

One more item to track in advance: depreciation. Equipment from the four-site expansion will gradually be transferred to fixed assets in 2026; amortized over 5–8 years, the additional depreciation each year from 2027 onward will press directly on gross margin. With gross margin flat and depreciation rising, the 3.75% operating margin is the high point for these two years. Early signs of this can be seen in the fixed-asset and depreciation detail of the interim report — no need to wait for the income statement to deteriorate.

Conclusion

CFO seat conclusion: The income statement is credible, the expense leverage is real but limited, cash flow is being consumed by growth, and the balance sheet is getting heavier. 12.6x 2026E PE corresponds to a business with ROE below 12% and negative free cash flow—by Buffett's standards, that combination is "not cheap enough." Rating: "Watch."
