Core view

Munger never asks "how much can it rise" — he asks "how does it die." The answer sits in two lines of this earnings report: gross margin of 6.12%, down two quarters running, against revenue +41%. The faster revenue grows, the lower the gross margin — scale and profit move in opposite directions in this business. In business history, that is a dangerous signal.

Failure mode one: mistaking scale for a moat

Above 40% market share, first in monthly shipments, September revenue through the trillion mark — all of that is scale. But scale is a moat only when unit economics are positive and pricing power exists; otherwise it is simply greater exposure. Hon Hai's scale buys order priority, not pricing power: revenue rose 41% while gross margin still fell 0.21pct.

Munger would say: "The other side of economies of scale is diseconomies of scale — you have to keep running just to stay in place." Capacity expansion across four locations, working capital fronted for materials, the climb up the yield curve — each amplifies with scale.

Failure mode two: incentives tie people to revenue

For contract manufacturers the key metrics are yield, delivery time and capacity utilisation, not return on capital. Management bonuses are tied to "how many cabinets we take in," not "how much profit per cabinet." Under such incentives scale inevitably takes priority over profit — a 6% gross margin is not an oversight but a product of the system.

Changing this requires the customer-set terms to change first: customers pay a processing fee per cabinet and switch suppliers project by project, leaving contract manufacturers to choose the former between "taking on more" and "raising prices." Munger had a line for this structure: "Show me the incentive and I will show you the outcome."

Failure mode three: mistaking the cycle peak for the norm

2026 marks the fourth year of AI capex and the most aggressive yet. The market prices the business at 12.6x P/E, embedding the assumption that growth continues into 2027. But the 2027 YoY base is quarterly revenue of 3 trillion, and any slowdown will make EPS fall faster than revenue.

Munger's inversion checklist: cloud providers cut capex → orders go from multiple growth to flat → a 6% gross margin cannot support a 3.75% operating margin → EPS falls from 19 to 10 → 12x P/E becomes 24x. This chain needs no black swan, only a normal cyclical pullback.

Conclusion

Munger's lens: this is not a company that "dies" — it is one that "halves" when the cycle turns down. All three failure modes — scale, incentives, cycle — point to the same conclusion: its profits have no buffer. Watch, and wait for bad news.
