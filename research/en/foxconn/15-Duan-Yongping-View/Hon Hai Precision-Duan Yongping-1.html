Core view: understandable, but not a business we want to own

Frame Hon Hai the way Duan Yongping would. His framework comes down to three questions: is this a good business, will it still be here in ten years, and at what price do you buy. Hon Hai's answers: not a great business, certainly still here in ten years, but buy it when nobody wants it. Q3 2026 revenue of RMB 303 billion, up 47% YoY, and September breaking the trillion mark — all real; so is a 2.37% net margin. He does not dispute that this is a good company; he simply does not equate "good company" with "good stock." For him, business quality ranks first, price second, management third — Hon Hai is not weak on the last two, but it fails the first.

Q: Is this a good business?

Not good. A good business is one where "you make money without trying too hard"; Hon Hai has to try very hard to earn just a little. Revenue of NTD 2 trillion 525.9 billion, and only 59.97 billion is left at the end — 2.37%. Think about it: the customer gives you the most expensive components, has you assemble them, and pays you a small processing fee — that money is earned solidly, but the margin is thin.

I don't deny it does well. Among the six, it has the highest gross margin at 6.12%, 1 point above second place. But with a 6% gross margin, one upstream price increase eats half of it. I'm generally reluctant to hold this kind of business long term, because you're always worrying about the next price increase. When the increase actually comes, you don't even have the standing to say "no" — the customer turns around and goes to Quanta.

Q: Will it still be around in ten years?

Yes. Contract manufacturing will not disappear; as long as someone designs things, someone has to build them. Hon Hai has global capacity and decades of yield experience, and in ten years it will probably still be the largest player. That I believe.

But "still there" and "making money" are two different things. In ten years it may have revenue of TWD 20 trillion and still a 2.4% net margin. Hold it for ten years and you earn the money of revenue growth, not the money of compounding. That is rather different. When I buy a company, I want its profits to roll the snowball for me, not to watch its revenue get bigger. Bigger revenue is the customer's doing; fatter profit is the company's own skill.

Q: Can you understand it?

The business itself is easy to understand: buy materials, assemble, deliver — that's it in one sentence. What I can't understand is next year. Its orders come from the capex of a handful of cloud companies, and those companies reset guidance every quarter — how could I possibly calculate how many racks it will take next year?

Not understanding doesn't mean you can't buy; it means you can't take a heavy position. I value the part I understand and don't pay for the part I don't. The logic is simple: if you can't calculate next year's rack volume, don't price it into this year's share price. At 12.6x P/E, the market has already paid for "AI will keep growing" — that part I won't pay for. I only pay for what I can see; what I can't see, I leave for others to earn.

Q: At what price would you buy?

Below 8x P/E, around RMB 160. Back then I did not need to call the next year — only to judge that the company would not collapse and the industry would not disappear. Time would do the rest.

Or the other scenario: gross margin genuinely rises and holds above 6.5% for two or three consecutive quarters, at which point I am willing to buy at RMB 240. By then it is no longer a "foundry" but an "integrator with pricing power," and the valuation framework has changed. But I will not bet on this in advance; I want to see the numbers.

Q: What is the biggest fear?

The real fear is not a decline but "revenue keeps rising while profit never does." Such companies grind you down: every earnings report brings good news, yet the stock never moves, and you hold it while others make money.

The second fear is customers doing it themselves. If cloud providers eventually standardise the racks and bring procurement and installation in-house, contract manufacturers are left with assembly only—and by then even a 6% gross margin won't hold. This may not happen, but the direction is there. Investors need not guess when it will happen—only know whether they will get hurt when it does.

Q: If I already hold it, should I sell?

Ask yourself whether the reason you bought still holds. If you bought on "AI rack volume ramp," that thesis is intact and you can stay in. If you bought on "gross margin will improve," two quarters have now disproved it, and you should exit.

Buying a stock is buying the company. You sell because the company has changed or because the price is too expensive, not because the stock has fallen. At 239 yuan the price is neither expensive nor cheap to me—but if my thesis is wrong, the price does not matter. Be clear about why you buy when you buy, and you will not need excuses when you sell.

Conclusion

Duan Yongping's verdict: a good company, but not a good business; he understands the business, but not next year; buy when nobody wants it. He would look at it near RMB 160; at RMB 240 he would not. He would not short it, nor tell others not to buy it — he would say only one thing: this money is too hard-earned. The market offers plenty of businesses that do not demand such effort, so why wear yourself down on this one? Spending your time on businesses you understand and that can actually earn beats waiting here for a gross-margin inflection point. Hard-earned money is not impossible to make; it is simply not worth paying a premium for. Once that sinks in, whether the price is RMB 239 or RMB 160, you will have your own answer.
