# Hon Hai Precision（2317.TW）— Duan Yongping Supplement

- Research date：2026/10/19
- Price：239.5 TWD
- Market cap：About TWD 3.37 trillion

## Notes

This piece is a speculative Q&A on Hon Hai derived from the thinking framework Duan Yongping has stated publicly (good-business criteria, circle of competence, price versus value); it is not his own specific view on Hon Hai. All financial figures are drawn from the 2026Q2 financial report, the September revenue announcement and foreign-brokerage models.

## Q: Is this a good business?

Not good. A good business is one where "you make money without trying too hard"; Hon Hai has to try very hard to earn just a little. Revenue of NTD 2 trillion 525.9 billion, and only 59.97 billion is left at the end — 2.37%. Think about it: the customer gives you the most expensive components, has you assemble them, and pays you a small processing fee — that money is earned solidly, but the margin is thin.

I don't deny it does well. Among the six, it has the highest gross margin at 6.12%, 1 point above second place. But with a 6% gross margin, one upstream price increase eats half of it. I'm generally reluctant to hold this kind of business long term, because you're always worrying about the next price increase. When the increase actually comes, you don't even have the standing to say "no" — the customer turns around and goes to Quanta.

## Q: Will it still be around in ten years?

Yes. Contract manufacturing will not disappear; as long as someone designs things, someone has to build them. Hon Hai has global capacity and decades of yield experience, and in ten years it will probably still be the largest player. That I believe.

But "still there" and "making money" are two different things. In ten years it may have revenue of TWD 20 trillion and still a 2.4% net margin. Hold it for ten years and you earn the money of revenue growth, not the money of compounding. That is rather different. When I buy a company, I want its profits to roll the snowball for me, not to watch its revenue get bigger. Bigger revenue is the customer's doing; fatter profit is the company's own skill.

## Q: Can you understand it?

The business is understandable: buy materials, assemble, deliver — that's it in one sentence. What I can't understand is next year. Its orders come from the capex of a few cloud companies, and those companies change guidance every quarter — how could I possibly calculate how many racks it will take next year?

Not understanding doesn't mean you can't buy; it means you can't take a heavy position. I value the part I understand, and I don't pay for the part I don't. The logic is simple: if you can't calculate next year's rack volume, don't price it into this year's share price. At 12.6x PE, the market has already paid for "AI will keep growing" — that part I won't pay for. I only pay for what I can see; what I can't see, I leave for others to earn.

## Q: At what price would you buy?

Below 8x PE, around RMB 160. Back then I did not need to judge the next year; I only needed to judge that "this company will not collapse and this industry will not disappear." Time would do the rest.

Or the other scenario: its gross margin really does rise, holding above 6.5% for two or three consecutive quarters, and then I am willing to buy at RMB 240. Because by then it is no longer a "foundry" but an "integrator with pricing power," and the valuation framework has changed. But I will not bet on this in advance; I want to see the numbers.

## Q: What is the biggest fear?

The real fear is not a decline, but "revenue keeps rising while profit never does." Such companies grind you down—every earnings report brings good news, yet the stock never moves, and you hold it while others make money.

The second fear is customers doing it themselves. If cloud providers eventually standardize the racks and handle procurement and installation in-house, contract manufacturers are left with assembly only. By then even a 6% gross margin won't hold. This may not happen, but the direction is there. Investors need not guess when it will happen—only know whether they will get hurt when it does.

## Q: If I already hold it, should I sell?

Check whether the reason you bought still holds. If you bought because of "AI rack volume ramp," that reason still holds and you can stay in. If you bought because "gross margin will improve," two quarters have now disproved that reason, and you should exit.

I often say that buying a stock is buying the company, and you sell because the company has changed or the price is too expensive, not because it has fallen. At 239 yuan, the price is neither expensive nor cheap to me—but if my thesis is wrong, the price does not matter. Think clearly about why you buy when you buy, and you will not need excuses when you sell.
