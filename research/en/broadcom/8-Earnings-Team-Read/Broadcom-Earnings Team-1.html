Core view

Duan Yongping reads financial reports in one order only: is this a good business, how does it make money, and is that money sustainable. The numbers are the result, not the reason.

The quarter was strong: revenue +86%, AI +221%, net margin 55%. But a bad trend hides inside the good results—gross margin 78%→73%, showing that what it sells is getting "heavier."

The business: why customers have no choice but to buy

Of the USD 16.7 billion in AI semiconductor revenue, 70% is XPU—custom chips built for specific customers. Why do customers buy? Hock Tan's answer is cost: chips customised for specific large-model workloads can cost half as much as GPUs.

That claim needs unpacking. Customers are not buying "faster chips"; they are buying "lower cost per token." The larger the model and the greater the inference volume, the stronger the motive. Demand in this business is therefore driven not by technological leadership but by customers' unit economics—as long as custom chips are cheaper than GPUs, demand exists; how much cheaper they are determines how urgent that demand is.

Why Broadcom? Because it has failed seven times. Seven generations of TPU, five generations of MTIA, two generations of OpenAI projects—experience that cannot be bought. When customers assemble their own teams to do it, the first generation will most likely fail, at a cost of two years. The moat in this business is therefore not patents but "others cannot build it."

How the money is made: design fees thin out, hardware thickens

The standout number this quarter is not +221% but gross margin −2.1pct.

The revenue mix is shifting: XPU hardware is taking a larger share, and HBM a larger share of cost. Design fees (NRE) carry high margins on small revenue; chip resale carries large revenue at low margin. The more AI Broadcom sells, the bigger the low-margin slice becomes. That is the natural result of the business model, not a management misstep.

How would Duan Yongping see it? He would say this is no bad thing, but it demands repricing. A company that sells IP is worth 30x; one that sells hardware, 15x. Broadcom is now a blend of both, and 18x is the compromise — neither cheap nor expensive.

He would also press one question: if gross margin falls to 70%, is this still a business worth owning? The answer hinges on whether customers can still switch away. As long as they cannot, 70% is still a good business; if they can, 75% is not worth it.

Can it last: 3.5x shipment volume is a warning

XPU shipments rose 3.5x YoY. Impressive, but it also means the base is climbing fast. Full-year FY26 AI revenue was RMB 58 billion, with FY27 guidance at RMB 115 billion, up 98% YoY—yet annualising Q4 at RMB 86.8 billion, FY27 needs only another 32%.

"Doubling" is the YoY measure; "+32%" is the run-rate measure. The market is really trading the latter. That is also why the stock did not rise on guidance: the number is big, but the acceleration is slowing.

Duan Yongping's approach is simple: ask whether the company will still be around in ten years and what it will still be doing. The answer is most likely yes—still making custom chips, possibly for a different set of customers. This business will not disappear, but its golden age depends on how many more years this AI infrastructure cycle lasts. His move would be: recognise the business, but buy only when the price offers a margin of safety.

Conclusion

Duan Yongping-style conclusion: a good business that is getting heavier, reasonably priced but not cheap. All three tests hold: customers cannot switch away, demand is driven by unit economics, and the moat is experience rather than patents. Gross margin falling from 78% to 73% shows the model shifting from "selling design" to "selling hardware," so the valuation approach must change accordingly. Action: do not chase; wait for below USD 300.
