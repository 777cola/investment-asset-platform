## Revenue up 86%, stock still fell

Start with the numbers. Broadcom reported FY26 third-quarter results on September 2: revenue USD 29.591 billion, up 86% year on year; AI semiconductors USD 16.7 billion, up 221% year on year; Non-GAAP net income USD 16.372 billion, a net margin of 55%.

What kind of level is that? A company with USD 100 billion in annual revenue grew 86% in a single quarter, with its AI business up 221%. In any industry, those are explosive numbers.

Then the stock fell. It closed at USD 357.16 on September 3, down 2.7%; by September 15 it had fallen to USD 339.27. Measured from USD 427.76 on August 7, it is down 20% in six weeks.

What the market hammered was not the results, but two things unrelated to the results.

## First point: it increasingly resembles a hardware company

Broadcom's Non-GAAP gross margin traced this path over the year: 78%, 77.1%, 75.0%, 73% (Q4 guidance).

Why the decline? Management's explanation is blunt: XPU hardware is a rising share of the mix, and HBM memory is a rising share of cost.

In plain terms: a growing share of what Broadcom sells is "reselling someone else's hardware," and a shrinking share is "selling its own designs." Custom chip design fees carry high margins and small revenue; volume-produced chips carry large revenue and low margins, because TSMC wafer costs and memory makers' HBM costs get passed through.

The brutal part is that this is the same thing as its success. The faster AI revenue grows, the higher the hardware mix and the lower the gross margin. The more successful custom ASIC becomes, the more the company resembles a manufacturer.

So is it still a good business? Yes. A 73% gross margin still tops TSMC's 58% and that of any large semiconductor company. But it no longer commands a "software company" valuation—the market will not pay 30x for a company whose gross margin drops two points a year. 18x is the price of that shift.

## Second point: it is borrowing money on behalf of clients

This is messier.

Broadcom provides credit support for AI chip customers' purchases, through arrangements involving Apollo and Blackstone; the size has not been disclosed. BofA estimates that the platform could issue up to USD 150 billion in new debt in 2027, accumulating USD 370 billion in senior debt by mid-2029.

Don't rush to call it "fraud." It isn't fraud, and it isn't even aggressive accounting. It is an extension of a business model—translating "the customer can't afford it" into "I'll lend you the money to buy it."

The problem is that this money is not on Broadcom's financial statements. If all goes well, Broadcom pays nothing and sells more chips; if customer deployment falls short of expectations, the guarantees return to the financial statements in the form of purchase commitments, residual-value guarantees and the like.

As a shareholder, you are buying a chip order with an implicit guarantee attached. That is why, for the same profit growth of around 50%, the market gives Nvidia thirty times and Broadcom eighteen times.This is not mispricing; it is risk pricing.

## The third point: growth momentum is slowing

Now consider a more interesting set of numbers. The single-quarter AI revenue trajectory: about USD 8.76 billion in Q1, about 10.84 billion in Q2, 16.7 billion in Q3, and guidance of 21.7 billion for Q4. The quarter-on-quarter growth rates are +24%, +54% and +30%.

The +54% in Q3 is an outlier, reflecting Google's large-scale TPU v7 shipments plus Anthropic's initial deployment. The fallback to +30% in Q4 signals that the high base has arrived.

The company's FY27 guidance is USD 115 billion, up +98% year on year, which sounds aggressive. But annualizing Q4's 86.8 billion, FY27 only needs another 32%.

"Doubling" is the year-on-year measure; "+32%" is the run-rate measure. The market trades the latter. That is why the stock did not rise after the guidance.

This is not bad news; it is the normal marker of a company moving from breakout phase to delivery phase. Valuation in the delivery phase rests on EPS, not on story.

## So can we buy now?

USD 355, corresponding to 18.3x FY27 expected earnings (USD 19.38 per share), 27x FY26.

Three scenarios laid out: bear case USD 225 (AI only reaches USD 85 billion, financing platform runs into trouble), base case USD 388, bull case USD 504. The base case offers only 9% upside; the bear case implies 37% downside. The magnitude is roughly symmetric; the certainty is not.

The path up requires six things to hold simultaneously: AI revenue delivers, six customers spend on plan, gross margin holds at 71%, software does not decelerate, the financing platform does not blow up, and the market is willing to pay 20x. The path down requires just one thing to fail.

So my answer is: watch, not buy.

Two conditions, stated clearly: the stock falls below USD 300 (about 15.5x FY27), or the financing arrangement is explicitly disclosed as limited recourse with quantifiable exposure. If either appears, build a position at 3%, capped at 5%.

The nearest checkpoint is the FY26 fourth-quarter earnings on 9 December. Watch three things: whether AI guidance holds at USD 115 billion, whether gross margin holds at 73%, and whether management is willing to answer questions about the financing platform.

A good company does not need a good story on top of a good price. What it lacks right now is a clear explanation of that off-balance-sheet item.

One last point: a good company is not the same as a good stock. Broadcom is one of the best capital allocators I have seen in semiconductors, but its share price has now fully priced in "good company" and leaves no discount for "off-balance-sheet items that cannot be calculated." Only when it explains the accounts clearly, or when the market prices in that discount, does this stock warrant action.
