## Chen Fuyang: applying financial discipline to tech M&A

Hock Tan is not a technical CEO but a financial one. His playbook has four steps: buy a company with high customer stickiness, slow growth and stable cash flow; cut unprofitable product lines and 30%–50% of costs; convert the remaining business to subscriptions or long-term contracts; then use the freed-up cash flow to pay down debt, buy back stock and acquire the next company.

This playbook worked at CA and Symantec: revenue at both declined post-acquisition, but operating margins and free cash flow rose sharply. VMware is a scaled-up version of the same logic—the deal closed in November 2023, and by 2024 perpetual licenses had been converted to subscriptions. Customers complained loudly, yet software revenue rose from USD 21.5 billion in fiscal 2024 to around USD 35 billion in FY26 (rough calculation).

The premise of this playbook is that the customers you buy cannot walk away. VMware's virtualization runs in enterprise data centers, and replacing it means rebuilding the entire IT architecture, so it can raise prices. The same premise holds for the chip business, except the counterparty shifts from IT departments to six AI labs.

## Integrity and governance: three controversies laid bare

The first came in 2018, when Broadcom launched a hostile USD 117 billion bid for Qualcomm. CFIUS blocked it on national-security grounds and Trump signed an executive order prohibiting the deal. That episode defined Broadcom's political identity: incorporated in Singapore but headquartered in California, it is treated as "foreign," and major M&A must clear political hurdles.

The second was VMware's pricing. From 2024, perpetual licences shifted to subscriptions and products were bundled, with some customers seeing price increases of several multiples; AT&T sued, and regulators in Europe and the UK also voiced concerns. This is not an accounting issue but a commercial credibility issue — revenue rose in the short term, while customer relationships were damaged over the long term.

The third came in September 2026, when the media questioned whether the company provides financing support to AI customers and whether its exposure is opaque. The company did not respond directly, stressing only on its earnings call that "orders are locked in." What the three controversies share: none is fraud; all are choices to "trade structure for growth."

## Succession: the most easily overlooked risk

Hock Tan was born in 1952 and turns 74 in 2026. There is no public succession plan. The most likely internal candidate, Charlie Kawwas, President of Semiconductor Solutions, has never independently run a merger integration and has no capital-markets credibility.

Much of Broadcom's value rests on Hock Tan: the judgement behind six acquisitions, the instinct for whether a customer "can walk away," and Wall Street's trust in him. When he leaves, the multiple de-rates first; only then do you get to judge whether the new CEO is up to it.

This is not alarmism. When Buffett buys a company, he asks whether it can still make money with a mediocre CEO in charge. Broadcom can — its customer relationships and IP will not disappear with a change of person. But the ability to "buy another VMware-scale company" will disappear, and that is precisely the source of the re-rating of the past decade.

## Governance structure: The board is highly cooperative — both a strength and a weakness

Broadcom's board is small and its independent-director ratio meets Nasdaq requirements, but its oversight of management is limited. Tan Hock Eng serves as both chairman and CEO; nearly every major acquisition has been his alone, with the board ratifying it after the fact.

That structure is an advantage in the "buy cash flow" phase: decisions are fast, there is no wrangling, and the company is not held hostage to short-term performance. In the "take on off-balance-sheet risk" phase it is a hidden hazard: no one can block an invisible guarantee before the fact.

The rating in this report therefore carries an implicit judgment: we trust Tan Hock Eng's judgment, but we do not trust anyone's self-restraint on off-balance-sheet exposure. Trusting a person's ability and trusting the boundaries of a mechanism are two different things.

One further detail: Tan Hock Eng's compensation is almost entirely tied to the share price and free cash flow, with no metric tied to "off-balance-sheet exposure." Incentives drive behaviour — he pushes financing arrangements because they lift orders and the share price at the same time, while the risk only surfaces two or three years later.By the time he leaves, the next person will have to own that bill.
