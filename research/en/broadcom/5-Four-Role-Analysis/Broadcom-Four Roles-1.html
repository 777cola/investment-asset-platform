Core view

This business is fundamentally about selling design capability, not chip capacity. Customers are paying for the second path they cannot buy within a two-year engineering cycle: a chip customised for their own models at half the cost of a GPU.

A good business, but not an easy one. Its ceiling lies not in technology but in the length of the customer list—only six.

Who pays, and why they pay

The customers are six frontier model companies: Google, Meta, OpenAI, Anthropic and ByteDance, plus one undisclosed customer. What they buy is not a standard product but "a chip customised for my model"—from architecture definition, SerDes and packaging through to the software stack, Broadcom is involved end to end.

Why pay? Inference cost. Hock Tan said on the earnings call that chips customised for specific large-model workloads can cost half as much as GPUs. In business terms: customers are not buying faster chips, they are buying a lower cost per token. The larger the model and the greater the inference volume, the stronger the motive.

Why Broadcom rather than in-house? Because an in-house team means 2,000 people, five years, and a first generation that most likely fails. What Broadcom offers is experience that has "already failed seven times"—seven generations of TPU accumulation, from 28nm to 3nm, from single chips to 3D packaging.

How the money is made: design fees + wafer resale

Broadcom's XPU revenue splits into two components: one-time or staged design and development fees (NRE), and per-unit chip sales once volume production begins. The former carries high gross margin on small dollar amounts; the latter carries large dollar amounts at low gross margin, since TSMC wafer costs and HBM memory costs are passed through.

This explains a counterintuitive phenomenon: the faster AI revenue grows, the faster gross margin falls. FY26Q3 gross margin was 75%, down 2.1pct QoQ, with Q4 guidance at 73% versus 78% a year earlier. Not because of price cuts, but because "hardware resale" has risen as a share of the revenue mix.

Is this a good business or a bad one? Our judgment: still a good business, but the premium is shrinking. Design fees are an IP business and can command 30x P/E; chip resale is a manufacturing business worth only 15x. Broadcom today is a blend of the two, and the market has assigned a compromise multiple of 18x, which is roughly fair.

How long it can pay: demand stems from an observable variable

The six customers' ability to pay hinges not on profits but on financing capacity. This is the most uncomfortable part of the business—your customers are not buying your product out of operating cash flow; they are buying it with capital-market money.

In 2026 the cycle is still accelerating: Anthropic plans to deploy 1GW of Ironwood in 2026 and 5GW in 2027; OpenAI's first-generation Jalapeno has shipped, with a 2027 target of 1.3GW; Meta's MTIA has shipped three consecutive generations, with cumulative deployment of about 3GW by 2028. Together these define AI chip demand over the next three years—the volume behind Broadcom's FY27 guidance of USD 115 billion.

But these are plans, not contracts. Plans can change; contracts carry penalties. The best indicators to watch here are not orders but customers' financing costs and data-centre power-on progress—when either moves, the plans move with it.

Conclusion

A good business, but the valuation needs a discount. It sells design capability and IP at a 75% gross margin, to customers who cannot switch — the kind of business Duan Yongping would like. But it has only six customers, and their ability to pay depends on the capital markets, which would lead him to cap the position at a level where "losing it all would not affect my life." Conclusion: worth long-term tracking, not worth a heavy position now.
