Core thesis

Custom ASICs are nothing new — Google was already running TPUs in 2015. What changed in 2026 is that exploding inference demand turned "half the cost" from an option into a necessity.

Broadcom leads this segment on share, IP depth and customer relationships.But Munger would caution: the best position is often the most crowded one.

Position in the supply chain: sandwiched between TSMC and six customers

Upstream sits TSMC (3nm/2nm foundry), SK hynix/Samsung/Micron (HBM) and TSMC CoWoS (advanced packaging). All three links are tight in 2026 and Broadcom holds no pricing power over any of them — it can only queue, lock in capacity and absorb price increases.

Downstream sit six customers, over whom Broadcom has partial pricing power, since switching suppliers takes two years. But concentration cuts both ways: pricing power runs one-to-one, not one-to-many. Against Google, Broadcom is the only supplier able to take on TPU; against the six combined, it is a vendor whose prices can be squeezed.

At this position in the chain, gross margin is 75% — above TSMC and above Nvidia (around 70%). That premium reflects not scarcity but a revenue mix of design fees plus IP licensing. As revenue shifts toward hardware, gross margin here will converge toward TSMC's. Hence the 73% guidance.

Competitive landscape: three chasing, one defending

Marvell ranks second and is stronger with customers such as Amazon, but lags a notch behind on both scale and IP depth. MediaTek won the Google TPU V80 project as a second source that Google uses to squeeze pricing; it trails Broadcom's v8i, but once it works, Broadcom's share and pricing will both suffer. Design-service firms such as Alchip divert small and mid-sized customers and pose no direct threat.

The real threat comes from above: Nvidia. If it launches a sufficiently cheap inference offering—for example, making GPUs semi-custom, or opening the NVLink ecosystem to ASIC vendors—the cost advantage of custom ASICs is halved. Broadcom's moat is "customer relationships + IP"; Nvidia's moat is "ecosystem + software," and the latter is wider.

A Munger-style judgment: no second player in this industry will live comfortably, because customers want only two suppliers—one primary, one to squeeze pricing. Broadcom is now the primary supplier, and holding that position costs it lower gross margins.

Cycle position: mid-upcycle, with financing conditions tightening

AI capex is still climbing in 2026: the four major North American cloud providers have raised capex guidance repeatedly, and inference demand has only just begun to scale. On this view the industry is mid-upcycle, not at the top.

One variable is shifting, however: financing costs. AI data centre construction relies increasingly on debt and structured financing (Broadcom's platform is one example); when rates rise or credit spreads widen, construction slows first and orders are cut afterwards. The semiconductor industry's rule: order cuts always lead capex cuts by two quarters.

The cycle call, then: demand is safe through the first half of 2027; from the second half of 2027, watch two things — customers' financing costs and power-on progress. Both lead any earnings report.

Conclusion

Top-tier industry position, but the cycle is not cheap. Broadcom is the de facto standard in custom ASICs, with a moat built on a decade of accumulated customer relationships and IP that no one can displace near term. But Munger would remind us: the industry's most profitable moments are often exactly when everyone is expanding capacity and customers are borrowing. The metrics to track are not orders but customers' financing costs and the number of data centers being energized.
