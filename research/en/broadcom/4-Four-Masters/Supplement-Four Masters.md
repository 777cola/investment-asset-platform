## Duan Yongping: A business where "customers can't switch away" — but there are only six of them

Duan Yongping asks only three questions of a company: is the business model good, can I understand it, and is the price right. Broadcom passes the first two.

The business model rests on one idea: "I define the chip for you, and you can't switch away from me." A custom ASIC takes two years from architecture to mass production and involves deep coupling across SerDes, packaging, power and the software stack; a customer switching suppliers must redo two years of engineering. The pricing power this relationship confers is real — a 75% Non-GAAP gross margin, 17 percentage points above TSMC, because Broadcom sells design and IP, not fab depreciation.

The downside is equally clear: six customers determine its fate. Google, Meta, OpenAI, Anthropic, ByteDance and one undisclosed customer underpin USD 16.7 billion in single-quarter AI revenue. Duan Yongping's habit is to treat "customer concentration" as a hard constraint, not as part of the growth story. He would ask: if Anthropic slows next year, who fills the gap? If Google hands its next-generation TPU to MediaTek, what happens to Broadcom? With no answers to these two questions, size the position so it doesn't keep you up at night.

## Buffett: full marks for capital allocation, deductions for off-balance-sheet commitments

Buffett would start with capital allocation, and on that measure Broadcom is close to textbook. Over twenty years Hock Tan has made seven major acquisitions: LSI at USD 660 million, Brocade at USD 5.5 billion, CA at USD 18.9 billion, Symantec's enterprise business at USD 10.7 billion and VMware at USD 61 billion. Each bought assets "already installed in customer data centers and impossible to rip out," after which cash flow went to paying down debt, buying back stock and paying dividends. This is not the growth-stock playbook; it is the classic "use someone else's company to generate your own cash flow."

He would also notice two things immediately. First, the USD 59.6 billion of fixed-rate debt — not frightening: with interest coverage of 25x, it amounts to buying a 40% return asset with 4% money. Second, the financing support provided for AI customers' purchases — frightening. Buffett's attitude toward off-balance-sheet commitments is zero tolerance, because a guarantee is a promise in good times and a liability in a downturn, and at a turning point you cannot choose to exit.

So he would most likely deliver this verdict: a first-class company, but I cannot understand its off-balance-sheet; wait until it explains the structure clearly, or until the price falls to where I can bear the worst case. 18x P/E is not enough, because 18x rests on the premise that the guarantee does not blow up.

## Munger: selling shovels to people who borrow money to buy shovels is the classic cycle-top setup

Munger wouldn't look at the model; he'd look at the incentives. Broadcom's incentive right now is to sell the chips, and it will take on customer credit risk to do so. Its customers' incentive is to build compute as fast as possible, because falling behind means losing the race. Both point the same way: grow the orders, push the risk into the future.

This is the classic setup at a cycle top. Munger would cite the equipment makers of 2000 and the mortgage underwriters of 2008: the business model itself is fine; the problem is the counterparty risk taken on for growth, taken on at precisely the moment it should least be taken.

He would also flag three specific triggers: ① capex at the six major customers peaks in 2028 and AI revenue growth slows from 100% to 20%; ② the first default appears on the financing platform and the guarantees come back onto the balance sheet; ③ MediaTek's TPU V80 or Nvidia's open ecosystem takes away a major customer. If any one of these happens, the stock doesn't fall 26%; it halves.

His conclusion is usually short: good company, bad timing.

## Li Lu: ASICs are part of the endgame, not the whole of it

Li Lu would frame this through technology evolution. AI compute is splitting away from the "general-purpose GPU" into "training GPU + inference ASIC": training demands flexibility, inference demands low cost. Inference is acutely cost-sensitive, and custom chips can come in at half the price — an irreversible structural shift. Once model architectures stabilise, dedicated chips will take share from general-purpose silicon.

Broadcom is best positioned for this trend because it does both XPU and the Ethernet switching that connects these chips (Tomahawk 6 already covers nearly all XPU customers). Many sell compute; only Broadcom sells both compute and "the roads between compute."

He would, however, flag the cyclicality: ASIC demand rests on the capex of six companies, and part of that money is financing-driven. When financing costs rise, growth across the entire chain steps down. His verdict, then, is "right direction, right position, uncertain timing" — a judgment that argues for small positions, long holding periods and patience on price.

## Odds: symmetric upside, asymmetric certainty

Three scenarios side by side: 37% downside, 42% upside, 9% neutral. The range is roughly symmetric; the probabilities are not.

The upside case needs six things to hold at once: FY27 AI revenue of RMB 115 billion delivered, six customers spending as planned, gross margin held at 71%, software not decelerating, the financing platform not blowing up, and the market willing to pay 20x. The downside case needs only one of them to fail.

On expected value: weighted 25%/50%/25%, the target price is about USD 351, essentially the current price. That is the mathematical meaning of "watch"—not bearish, just no positive expectancy.

Only two paths turn it into positive expectancy: the price falls below USD 300 (29% upside in the neutral scenario), or uncertainty over the financing structure clears, cutting the pessimistic scenario's probability from 25% to 10%. The first waits on the market, the second on earnings.

## The question none of the four answered: what comes after 73%

One gap runs through all four analyses: nobody can say where the gross margin floor is.

What is known: FY25Q4 78%, FY26Q2 77.1%, FY26Q3 75.0%, FY26Q4 guidance 73%. On the AI revenue path of RMB 58 billion in FY26 and RMB 115 billion in FY27, the XPU mix will keep rising and so will the HBM cost share, so 73% is most likely not the floor.

Rough estimate: if 66% of FY27 revenue of USD 173.5 billion is AI chips and 34% is software and non-AI, blended gross margin will fall another 2–3 points from FY26 to around 71%. That number is not a disaster—a 71% gross margin still exceeds that of any large semiconductor company—but it changes how the stock is valued: the market will no longer apply a "software company" multiple, but a "hardware company plus software attach" multiple.

That is also why 18x is reasonable: the market is already pricing in gross margin decline. The real risk is not gross margin falling to 71%, but falling below 68% while still being called a "structural issue." That would signal pricing power is being lost.
