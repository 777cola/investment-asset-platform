## I. Company Overview

Broadcom Inc. (NASDAQ: AVGO) is incorporated in Delaware and headquartered in Palo Alto, California. Its predecessor, Avago, was the semiconductor division of Agilent and listed in 2009. In 2016 the company acquired the original Broadcom Corporation for USD 37 billion and took its current name; in November 2023 it completed the acquisition of VMware for consideration of approximately USD 61 billion.

Broadcom operates two segments: semiconductor solutions (FY26Q3 revenue USD 20.84 billion, 70%) and infrastructure software (USD 8.751 billion, 30%). Semiconductors are anchored by AI semiconductors (USD 16.7 billion, 80% of the segment), split between custom XPU (73% of AI revenue) and AI networking chips (27%).

Headcount is around 30,000 following M&A integration, and R&D expense runs at 9%–10% of revenue. Customer concentration is extreme: XPU has only six customers — Google, Meta, OpenAI, Anthropic, ByteDance and one undisclosed customer.

The revenue mix shows how far the company has changed. In FY2018 software revenue was near zero and the business was entirely semiconductors; in FY2026 software is about USD 40 billion, or 30%. That shift was bought — CA for USD 18.9 billion, Symantec for USD 10.7 billion, VMware for USD 61 billion. Post-acquisition, Broadcom's revenue went from "cyclical chips" to "chips + annuities," and its valuation multiple re-rated from 12x to above 18x.

## II. Industry analysis

AI compute is splitting into two markets: general-purpose GPUs for training and custom ASICs for inference. In 2026, inference volume exceeds training volume for the first time — the structural driver behind ASIC share gains.

Backing out the custom ASIC market size from Broadcom's FY26 AI revenue of USD 58 billion and a share of around 60% implies a market of roughly USD 90–100 billion. Growth over the next three years hinges on two variables: how far the inference mix can rise, and chip value per GW of compute.

The competitive landscape has three tiers: Nvidia at the top (broadest ecosystem), Broadcom and Marvell in the middle (custom capability), and design-service firms such as Alchip at the bottom (project-based). Broadcom holds the strongest position as the only company doing both XPU and switch chips.

Industry growth can be estimated as follows: Broadcom AI revenue of USD 58 billion in FY26, guidance of USD 115 billion for FY27, and our forecast of USD 132 billion for FY28 — a two-year CAGR of 51%. If Broadcom's share holds at 60%, the market will be about USD 220 billion in 2028. Against the GPU market, where Nvidia alone generates annual revenue in the hundreds of billions, ASIC scale is still small but growing faster.

Demand is geographically uneven: six customers are mainly in the US (ByteDance excepted), so this market is largely insulated from direct US export controls on China. Conversely, if US cloud capex slows, no second market globally can absorb this volume — this is where the ASIC business is more fragile than smartphone chips.

## III. Business model

Broadcom sells three things: design capability (NRE and IP), chips (XPU and networking), and software subscriptions (VMware and others).

Design capability is the entry point: customers wanting custom chips turn first to Broadcom for architecture definition. The revenue is small but high-margin. Chips are the main body: once in volume production they are priced per unit, generating large revenue at lower margins, since wafer and HBM costs must be passed through. Software is the annuity: customers renew, margins are 90%, and cash flow is stable.

The benefit of this mix is extremely strong customer stickiness — chips cannot be swapped out, software cannot be migrated away. The drawback is that the revenue mix "gets heavier" as AI's share rises, with gross margin falling from 78% to 73%. This is not a decline in competitiveness; the business model is drifting from "selling IP" to "selling hardware," and the valuation approach must change accordingly.

Unit economics can be roughly estimated: within a custom XPU's revenue, design fees (NRE) account for 10–20% at around 80% gross margin, while volume-production chips account for over 80% at 55%–60% gross margin. A customer's procurement cycle typically spans three product generations, five to six years — so once a customer is signed, it represents more than five years of cash flow. That is also why Broadcom is willing to solve financing problems for customers: what it is buying is not a single deal but five years of cash flow.

## IV. Financial analysis

FY26Q3: revenue USD 29.591 billion (+86%), AI semiconductors USD 16.7 billion (+221%), Non-GAAP gross margin 75.0% (down 2.1pct QoQ), Non-GAAP net income USD 16.372 billion (+95%), free cash flow USD 13.67 billion.

Three takeaways. First, AI accounts for all of the growth: of the USD 13.68 billion revenue increase, AI contributed USD 11.5 billion, while non-AI semiconductors grew just 4%. Second, gross margin is on a one-way slide: 78% → 77.1% → 75.0% → 73% (Q4 guidance). Third, cash conversion is weakening: free cash flow/net income of 0.83, with receivables and inventory absorbing more cash.

The on-balance-sheet position is clean: USD 59.6 billion of fixed-rate debt, interest coverage 25x. Off-balance-sheet is where the problem lies: financing support extended to AI customers is undisclosed, and BofA estimates USD 370 billion of senior debt could accumulate by mid-2029.

Three-year trend: FY24 revenue of about USD 51.6 billion (first full year of VMware consolidation), FY25 about USD 63.3 billion (rough estimate), FY26E USD 114.5 billion, FY27E USD 173.5 billion — nearly tripling in two years, all AI-driven. Expense ratios fell steadily over the same period: R&D from 15% to 9%–10%, SG&A from 6% to 2%–3%. That is why gross margin falls while net margin rises.

Two more numbers worth remembering: a Non-GAAP tax rate of 10%–12% (below the statutory rate, as much revenue comes from low-tax jurisdictions such as Ireland), and interest expense of about USD 2.5 billion/year (USD 59.6 billion debt × 4.2%). Low tax and low interest — not operating improvement — are the two technical reasons net margin reaches 55%.

## V. Management

Chairman and CEO Hock Tan has led the company since 2006. Born in 1952, he holds a degree in mechanical engineering from MIT and an MBA from Harvard. His playbook: "buy companies whose customers can't walk away, cut costs, convert to subscriptions, pay down debt, then buy again." Over twenty years he has completed six major acquisitions: LSI, Broadcom, Brocade, CA, Symantec's enterprise business, and VMware.

The track record is indisputable: Non-GAAP gross margin of 75%, net margin of 55%, annualized free cash flow of USD 52 billion. But in 2026 he made a new move—arranging financing for AI customers, swapping operating risk for credit risk. This move has no historical track record to reference.

On governance, he serves as both chairman and CEO, with major decisions driven by him alone. No succession plan has been disclosed; the most likely internal candidate is semiconductor president Charlie Kawwas. This is one of the hidden risks listed in this report.

The compensation structure is worth a look: Hock Tan's pay is almost entirely tied to the share price and free cash flow, with no metric tied to off-balance-sheet exposure. Incentives determine behavior—promoting financing arrangements can lift orders and the share price at the same time, while the risk only surfaces two or three years later. This is not a moral issue; it is a design issue.

## Six, growth drivers

Three drivers.

First, XPU shipments. FY26 shipments are up 3.5x YoY, and FY27 guidance calls for AI revenue of USD 115 billion (+98%). Customer deployment plans support this: Anthropic 1GW in 2026 and 5GW in 2027; OpenAI 1.3GW in 2027; Meta ~3GW cumulative by 2028.

Second, networking chips. Tomahawk 6 covers nearly all XPU customers, and Tomahawk 7 has taped out at 200Tbps. AI networking revenue is running at USD 4.51 billion per quarter, on a more diversified customer base than XPU.

Third, software. Following the VMware subscription transition, software revenue rose from USD 21.5 billion to USD 35 billion (rough calculation), growing 30%. This line is not exciting, but it cushions the downside.

The three lines differ in capital intensity: XPU requires upfront capital to lock in capacity (prepayments to TSMC, prepayments for HBM), tying up working capital; networking chips are standard products with fast inventory turnover; software carries almost no working capital. A rising AI revenue mix therefore compresses gross margin and raises working capital needs at the same time — the 0.83 free cash flow conversion rate is the combined result of these two factors.

On software, the key metric is VMware's subscription conversion rate. When the model switched to subscriptions in 2024, customer renewal rates dipped; by 2026 they have stabilized. The next step is selling VMware's private cloud stack (VCF) to enterprises already on public cloud. Whether this line succeeds determines whether software is USD 35 billion or USD 45 billion.

## VII. Competitive landscape

Broadcom faces three main rivals: Nvidia, which defends inference through generality; Marvell, its head-on competitor in custom ASIC; and MediaTek, Google's second source and the price challenger.

The Nvidia threat is structural: if it prices inference GPUs at only 20% above ASIC, the incentive to go custom weakens. It also has the means to wage a price war (70% gross margin, three times Broadcom's scale); Broadcom does not.

MediaTek winning Google TPU V80 is the more direct threat: not taking share, but compressing price. Once the second source is up and running, Broadcom's pricing power erodes.

Competitive conclusion: no share loss near term, but price compression. That is the other meaning of the 73% gross margin guidance.

The second-source economics are simple: a customer bringing in a second supplier does not need it to perform equally well, only to produce a comparable quote. MediaTek's TPU V80 is behind schedule, but if it can quote a price 15% lower, Google's negotiating position changes. Broadcom has only two choices: cut price to hold share, or hold price and lose share. Judging by the 73% guidance, it chose the former.

The barrier to custom chips is not technology but teams. No more than five teams worldwide can take on a 3nm custom project (Broadcom, Marvell, MediaTek, Alchip, Qualcomm). Building such a team takes five years, several billion dollars, and a customer willing to give you the first order. That is why new entrants struggle, and why Broadcom will not lose share even if it cuts price.

## VIII. Risk factors

Six risks: customer concentration (six customers drive revenue), gross margin erosion (78%→73%), financing guarantees (exposure undisclosed), technology roadmap (NVIDIA price cuts), policy controls (exports to China) and valuation (18x with no margin of safety).

Financing guarantees are the most dangerous. This is not a "risk" but an "uncertainty"—no probability distribution, impossible to price. Worst case: USD 50 billion loss, equivalent to USD 10.5 per share and USD 500 billion of market cap (−29%).

The other five are all trackable on public information, and bad news typically surfaces two quarters ahead.

The risks are also correlated: customer concentration and financing guarantees are two sides of the same coin. With only six customers, Broadcom has no bargaining power and can only help them solve their funding; helping them solve their funding binds its fate even tighter to theirs. The six risks therefore cannot be treated as six independent events for probability calculation—they will occur simultaneously.

## 9. Earnings forecast

FY27E: AI 115.0 billion + software 41.0 billion + non-AI 17.5 billion = USD 173.5 billion, net margin 53.3%, net income USD 92.4 billion, EPS USD 19.38, in line with consensus.

Sensitivity: if FY27 AI revenue reaches only 98.0 billion (−15%), revenue falls to USD 156.5 billion, net income to about USD 83.0 billion and EPS to USD 17.4, down 10%; at 85.0 billion (−26%), EPS is about USD 15, down 23%. Conversely, AI revenue of 130.0 billion implies EPS of about USD 21.5, up 11%. Upside and downside elasticity are asymmetric, with the downside larger.

## X. Valuation

On FY27 EPS of USD 19.38 and a 15–24x range: bear case at 15x (EPS USD 15, AI revenue reaching only USD 85 billion) = USD 225; base case at 20x = USD 388; bull case at 24x (EPS USD 21) = USD 504.

At USD 355.14 the stock trades on 18.3x, between the base and bear cases. Our sum-of-the-parts value of USD 386 is almost identical to the base case, implying market pricing is broadly reasonable.

The core contradiction in valuation: 18x is cheap among AI semiconductors, but the reason it is cheap is risk pricing, not mispricing.The market gives Nvidia 30x (cash-collecting) and Broadcom 18x (some orders rely on financing); this gap is reasonable.

Historical range: Broadcom traded at 12–15x from 2018–2023, when it was a cash-flow company; it re-rated to 25–35x in 2024–2025 on the AI narrative; by September 2026 it had fallen back to 18x as the narrative receded and the stock priced off EPS. Today's 18x sits at the midpoint of these three phases. A return to 30x requires a clear financing structure plus AI growth re-accelerating; neither is present currently.

Two supports on the downside: a free cash flow yield of 3.1% (annualized USD 52 billion / USD 1.69 trillion market cap) and a dividend yield of 0.66% (annualized USD 2.36). That support is not thick, but sufficient to prevent a sharp selloff absent bad news—what could truly break through is an event like guarantees returning to the balance sheet, not the valuation itself.

## XI. Investment recommendation

Rating: Watch, pending confirmation from the earnings report. No position.

Rationale: First-class business, reasonable valuation, structural doubts. One premise for buying cannot be verified (financing exposure), and the first principle of investing is not to lose money.

Triggers: (1) Share price falls below USD 300 (15.5x FY27), initiate a 3% position; (2) The December 9 earnings report clearly establishes the financing platform as limited recourse with quantifiable exposure, upgrade to Buy; (3) Gross margin falls below 70% with no disclosure, downgrade to Avoid.

Position cap 5%. Correlation with AI holdings such as Nvidia and TSMC is 0.7–0.8; do not double down on the same bet.

Watchlist rule: Update three readings after each quarterly report (AI revenue, gross margin, financing disclosure); remove from the list if two of the three deteriorate. The rule guards against "watching turning into buying" — the watchlist is not a list for waiting to buy, it is a list for waiting to judge.
