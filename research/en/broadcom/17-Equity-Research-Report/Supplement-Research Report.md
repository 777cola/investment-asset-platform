## I. Company Overview

Broadcom Inc. (NASDAQ: AVGO) is incorporated in Delaware and headquartered in Palo Alto, California. Its predecessor was Avago, the semiconductor division of Agilent, which listed in 2009; in 2016 it acquired the original Broadcom Corporation for USD 37 billion and adopted its current name; in November 2023 it completed the acquisition of VMware for consideration of approximately USD 61 billion.

The business has two segments: semiconductor solutions (FY26Q3 revenue USD 20.84 billion, 70%) and infrastructure software (USD 8.751 billion, 30%). Within semiconductors, the core is AI semiconductors (USD 16.7 billion, 80% of semiconductors), comprising custom XPU (73% of AI revenue) and AI networking chips (27%).

Headcount is around 30,000 (after M&A integration), with R&D expense at 9%–10% of revenue. Customer concentration is extreme: XPU has only six customers — Google, Meta, OpenAI, Anthropic, ByteDance, plus one undisclosed customer.

Revenue mix shows how the company has changed: in FY2018 software revenue was near zero and the business was entirely semiconductors; in FY2026 software is about USD 40 billion, or 30%. That shift was bought — CA for USD 18.9 billion, Symantec for USD 10.7 billion, VMware for USD 61 billion. Post-acquisition, Broadcom's revenue went from "cyclical chips" to "chips + annuities," and its valuation multiple re-rated from 12x to above 18x.

## II. Industry analysis

AI compute is splitting into two markets: general-purpose GPUs for training and custom ASICs for inference. In 2026, inference volume exceeds training volume for the first time, and this is the structural reason behind ASIC share gains.

Backing out the custom ASIC market size from Broadcom's FY26 AI revenue of USD 58 billion and a share of around 60%, the market is roughly USD 90–100 billion. Growth over the next three years depends on two variables: how far the inference mix can rise, and the chip value per GW of compute.

The competitive landscape has three tiers: Nvidia at the top (broadest ecosystem), Broadcom and Marvell in the middle (custom capability), and design-service firms such as Alchip at the bottom (project-based). Broadcom holds the best position, as the only company doing both XPU and switch chips.

Industry growth can be estimated as follows: Broadcom AI revenue of USD 58 billion in FY26, guidance of USD 115 billion for FY27, and our forecast of USD 132 billion for FY28 — a two-year CAGR of 51%. If Broadcom's share holds at 60%, the market will be about USD 220 billion in 2028. Against the GPU market (where Nvidia alone generates annual revenue in the hundreds of billions), ASIC scale is still small but growing faster.

Demand is geographically uneven: six customers are mainly in the US (ByteDance excepted), so this market is largely insulated from direct US export controls on China. Conversely, if US cloud capex slows, no second market globally can absorb this volume — this is where the ASIC business is more fragile than smartphone chips.

## III. Business model

Broadcom sells three things: design capability (NRE and IP), chips (XPU and networking), and software subscriptions (VMware and others).

Design capability is the entry point: when customers want custom chips, they first turn to Broadcom for architecture definition. This revenue is small but high-margin. Chips are the main body: once in volume production, they are priced per unit, generating large revenue at lower margins, because wafer and HBM costs must be passed through. Software is the annuity: customers renew, margins are 90%, and cash flow is stable.

The benefit of this mix is extremely strong customer stickiness — chips cannot be swapped out, software cannot be migrated away. The drawback is that the revenue mix "gets heavier" as AI's share rises, with gross margin falling from 78% to 73%. This is not a decline in competitiveness; the business model is drifting from "selling IP" to "selling hardware," and the valuation approach must change accordingly.

Unit economics can be roughly estimated: of a custom XPU's revenue, design fees (NRE) account for 10–20% at around 80% gross margin; volume-production chips account for over 80% at 55%–60% gross margin. A customer's procurement cycle typically spans three product generations, five to six years, meaning once a customer is signed, it represents more than five years of cash flow. This is also why Broadcom is willing to solve financing problems for customers — what it is buying is not a single deal but five years of cash flow.

## IV. Financial analysis

FY26Q3: revenue USD 29.591 billion (+86%), AI semiconductors USD 16.7 billion (+221%), Non-GAAP gross margin 75.0% (down 2.1pct QoQ), Non-GAAP net income USD 16.372 billion (+95%), free cash flow USD 13.67 billion.

Three takeaways. First, all growth comes from AI: of the USD 13.68 billion revenue increase, AI contributed USD 11.5 billion, while non-AI semiconductors grew only 4%. Second, gross margin is declining one way: 78% → 77.1% → 75.0% → 73% (Q4 guidance). Third, cash conversion is weakening: free cash flow/net income 0.83, with receivables and inventory absorbing more cash.

The on-balance-sheet position is clean: USD 59.6 billion of fixed-rate debt, interest coverage 25x. Off-balance-sheet is the problem: financing support provided to AI customers is undisclosed, and BofA estimates USD 370 billion of senior debt could accumulate by mid-2029.

Three-year trend: FY24 revenue about USD 51.6 billion (first full year of VMware consolidation), FY25 about USD 63.3 billion (rough estimate), FY26E USD 114.5 billion, FY27E USD 173.5 billion. Nearly tripling in two years, all driven by AI. Expense ratios fell steadily over these three years: R&D from 15% to 9%–10%, SG&A from 6% to 2%–3%. That is why gross margin falls while net margin rises.

Two more numbers worth remembering: Non-GAAP tax rate 10%–12% (below the statutory rate, as much revenue comes from low-tax jurisdictions such as Ireland), interest expense about USD 2.5 billion/year (USD 59.6 billion debt × 4.2%). Low tax and low interest are the two technical reasons net margin reaches 55%, not operating improvement.

## V. Management

Chairman and CEO Hock Tan has led the company since 2006. Born in 1952, he holds a degree in mechanical engineering from MIT and an MBA from Harvard. His methodology is "buy companies whose customers can't walk away, cut costs, convert to subscriptions, pay down debt, then buy again." Over twenty years he has completed six major acquisitions: LSI, Broadcom, Brocade, CA, Symantec's enterprise business, and VMware.

The track record of this approach is indisputable: Non-GAAP gross margin of 75%, net margin of 55%, annualized free cash flow of USD 52 billion. But in 2026 he made a new move—arranging financing for AI customers, swapping operating risk for credit risk. This move has no historical track record to reference.

On governance, he serves as both chairman and CEO, with major decisions driven by him alone. No succession plan has been disclosed; the most likely internal candidate is semiconductor president Charlie Kawwas. This is one of the hidden risks listed in this report.

The compensation structure is worth a look: Hock Tan's pay is almost entirely tied to the share price and free cash flow, with no metric tied to off-balance-sheet exposure. Incentives determine behavior—promoting financing arrangements can lift orders and the share price at the same time, while the risk only surfaces two or three years later. This is not a moral issue; it is a design issue.

## Six, growth drivers

Three drivers.

First, XPU shipments. FY26 shipments up 3.5x year over year; FY27 guidance calls for AI revenue of USD 115 billion (+98%). Customer deployment plans support this: Anthropic 1GW in 2026 and 5GW in 2027; OpenAI 1.3GW in 2027; Meta cumulative ~3GW by 2028.

Second, networking chips. Tomahawk 6 covers nearly all XPU customers; Tomahawk 7 has taped out at 200Tbps. AI networking revenue USD 4.51 billion per quarter, with a more diversified customer base than XPU.

Third, software. After the VMware subscription transition, software revenue rose from USD 21.5 billion to USD 35 billion (rough calculation), growing 30%. This line is not exciting, but it cushions the downside.

The three lines differ in capital returns: XPU requires upfront capital to lock in capacity (prepayments to TSMC, prepayments for HBM), tying up working capital; networking chips are standard products with fast inventory turnover; software carries almost no working capital. So a rising AI revenue mix simultaneously compresses gross margin and raises working capital needs — the 0.83 free cash flow conversion rate is the combined result of these two factors.

On software, the key metric is VMware's subscription conversion rate. When the model switched to subscriptions in 2024, customer renewal rates dipped; by 2026 they have stabilized. The next step is selling VMware's private cloud stack (VCF) to enterprises already on public cloud. Whether this line succeeds determines whether software is USD 35 billion or USD 45 billion.

## VII. Competitive landscape

Broadcom faces three main rivals: Nvidia (defending inference through generality), Marvell (head-on competition in custom ASIC), and MediaTek (Google's second source, the price challenger).

The Nvidia threat is structural: if it prices inference GPUs at only 20% above ASIC, the incentive to go custom weakens. And it has the means to wage a price war (70% gross margin, three times Broadcom's scale); Broadcom does not.

MediaTek winning Google TPU V80 is the more direct threat: not to take share, but to compress price. Once the second source is up and running, Broadcom's pricing power erodes.

Competitive conclusion: share will not be taken near term, but price will be compressed. That is the other meaning of the 73% gross margin guidance.

The second-source economics are simple: a customer bringing in a second supplier does not need it to perform equally well, only to produce a comparable quote. MediaTek's TPU V80 is behind schedule, but if it can quote a price 15% lower, Google's negotiating position changes. Broadcom has only two choices: cut price to hold share, or hold price and lose share. Judging by the 73% guidance, it chose the former.

The barrier to custom chips is not technology but teams. No more than five teams worldwide can take on a 3nm custom project (Broadcom, Marvell, MediaTek, Alchip, Qualcomm). Building such a team takes five years, several billion dollars, and a customer willing to give you the first order. That is why new entrants struggle, and why Broadcom will not lose share even if it cuts price.

## VIII. Risk factors

Six risks: customer concentration (six customers determine revenue), gross margin decline (78%→73%), financing guarantees (exposure undisclosed), technology roadmap (NVIDIA price cuts), policy controls (exports to China), valuation (18x with no margin of safety).

The most dangerous is financing guarantees. It is not a "risk" but an "uncertainty"—no probability distribution, impossible to price. Worst case estimated at USD 50 billion loss, equivalent to USD 10.5 per share and USD 500 billion market cap (−29%).

The other five can all be tracked with public information; bad news typically surfaces two quarters ahead.

The risks are also correlated: customer concentration and financing guarantees are two sides of the same coin—with only six customers, Broadcom has no bargaining power and can only help them solve their funding; helping them solve their funding binds its fate even tighter to theirs. So the six risks cannot be treated as six independent events for probability calculation—they will occur simultaneously.

## 9. Earnings forecast

Sensitivity: if FY27 AI revenue reaches only 98.0 billion (−15%), revenue falls to USD 156.5 billion, net income to about USD 83.0 billion and EPS to USD 17.4, down 10%; if it reaches only 85.0 billion (−26%), EPS is about USD 15, down 23%. Conversely, if AI reaches 130.0 billion, EPS is about USD 21.5, up 11%. Upside and downside elasticity are asymmetric: the downside is larger.

## X. Valuation

Using FY27 EPS of USD 19.38, applying 15–24x: bear case 15x (EPS USD 15, AI reaching only USD 85 billion) = USD 225; base case 20x = USD 388; bull case 24x (EPS USD 21) = USD 504.

The current price of USD 355.14 corresponds to 18.3x, sitting between the base and bear cases. Sum-of-the-parts gives USD 386, almost identical to the base case, indicating market pricing is broadly reasonable.

The core contradiction in valuation: 18x is cheap among AI semiconductors, but the reason it is cheap is risk pricing, not mispricing.The market gives Nvidia 30x (cash-collecting) and Broadcom 18x (some orders rely on financing); this gap is reasonable.

Historical range reference: from 2018–2023 Broadcom traded between 12–15x (when it was a cash-flow company); from 2024–2025 it re-rated to 25–35x (AI narrative); in September 2026 it fell back to 18x (narrative receding, EPS pricing). Today's 18x is the midpoint of these three phases. For it to return to 30x requires a clear financing structure + AI growth re-accelerating; neither is present currently.

Two supports on the downside: free cash flow yield of 3.1% (annualized USD 52 billion / USD 1.69 trillion market cap), dividend yield of 0.66% (annualized USD 2.36). The support is not thick, but sufficient to prevent a sharp selloff absent bad news—what could truly break through is an event like guarantees returning to the balance sheet, not the valuation itself.

## XI. Investment recommendation

Rating: Watch, pending confirmation from the earnings report. No position.

Rationale: First-class business, reasonable valuation, structural doubts. One premise for buying cannot be verified (financing exposure), and the first principle of investing is not to lose money.

Triggers: (1) Share price falls below USD 300 (15.5x FY27), initiate a 3% position; (2) The December 9 earnings report clearly establishes the financing platform as limited recourse with quantifiable exposure, upgrade to Buy; (3) Gross margin falls below 70% with no disclosure, downgrade to Avoid.

Position cap 5%. Correlation with AI holdings such as Nvidia and TSMC is 0.7–0.8; do not double down on the same bet.

Watchlist rule: Update three readings after each quarterly report (AI revenue, gross margin, financing disclosure); remove from the list if two of the three deteriorate. This rule exists to prevent "watching turning into buying" — the watchlist is not a list for waiting to buy, it is a list for waiting to judge.
