# Broadcom（AVGO）— Duan Yongping Supplement

- Research date：2026/10/02
- Price：355.14 USD
- Market cap：Approximately USD 1.69 trillion (rough estimate based on 4.77 billion shares)

## Notes

This module re-examines Broadcom through the investment principles Duan Yongping has stated publicly (circle of competence, business model, margin of safety, not-to-do list); these are not his own views. All figures match the Financial Report Close Read: FY26Q3 revenue 29,591, AI 16,700, Non-GAAP gross margin 75%, FY27 EPS USD 19.38. Price triggers: USD 300 ≈ 15.5x, USD 280 ≈ 14.4x.

## Q: Is this a good business?

Good. Good because customers cannot switch away from what it sells.

A custom chip takes two years from architecture to mass production, and the customer's models, software stack and data-center layout are all designed around that chip. Switching suppliers means redoing two years of engineering. In China this kind of business is called "locking in"; in the US it is called "sticky." The essence is the same: customers do not lack the will to switch, they lack the means.

Gross margin 75%, net margin 55% — better than almost any manufacturer I have seen. It does not need to build fabs or buy equipment; the capacity is TSMC's, the depreciation is TSMC's. **This is my favorite kind of business: asset-light, high-margin, customers cannot run away.**

But one thing is uncomfortable: it has only six customers. A good business should have many customers, so that losing one does not matter. Broadcom loses one customer and the financials show a hole.

## Q: Will it still be around in ten years?

Most likely yes. Demand for custom chips will not disappear; as long as models keep running, someone will need to make compute cheaper. Broadcom has the deepest accumulated expertise here—seven generations of TPU work is not for nothing.

But whether it will still look the way it does now in ten years, I cannot say. Customers may learn to design chips themselves, Nvidia may drive prices down, TSMC may start offering design services. **If any one of these three happens, Broadcom's role will change.**

When I buy a company, I like to ask "what will it earn money from in ten years." Broadcom's answer now is "designing chips for six companies." In ten years that answer may become "networking chips and software," and its valuation method will change then too. So I will not use "ten years" to talk myself into courage; I will look at it year by year.

## Q: Can you understand it?

The business itself is understandable, except for one piece I cannot figure out.

The understandable part: where revenue comes from (design fees + chip payments), why gross margins are high (selling IP, not capacity), why customers cannot switch (two years of engineering), and who the competitors are (Marvell, MediaTek, Nvidia).

The part I cannot figure out: why it borrows money on behalf of customers. **The company provides credit support for AI customers' procurement, and it has said not a word about the scale, the tenor, or the conditions under which it would have to pay.** BofA says this platform could carry USD 370 billion of senior debt by 2029, and I cannot verify whether that number is real or not.

Fail to understand one piece, and you fail to understand the whole company. Because that piece could be zero, or it could be the entire company.

## Q: At what price would you buy?

I'd start looking below USD 300, and I'd pull the trigger below 280.

Do the math: FY27 consensus EPS of USD 19.38 puts USD 355 at 18.3x, USD 300 at 15.5x and USD 280 at 14.4x. **Buying a company with AI revenue doubling and 73% gross margins at 15x, even in the worst case (AI revenue only reaching USD 85 billion, EPS USD 15), still leaves USD 20 of earnings as a floor — there's limited downside from here.**

It's not that 18x is uninvestable; it's that I won't buy when there's a piece I don't understand. A 20% discount is the margin of safety — that's the rule I set for myself.

If I were to change that rule, there's only one condition: the company spells out the terms of its financing platform. Once that's clear, I'd buy at 18x too.

## Q: What is the biggest fear?

The biggest fear is not customers cutting orders, but "guarantees coming back onto the balance sheet."

Customer order cuts are business fluctuations that can return next year; guarantees coming back onto the balance sheet are capital losses that cannot. If a company loses USD 50 billion on guarantees for its customers, it would need five years of free cash flow to make up for it—five years in which it cannot buy back stock, pay dividends, or make acquisitions.**For shareholders, this is not "a slightly weaker quarter," it is "a smaller principal."**

The second fear is gross margin. At 73% now, if it falls to 68% in two years, that means the company no longer has pricing power with its customers. At that point, 18x is expensive.

The third fear is Chen Fuyang's retirement. He is 74 and has no successor. The company's M&A capability rests on him alone.

## Q: Will you regret not buying?

A little. If AI stays hot for another three years and Broadcom runs to USD 600, I will regret not buying at 355.

But regret is bearable; losing money is not.** I would rather miss out than place a heavy bet where I cannot see clearly.** That is not conservatism, it is arithmetic: miss an opportunity and the cost is forgone gains; get it wrong once and the principal is gone, so when the next opportunity comes there is no money to play with.

So my move now is to put it on the watchlist and read the financials once a quarter. If it falls to 280, or if the off-balance-sheet items are clearly explained, I will buy. Until then, I watch.
