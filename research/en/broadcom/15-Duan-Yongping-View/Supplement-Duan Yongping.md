# Broadcom（AVGO）— Duan Yongping Supplement

- Research date：2026/10/02
- Price：355.14 USD
- Market cap：About USD 1.69 trillion (rough estimate on 4.77 billion shares)

## Notes

This module re-examines Broadcom against the investment principles Duan Yongping has stated publicly — circle of competence, business model, margin of safety, not-to-do list. These are not his own views. All figures tie to the Financial Report Close Read: FY26Q3 revenue 29,591, AI 16,700, Non-GAAP gross margin 75%, FY27 EPS USD 19.38. Price triggers: USD 300 ≈ 15.5x, USD 280 ≈ 14.4x.

## Q: Is this a good business?

Good — and good because customers cannot switch away from what it sells.

A custom chip takes two years to go from architecture to mass production, and the customer's models, software stack and data-center layout are all designed around that chip. Switching suppliers means redoing two years of engineering. In China this is called "locking in"; in the US it is called "sticky." The essence is the same: customers do not lack the will to switch, they lack the means.

Gross margin 75%, net margin 55% — better than almost any manufacturer I have seen. It does not need to build fabs or buy equipment; the capacity is TSMC's, the depreciation is TSMC's. This is my favorite kind of business: asset-light, high-margin, customers cannot run away.

But one thing is uncomfortable: it has only six customers. A good business should have many customers, so that losing one does not matter. Broadcom loses one customer and the financials show a hole.

## Q: Will it still be around in ten years?

Most likely yes. Custom chip demand will not disappear: as long as models keep running, someone will need to make compute cheaper. Broadcom has the deepest accumulated expertise here—seven generations of TPU work is not for nothing.

Whether the business still looks the way it does now in ten years, I cannot say. Customers may learn to design chips themselves, Nvidia may drive prices down, TSMC may start offering design services. If any one of these three happens, Broadcom's role will change.

When I buy a company, I like to ask what it will earn money from in ten years. Broadcom's answer now is "designing chips for six companies." In ten years that answer may become "networking chips and software," and its valuation method will change then too. So I will not use "ten years" to talk myself into courage; I will look at it year by year.

## Q: Can you understand it?

The business itself is understandable, apart from one piece I cannot figure out.

The understandable part: where revenue comes from (design fees plus chip payments), why gross margins are high (it sells IP, not capacity), why customers cannot switch (two years of engineering), and who the competitors are (Marvell, MediaTek, Nvidia).

The part I cannot figure out: why it borrows money on behalf of customers. The company provides credit support for AI customers' procurement, and it has said not a word about the scale, the tenor, or the conditions under which it would have to pay. BofA says this platform could carry USD 370 billion of senior debt by 2029, and I cannot verify whether that number is real.

Fail to understand one piece and you fail to understand the whole company, because that piece could be zero, or it could be the entire company.

## Q: At what price would you buy?

I'd start looking below USD 300 and pull the trigger below 280.

Do the math: on FY27 consensus EPS of USD 19.38, USD 355 is 18.3x, USD 300 is 15.5x and USD 280 is 14.4x. Buying a company with AI revenue doubling and 73% gross margins at 15x — even in the worst case, with AI revenue reaching only USD 85 billion and EPS of USD 15 — still leaves USD 20 of earnings as a floor, so downside from here is limited.

It's not that 18x is uninvestable; it's that I won't buy into a piece of the story I don't understand. A 20% discount is the margin of safety — that's the rule I set for myself.

I'd change that rule on one condition only: the company spells out the terms of its financing platform. Once that's clear, I'd buy at 18x too.

## Q: What is the biggest fear?

The biggest fear is not customers cutting orders but "guarantees coming back onto the balance sheet."

Order cuts are a business cycle that can reverse next year; guarantees coming back onto the balance sheet are capital losses that cannot. A company that loses USD 50 billion on customer guarantees would need five years of free cash flow to make it whole—five years in which it cannot buy back stock, pay dividends or make acquisitions.For shareholders, this is not "a slightly weaker quarter" but "a smaller principal."

The second fear is gross margin. At 73% now, a fall to 68% in two years would signal that the company no longer has pricing power with its customers—and at that point 18x is expensive.

The third fear is Chen Fuyang's retirement. He is 74 and has no successor; the company's M&A capability rests on him alone.

## Q: Will you regret not buying?

A little. If AI stays hot for another three years and Broadcom runs to USD 600, I will regret not buying at 355.

But regret is bearable; losing money is not. I would rather miss out than place a heavy bet where I cannot see clearly. That is not conservatism, it is arithmetic: miss an opportunity and the cost is forgone gains; get it wrong once and the principal is gone, so when the next opportunity comes there is no money to play with.

So my move now is to put it on the watchlist and read the financials once a quarter. If it falls to 280, or if the off-balance-sheet items are clearly explained, I will buy. Until then, I watch.
