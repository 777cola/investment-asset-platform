Core thesis

A rough screen of the AI semiconductor space leaves only two of six companies worth a deeper look: Nvidia and Broadcom. Nvidia has the strongest ecosystem but the richest valuation; Broadcom has the fastest growth but structural questions.

Broadcom is the final pick not because it is "the best" but because it offers "the best odds": 18x FY27 for 48% EPS growth, plus an undervalued second leg in networking chips.

Screening: eliminate three first

The first round screens on two things: AI revenue share and whether the moat is replicable.

AMD is out. Its MI-series AI accelerators are closing the hardware gap fast, but the software ecosystem (ROCm) still trails CUDA by a clear margin, customer migration costs are high, and its 2026 share remains in the single digits. Hardware can be caught up; ecosystems cannot.

Alchip is out. It is a design services business earning NRE plus a small volume-production cut, project-based, with no customer lock-in and 30% gross margins. Such companies see orders surge in boom times and drop to zero in downturns.

MediaTek is out. Its AI exposure is too small. Winning Google's TPU V80 is a positive, but as a second source its share and pricing are set by Google, and its core business remains smartphone SoCs, with limited correlation to AI compute.

Second screen: where Marvell falls short

Marvell most resembles Broadcom: custom ASIC, optical interconnect, storage controllers and a similar customer mix. Its custom business will be about one-third the size of Broadcom's in 2026, with gross margins around 60%.

It falls short in three places. First, no switch chips. Broadcom can sell "compute + connectivity"; Marvell can only sell "compute + optical interconnect", a weaker cross-sell. Second, IP depth. SerDes is Broadcom's in-house core; Marvell relies partly on external licensing. Third, customer relationships. Broadcom has built seven generations of TPU with Google, while Marvell's customers are more fragmented and its projects shorter.

Conclusion: Marvell is a "smaller Broadcom", yet its valuation is not cheap (around 25x). There is no reason to buy the smaller version instead of the original.

The boundaries of the funnel: why only these six companies were screened

This funnel screens only for "AI compute names you can buy directly," so it excludes three categories of company.

The first is captive silicon not sold externally: Google TPU, Amazon Trainium, Microsoft Maia. However good these chips are, investors cannot buy standalone exposure—only the parent, whose valuation is set by its cloud business.

The second is equipment and materials: ASML, Applied Materials, Lam Research. These names benefit from capex across the entire chain, but AI revenue elasticity is small and they have already run a long way.

The third is optical modules and connectors: Zhongji Innolight, Eoptolink, Credo. Elasticity here is the highest, but the technical barrier is lower than in chip design and cyclical swings are more violent. Exclusion is not a bearish call; this round of the funnel is looking for "the highest-certainty position," not "the highest-elasticity position."

Final pick: why Broadcom

That leaves NVIDIA and Broadcom. NVIDIA is the better company — widest ecosystem, fastest growth, deepest moat — but trades at around thirty times earnings, and the "AI compute leader" story is already fully priced in.

Broadcom offers better odds: 18.3x FY27 expected earnings against 48% EPS growth (USD 13.1 to 19.38), a free cash flow yield of 3.1%, plus an underappreciated second leg — AI networking chips at USD 4.51 billion per quarter, with a more diversified customer base than XPU.

Buying Broadcom is not buying "the best AI chip company"; it is buying "the cheapest AI chip leader." The distinction matters: the former requires judging where the technology ends up, the latter only whether 18x can hold.

Two risks could break that: gross margin falling below 70%, and a blow-up in financing guarantees. Neither means "the company is getting worse" — both mean "the structure is deteriorating." So after the final selection, this stays on the watchlist, not the buy list.

Conclusion

Funnel conclusion: six screened down to two, final pick Broadcom. Eliminated AMD (ecosystem), Alchip (project-based), MediaTek (small exposure) and Marvell (a smaller version that isn't cheap). Nvidia is the better business but the more expensive stock; Broadcom is cheaper but carries structural questions. At 18x, Broadcom offers the best risk-reward among large caps in this AI cycle; at 30x, it offers nothing.

One more note to myself: this funnel selects for risk-reward, not quality. On quality alone the answer is always Nvidia — the only question is price. Broadcom wins because the market has already priced its structural problems once: 18x embeds a discount for gross-margin decline and financing exposure, while Nvidia's 30x embeds nothing.
