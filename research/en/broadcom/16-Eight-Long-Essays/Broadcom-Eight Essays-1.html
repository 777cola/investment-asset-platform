Core thesis

Most people understand Broadcom through two labels: a chip company, and a serial acquirer. Neither label works in 2026.

Its real identity now: custom compute supplier to six frontier labs, plus a cash-flow machine assembled through acquisitions. Miss that identity and you will read its 18x as "cheap," or its 73% gross margin as "decline."

The first assumption to reset: it is not Nvidia's rival

The market likes to frame Broadcom and Nvidia as opposites: one sells general-purpose GPUs, the other custom ASICs, and one must win while the other loses. That framework is wrong.

The real picture is division of labor. Training demands flexibility—model architectures change constantly—so general-purpose GPUs prevail; inference demands low cost, and once models stabilize, purpose-built chips prevail. Broadcom plays the latter game, Nvidia the former. In 2026 inference volume overtakes training volume—that is why ASIC share is rising, not why Nvidia is failing.

So the right question is not "who wins" but "how big is the inference pie, and how much of it can Broadcom carve out." The pie is growing, Broadcom's share of it is rising, but the slice it takes is a low-margin one—hence the 73% gross margin.

The second assumption to reset: its growth is not a "cyclical upswing" but a "change in customer mix"

From 2018 to 2023, Broadcom's revenue relied almost entirely on M&A: buy a company, cut costs, revenue carries over and profit rises. Over those five years its organic growth was in the single digits, and the market gave it 12–15x, treating it as a bond substitute.

After 2024, that changed. Not because of the semiconductor cycle, but because the customer base changed—from "the procurement departments of thousands of enterprises" to "the CTOs of six labs." These six customers do not budget annually; they budget by GW. Only when the unit of budgeting shifted from "millions of dollars" to "billions of dollars" did Broadcom's revenue curve take this shape.

The upside of this shift is faster growth; the downside is concentration. Two sides of the same coin—you cannot take only one.

The third perception to reset: software is not a supporting player but the ballast

Many people look at Broadcom and see only AI semiconductors, treating its USD 8.75 billion in software revenue as "some other business." That view undervalues the company.

Software (VMware, CA, Symantec) carries gross margins around 90%, generates steady cash flow, and serves hundreds of thousands of enterprise customers. It does two things: it props up profit when the AI cycle turns down, and it supplies cash flow to the chip business to lock in capacity and fund R&D. Without this software, Broadcom could not have carried USD 60 billion of debt through the 2022–2023 semiconductor downturn.

Valuation should therefore treat the two parts separately: software at 20–25x (annuity-like cash flow), AI chips at 18–22x (cyclical growth), non-AI chips at 12x (mature business). Adding the three gives roughly USD 386 per share — almost identical to the USD 388 neutral-case figure.

After the cognitive reset, what questions remain?

With the perception reset complete, the questions become very specific: how many years can the budgets of six customers last? Where is the floor for gross margin? How big is the book of financing provided to customers?

None of these three questions requires judging whether AI is a bubble; they only require tracking three sets of numbers: customers' capital expenditure, quarterly gross margin, and disclosures from the financing platform. This is the right posture for researching Broadcom — do not guess the endgame, just reconcile the accounts.

One final reminder: a perception reset does not equal being bullish. Viewing Broadcom as an "AI infrastructure supplier" leads to the conclusion that "it is worth 18–22x," not that "it should rise." At 18.3x currently, it sits right within that range, so the action is to watch.

Conclusion

Conclusion of the cognitive reset: do not view Broadcom through the framework of an "AI concept stock" or an "Nvidia substitute." It is a custom compute supplier to six labs, plus a cash-flow machine assembled through M&A; its three layers (software, AI chips, non-AI chips) should be valued separately. Valued separately, it comes out to USD 386, a 9% gap from the current price of USD 355—not expensive, not cheap.

A final note on why a cognitive reset is needed "now." On August 7, 2026, the stock price was USD 427.76, and the market was still pricing it as an "AI concept stock"; by September 15 it had fallen to USD 339.27, and the framework had changed entirely within six weeks. Same fundamentals, two prices—the only difference is which framework the market uses to view it. Recognizing the framework is more useful than forecasting the price.
