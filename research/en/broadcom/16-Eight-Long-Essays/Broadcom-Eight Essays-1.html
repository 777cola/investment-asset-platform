Core thesis

Most investors still frame Broadcom through two labels: a chip company and a serial acquirer. Neither holds in 2026.

Its real identity: custom compute supplier to six frontier labs, and a cash-flow machine assembled through acquisitions. Miss that and you will misread 18x as "cheap," or a 73% gross margin as "decline."

The first assumption to reset: it is not Nvidia's rival

The market likes to frame Broadcom and Nvidia as opposites: one sells general-purpose GPUs, the other custom ASICs, and one must win for the other to lose. That framework is wrong.

The real picture is a division of labour. Training demands flexibility—model architectures change constantly—so general-purpose GPUs prevail; inference demands low cost, and once models stabilise, purpose-built chips prevail. Broadcom plays the latter game, Nvidia the former. In 2026 inference volume overtakes training volume—that is why ASIC share is rising, not why Nvidia is failing.

So the right question is not "who wins" but "how big is the inference pie, and how much of it can Broadcom carve out." The pie is growing and Broadcom's share of it is rising, but the slice it takes is a low-margin one—hence the 73% gross margin.

The second assumption to reset: growth here reflects a change in customer mix, not a cyclical upswing

From 2018 to 2023 Broadcom's revenue growth came almost entirely from M&A: buy a company, cut costs, and the top line carries over while profit rises. Organic growth ran in the single digits over those five years, and the market treated the stock as a bond substitute, awarding it 12–15x.

That changed after 2024—not because of the semiconductor cycle, but because the customer base changed, from the procurement departments of thousands of enterprises to the CTOs of six labs. These six customers do not budget annually; they budget by GW. Only once the unit of budgeting shifted from millions of dollars to billions did Broadcom's revenue curve take this shape.

The upside of this shift is faster growth; the downside is concentration. Two sides of the same coin—you cannot take only one.

The third perception to reset: software is not a supporting player but the ballast

Broadcom is widely viewed as an AI semiconductor story, with its USD 8.75 billion software business treated as an afterthought. That view undervalues the company.

Software (VMware, CA, Symantec) carries gross margins of around 90%, generates steady cash flow and serves hundreds of thousands of enterprise customers. It does two things: it cushions profit when the AI cycle turns down, and it supplies the chip business with cash to lock in capacity and fund R&D. Without this software, Broadcom could not have carried USD 60 billion of debt through the 2022–2023 semiconductor downturn.

Valuation should therefore treat the two parts separately: software at 20–25x (annuity-like cash flow), AI chips at 18–22x (cyclical growth) and non-AI chips at 12x (mature business). Summing the three gives roughly USD 386 per share — almost identical to the USD 388 neutral-case figure.

After the cognitive reset, what questions remain?

With the perception reset complete, the questions become very specific: how many years can the budgets of six customers last? Where is the floor for gross margin? How big is the book of financing provided to customers?

None of these three questions requires a view on whether AI is a bubble; they only require tracking three sets of numbers: customer capex, quarterly gross margin, and disclosures from the financing platform. That is the right posture for researching Broadcom — don't guess the endgame, just reconcile the accounts.

One final reminder: a perception reset does not equal being bullish. Framing Broadcom as an "AI infrastructure supplier" gets you to "it is worth 18–22x," not "it should rise." At 18.3x currently, the stock sits right within that range, so the action is to watch.

Conclusion

Conclusion of the cognitive reset: stop viewing Broadcom as an "AI concept stock" or an "Nvidia substitute." It is a custom compute supplier to six labs and a cash-flow machine assembled through M&A; its three layers (software, AI chips, non-AI chips) should be valued separately. On a sum-of-the-parts basis, that yields USD 386, a 9% gap to the current USD 355—neither expensive nor cheap.

A final note on why the cognitive reset is needed "now." On August 7, 2026, the stock was at USD 427.76 and the market was still pricing it as an "AI concept stock"; by September 15 it had fallen to USD 339.27, and the framework had changed entirely within six weeks. Same fundamentals, two prices—the only difference is the framework through which the market views it. Recognising the framework is more useful than forecasting the price.
