Core view

Broadcom's moat is not patents, not scale, but time—a customer switching suppliers must redo two years of engineering.

That moat runs deep: seven generations of TPU accumulation, in-house SerDes, advanced packaging, and the top position in Ethernet switching—none of which money can buy in the short term. But it protects only six customers, so the channel is narrow.

The hardest lesson: failure experience

In custom chips, success teaches you little; failure teaches you everything. A chip takes two years from architecture to volume production, and the pitfalls along the way include SerDes timing closure, HBM thermal dissipation, package warpage, the power wall and yield ramp.

Broadcom has been through seven generations of TPU, five generations of MTIA, two generations of OpenAI projects and Anthropic's Ironwood, and it has hit every one of these pitfalls. When customers assemble their own teams, the first generation most likely fails, and what is lost is not money but two years. In the AI race, two years means elimination.

This moat has a distinctive trait: it is not written in patents but in engineers' memory. Rivals can poach people and take away part of it, but they cannot take away an organization's muscle memory.

Rule two: SerDes and packaging determine whether the pieces can "connect"

No matter how powerful an AI chip's compute is, it is useless if it cannot connect to other chips. SerDes is the physical layer for high-speed communication between chips, with speeds moving from 56G to 112G and then 224G—each generation is a hard-fought battle.

Broadcom's in-house SerDes offers two advantages: low cost (no licensing fees) and fast iteration (it can be designed in sync with switch chips). It also sells this IP to other ASIC vendors—effectively helping competitors build weapons while collecting payment.

Packaging is the other track: 3D packaging, CoWoS, and CoWoS-L, co-developed by Broadcom and TSMC.Together, these two tracks form the barrier to "whether compute can be connected"—it is harder to replicate than the performance of a single chip.

Article 3: Ethernet, a standards war still being fought

For interconnect inside AI clusters, Nvidia uses NVLink (proprietary) and Broadcom uses Ethernet (open). These are two philosophies: proprietary pursues performance, open pursues cost and ecosystem.

Broadcom's bet is that as clusters scale from a thousand chips to a hundred thousand, the cost of proprietary interconnect will spiral out of control and customers will shift to Ethernet. Tomahawk 6 already covers nearly all XPU customers; Tomahawk 7 runs at 200Tbps and has taped out.

This battle is not over.If Nvidia opens NVLink to third parties, or the UALink standard fragments, Broadcom's networking leg will suffer. The quality of this moat is "strong," but not "strongest," because it depends on where industry standards go.

The boundaries of the moat: what it cannot protect

Three things it cannot protect.

First, it cannot protect manufacturing. Wafers sit with TSMC, HBM sits with the memory makers, and Broadcom has no pricing power over either—it queues and accepts price increases. The moat lies in the "design" segment; the manufacturing segment belongs to others.

Second, it cannot protect against customer in-house development. Google's TPU team can already define architectures independently, and Broadcom's role has retreated from "co-design" to "implementation and volume production." This trend will continue, and the value captured will decline.

Third, it cannot protect against second sourcing. MediaTek winning TPU V80 reflects Google building a second supplier. Once the second source is up and running, Broadcom's share and pricing will both come under pressure.

So the accurate description of this moat is: deep enough to support an 18–22x valuation, not wide enough to support 30x.

Conclusion

Moat verdict: depth 9, width 6, overall 7.5. The hardest element is "failure experience" plus the two-year engineering cycle; the least certain is the Ethernet standards war. Investment implication: the moat is enough for Broadcom to thrive in the AI chain, but not enough to earn it a software-company valuation — 18x is where it belongs.

The moat carries another often-overlooked cost: it takes money to maintain. Broadcom's R&D expense ratio has long run at 9%–10%, and adding tape-out (tens of millions of dollars per 3nm run) puts the bill at several billion dollars a year.
