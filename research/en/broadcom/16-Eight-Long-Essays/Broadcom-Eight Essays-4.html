Core thesis

Broadcom's results look strong: revenue USD 29.591 billion, up 86% year on year, Non-GAAP net margin 55.3%, free cash flow USD 13.67 billion. But three numbers in these strong results are easy to misread.

The 55% net margin is adjusted, the USD 13.67 billion of free cash flow represents only an 83% conversion rate, and the 60% ROE stems from a small denominator. One more overlooked figure: dividends plus buybacks — shareholder returns — total USD 11.3 billion a year.

Figure one: the 55% net margin is "adjusted"

Non-GAAP net income USD 16.372 billion, net margin 55.3%. GAAP net income USD 13.1 billion, net margin 44.3%. The gap is USD 3.27 billion.

What sits inside that USD 3.27 billion? Amortization of acquired intangibles (VMware, CA, Symantec), stock-based compensation, and possibly restructuring charges. These are real costs — amortization maps to consideration paid in the acquisition year, stock-based compensation maps to shareholder dilution.

Valuing on Non-GAAP is industry convention, because amortization does not reflect current-period operations; but using Non-GAAP to measure "how profitable this company is" overstates it by 11 percentage points. The correct use: Non-GAAP for valuation, GAAP for judging asset quality.

Figure two: free cash flow of RMB 13.67 billion, a conversion rate of only 83%

Quarterly free cash flow was USD 13.67 billion, a historical high. But Non-GAAP net income over the same period was 16.372 billion, a conversion rate of 0.83.

The 2.7 billion gap sits in two places: accounts receivable (AI revenue up 54% QoQ, customer payment terms unchanged, so receivables rose in step) and inventory (materials stocked ahead of next quarter's 21.7 billion in AI shipments).

This is normal in an expansion phase, even necessary — without the inventory build, shipments cannot be delivered.But it carries two implications: the income statement's growth rate looks better than cash flow; and if demand turns, the inventory build becomes an impairment.

The tracking method is simple: watch next quarter's cash conversion rate. If it recovers above 0.95, the issue was timing; if it keeps falling, revenue quality is deteriorating.

Figure three: ROE 60%, half of it created by accounting

ROE of about 60% looks like a money-printing business. But break it down: numerator USD 62.5 billion (FY26E net profit, rough estimate), denominator about USD 105 billion (shareholders' equity).

Why is the denominator so small? Because Broadcom has repurchased stock for years, and amortization of acquisition intangibles also reduces equity. If a company distributes all its profit or buys it back, equity gets compressed very low and ROE becomes very high — this is an accounting phenomenon, not an operating improvement.

A more reliable metric is ROIC: invested capital of about USD 150 billion (equity + debt − cash), after-tax operating profit of USD 62.5 billion, a return of 42%. That number is still very high, showing capital efficiency is real — just not as exaggerated as 60%.

The overlooked number: USD 11.3 billion of shareholder returns a year

At a quarterly dividend of USD 0.59 and 4.77 billion shares, the annual payout is USD 11.3 billion, a dividend yield of 0.66%. Including buybacks, shareholder returns come to roughly 60%–70% of free cash flow.

That number says two things. First, Broadcom is not a growth stock that plows everything back into R&D; it is a company that returns most of its cash to shareholders — Hock Tan's consistent style. Second, it still has room: annualized free cash flow is USD 52 billion, the dividend uses only USD 11.3 billion, and the rest can go to debt repayment, buybacks, or another acquisition.

For investors, that means two things: the dividend cushions the downside (though only 0.66%), and buybacks support EPS on the upside. This is a growth stock with cash-flow discipline, not a pure growth stock.

Conclusion

Financial reality: the numbers are real, but read them by definition. Non-GAAP net margin of 55.3% corresponds to GAAP 44.3%, free cash flow of RMB 13.67 billion corresponds to a conversion rate of 0.83, and ROE of 60% corresponds to ROIC of 42%. The company's true profitability is "excellent", not "miraculous"; its cash flow is "strong", not "limitless".

One more seasonal caveat: Broadcom's fiscal quarters end in early November, early February, early May and early August, offset from calendar quarters. Do not read "Q3" as July-September; it is May-August. This is an easy place to err when comparing growth with peers, especially against Nvidia (fiscal quarters ending late January, late April, late July and late October).
