Core view

Broadcom's risks break down into six: customer concentration, gross-margin erosion, financing guarantees, technology roadmap, regulatory control, and valuation.

Four of the first five can be quantified; only the financing guarantees cannot. And that unquantifiable one is precisely the one that could halve the stock price.

Four trackable metrics: all have tracking indicators

Customer concentration, gross margin, technology roadmap and valuation — all four can be tracked using public information.

Customer concentration comes down to two numbers: capex guidance from the six largest customers, and MediaTek's share of the Google project. Gross margin comes down to quarterly figures and the explanation of mix. Technology roadmap comes down to the per-token cost of Nvidia's inference cards and whether NVLink is opened to third parties. Valuation comes down to interest rates and risk appetite for the AI sector.

The common thread: bad news shows up two quarters early. Before customer capex guidance is cut, language shifts to "delayed" and "phased". Before gross margin declines, the explanation shifts to "mix change". Listening to the wording gets you there earlier than watching the numbers.

The line that cannot be counted: three possible outcomes of the guarantee

The financing guarantee exposure is not disclosed, so only three outcomes can be envisaged.

The first, most optimistic: the arrangement is limited-recourse structured financing, with Broadcom providing only credit enhancement and bearing no ultimate loss. In this case, its value to Broadcom is "locking in orders," with risk close to zero.

The second, neutral: Broadcom bears partial residual-value guarantees or purchase commitments, in the USD 10 billion range. If customer deployment falls short of expectations, Broadcom would absorb an impairment of USD 10–20 billion, equivalent to one-quarter to one-half of a year's free cash flow.

The third, worst case: Broadcom bears a substantive guarantee, in the tens of billions of dollars. At USD 50 billion, divided by 4.77 billion shares, that is USD 10.5 per share, equivalent to 54% of FY27 EPS; by market cap, USD 500 billion, the stock price −29%.

My guess for the probabilities of the three outcomes is 40%/45%/15%. The weighted expected loss is about USD 10.5 billion, or USD 2.2 per share. That number is not large, but it has no cap—that is the problem.

Policy risk: an underpriced thread

Broadcom's China revenue share is low, with exposure mainly in WiFi/RF and some networking chips; its XPU customers are all US companies. But the impact of export controls will transmit through two channels.

First, TSMC's capacity allocation. If controls expand to "custom chips containing US IP," TSMC will reallocate capacity away from Chinese customers, indirectly affecting Broadcom's production scheduling.

Second, customers' compliance costs. If AI chip exports require licenses, the six customers' global deployment plans will become more complex and buildout will slow. This risk is low-probability, but if it materializes it hits the entire chain, not just Broadcom.

Tail scenario: if three things happen at once

Run a stress test: assume 2027 AI revenue reaches only USD 85 billion (26% below guidance), gross margin falls to 68%, and guarantee impairments hit USD 20 billion.

EPS drops from 19.38 to around USD 12, the market multiple compresses from 18x to 12x, implying a share price of USD 144 — down 59%.

I put the probability of this scenario at 5%–10%, but its existence determines the position cap. A 5% position loses 3 points in this scenario, which is bearable; a 20% position loses 12 points, which would hit full-year returns.

This is why this report repeatedly emphasizes position sizing rather than price — tail risk cannot be managed through valuation, only through position sizing.

Conclusion

Risk conclusion: four can be quantified, one cannot, and one is a tail risk. The four that can be quantified all have tracking indicators, and bad news will surface two quarters in advance; the one that cannot be quantified (guarantees) dictates "no heavy position"; the tail scenario (three things happening at once) dictates a "position cap of 5%". This is not pessimism — it is calculating the worst case first, then deciding how much to bet.

The six risks are not independent. Customer concentration and financing guarantees are two sides of the same coin: because there are only six customers, Broadcom has no pricing power and can only solve their funding problems for them; and because it solves their funding problems, it binds its own fate even tighter. The correlation of risks is more dangerous than the number of risks.
