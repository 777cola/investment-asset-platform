Core thesis

Capital allocators fall into two camps: those who buy growth (paying a high price for growth) and those who buy cash flow (paying a low price for cash). Hock Tan is an extreme specimen of the latter.

His method is "buy companies whose customers can't walk away, cut costs, convert to subscriptions, pay down debt, then buy again." That method has run for twenty years, with only one exception in 2026 — he started lending money to customers.

His method: four steps, unchanged for two decades

Step one: find a company with high customer stickiness, slow growth and steady cash flow. LSI, Brocade, CA, Symantec and VMware all fit: customers install their products in on-premise data centers, and switching costs far exceed renewal costs.

Step two: cut costs. Halve the sales force, cut a third of the product lines, exit office leases. CA and Symantec cut their expense ratios by more than 15 percentage points within two years of acquisition.

Step three: change the business model. Convert perpetual licenses to subscriptions, and per-seat pricing to usage-based pricing. This step alienates customers the most but earns the most — VMware's revenue rose from RMB 21.5 billion to RMB 35 billion.

Step four: use cash flow to pay down debt, buy back stock and acquire the next company. This is a model that can loop indefinitely, provided you can "always find the next one."

Why this approach works: because it buys "lazy customers"

Enterprise software customers are lazy. A virtualization system stays in place for a decade because no one wants to touch it—touching it means downtime, retesting, and persuading the boss. That laziness is pricing power.

That is exactly what Chen Fuyang is after. He does not buy "the company with the best technology"; he buys "the company customers are too lazy to switch away from." This criterion follows the same logic as Buffett buying railroads and candy makers—not buying growth, but buying customer inertia.

But inertia has limits. Cloud-native lets new companies skip the virtualization layer from day one, and that is VMware's long-term risk. Chen Fuyang's response is to raise prices—collect the next decade of revenue now, while customers are still too lazy to switch. The strategy is financially correct, but it carries a cost in customer relationships.

The 2026 Exception: From Buying Assets to Buying Orders

The first six acquisitions bought assets: customers, products, cash flow. The 2026 deal is different—Broadcom is providing credit support for AI customers' chip purchases, with the size undisclosed.

The nature of this move is "trading balance sheet for orders." The upside is locking in USD 115 billion of FY27 AI revenue; the downside is assuming customer credit risk, and that risk sits off the financial statements.

Why would Hock Tan do this? Because customers want to buy but lack the money. Building an AI data center costs in the hundreds of billions, and among the six customers, apart from Google and Meta, the rest cannot sustain it on cash flow. If Broadcom wants to sell chips, it must help customers solve the funding problem.

This is the step from "capital allocator" to "bank." Many equipment vendors have walked this path—Lucent extended vendor financing to CLECs in 2000, and when the bubble burst, its receivables turned into bad debt.

Succession and governance: a one-man company

Hock Tan was born in 1952 and is 74 in 2026. The company has no public succession plan; the most likely internal candidate is semiconductor president Charlie Kawwas, but he has never independently run an M&A integration.

Part of this company's valuation rests on him: the judgment behind six acquisitions, the instinct for whether a customer "can walk away," and Wall Street's trust in him. Once he leaves, the valuation multiple drops a notch first, and only then does the market get around to judging whether the new CEO is up to the job.

The governance structure also deserves a mention: he serves as both chairman and CEO, and major acquisitions are driven almost entirely by him alone. That structure is an advantage in the "buy cash flow" phase and a hidden risk in the "take on off-balance-sheet risk" phase—no one can stop an invisible guarantee before the fact.

Conclusion

Management conclusion: first-class capital allocation, but a fading sense of boundaries. The first six acquisitions were textbook; the seventh (financing a customer) has no track record to reference. Watchpoint: whether he is willing to spell out the guarantee terms on the December 9 earnings call. If he is, boundaries remain; if not, he has not fully worked out the risk himself.

Note also that the "acquisition machine" is running low on fuel. In semiconductors, large targets worth buying are increasingly scarce, regulation is tightening (the 2018 CFIUS lesson), and USD 59.6 billion of debt caps the size of the next big deal. So after 2026, Broadcom's growth must come organically — which is why it has started financing customers: acquisitions can no longer buy growth, so it can only be swapped for sales terms.
