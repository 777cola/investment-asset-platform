Core view

Micron has four lines of defence: the cost curve, process technology, customer qualification and US capacity. Together they carry it through the worst years and let it earn heavily in the best, yet they cannot block the one thing that matters — falling DRAM prices. That is not Micron's problem; it is the nature of the business.

First line of defence: the cost curve — position matters more than width

The memory cost curve slopes upward from bottom-left to top-right: fabs on the left carry low costs, fabs on the right high costs. Price is set by "marginal capacity" — whether the highest-cost producer is willing to keep producing. Once price falls below a given producer's cost line, that producer cuts output and the price stops falling.

Micron sits "lowest of the three" on this curve, which means two things: when prices fall, it is the last to lose money; when prices rise, it earns the most (low cost, high gross margin). The value of this defense line is not "not losing money" — it is "losing later than others and earning earlier than others."

Cost leadership comes from three sources: process (bit density at 1γ/1δ), scale (15 fabs spreading R&D and procurement), and geography (subsidised power prices in Hiroshima, Japan; the supply-chain cluster in Taichung, Taiwan). None of the three is durable — process will be caught up, scale will be overtaken by Samsung, and geographic advantage will be offset by the high costs of new US fabs.

The cost defense line should therefore be judged as follows: it is a "relative advantage," not an "absolute moat." At the cycle bottom it determines who survives; at the cycle top it does not determine price.

Second line of defence: technology and yield — one generation ahead, one generation behind

Micron's position in HBM4 is delicate. It was not first to mass-produce—Samsung got there globally first in February 2026—but its 12-layer HBM4 ramp is twice as fast as the previous generation's 12-layer HBM3E, implying a steeper yield learning curve and more qualified units out of the same amount of time.

Why does yield matter so much? Because yield loss is the single largest component of HBM's cost structure. With 12-layer stacking, one defective layer scraps the entire die, so lifting yield from 60% to 80% equates to a 25% cost reduction. Hence Micron's comment that it "will strategically keep HBM share near its DRAM share"—not for want of share, but because taking share would cannibalise ordinary DRAM capacity, and ordinary DRAM is more profitable right now.

The technology moat has a shelf life of one product generation. HBM4 corresponds to Nvidia's Vera Rubin, HBM5 to the next-generation platform, and each transition requires re-certification and re-ramping of yield. A technology lead therefore buys a 12–18 month window, not a permanent barrier.

Third line of defence: customer certification, a six-month threshold

NVIDIA's HBM supplier certification takes 6–12 months: sample submission, testing, reliability validation, small-batch production, volume ramp. That cycle is a genuine barrier for latecomers—not a technology barrier but a time barrier.

The catch is that it blocks only new entrants, not incumbents. In June 2026, Jensen Huang said explicitly in Seoul that Samsung, SK hynix and Micron have all passed HBM4 certification and will supply Vera Rubin. All three passing means certification is no longer a differentiator—just a ticket to entry.

Certification's real value lies in switching costs: once a customer's design—the GPU's memory controller, the packaging scheme—has been validated around one supplier's HBM, switching suppliers requires revalidating the entire system at high cost. HBM switching costs therefore exceed those of ordinary DRAM—this is the most valuable stretch of Micron's moat, but it is only wide enough to last one product generation.

The fourth line of defence: US capacity as strategic asset and cost burden

Micron is the only one of the big three with advanced DRAM capacity on US soil. It will invest USD 250 billion through 2035 across New York, Idaho and Virginia, targeting 40% of DRAM production in the US; the CHIPS Act provides USD 6.165 billion in subsidies.

The value of this line of defense is strategic, not financial. The US government has clear demand for "domestic HBM capacity" — a critical component of AI infrastructure cannot rely entirely on Asia — and defense and government orders carry a domestic-procurement preference. These orders are stickier than commercial orders and less price-sensitive.

The cost is equally clear: building in the US costs 30%–50% more than in Asia, with higher labor and electricity prices. Management says scale and process nodes will offset this, but the investment community broadly expects US fabs to run gross margins 5–10 percentage points lower. This is a trade of margin for strategic position—it makes Micron harder to replace, but also less profitable.

Conclusion

Moat anatomy conclusion: four lines of defence — one relative (cost), one time-limited (technology), one qualification-based (certification), one strategic (US capacity). Together they explain why Micron will not become Elpida, but not why it deserves a 15x P/E. The real moat is not in the fab but in the contract: if the SCA long-term agreements can lock in 20% of revenue for five years and hold through a downcycle, that would be the first line of defence able to withstand the cycle. It has yet to be tested.
