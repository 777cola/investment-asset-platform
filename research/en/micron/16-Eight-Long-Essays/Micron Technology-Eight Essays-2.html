Core view

On the surface Micron sells DRAM and NAND chips; in essence it sells three things: bits (capacity), bandwidth (speed) and certainty (supply commitments). The first two are commodities priced by the market; the third is a new product it only began truly selling in 2026, and the only thing that could change its valuation.

Layer one: bits, a cost-leadership business

The bit is the unit of measurement for storage. When customers buy DRAM, they are essentially buying "how many GB" and "how many GB per second." The competitive logic at the bit level is extremely simple: for the same bits, whoever has the lowest cost wins. Cost is determined by three things—process node (1γ yields 20%–30% more die per wafer than 1β), scale (how much fixed cost is spread across a single wafer), and yield (what share of output is good product).

Micron's position at this level is "the lowest-cost player among the big three." Its 1γ/1δ process bit costs are lower than Samsung's and SK hynix's, partly because it built fabs later and has newer equipment, and partly because its fab sites in Hiroshima, Japan and Taichung, Taiwan run efficiently.Cost leadership means "earning more" in shortage years and "being the last to lose money" in surplus years—the latter is its real value.

The ceiling on this business is clear: bits are a commodity, and the long-term price trend is downward (historically, price per GB has fallen 15%–25% per year). So companies in the bit business must cut costs every year or be eliminated. This is a business of "constantly accelerating on the track," not one of "collecting rent while lying down."

Layer two: bandwidth, and why HBM is valuable

What AI training cards really lack is not capacity, but bandwidth. No matter how powerful a GPU's compute is, if memory cannot feed it data, that compute sits idle. HBM solves exactly this problem: stack DRAM dies, place them next to the GPU, and move data through an extremely wide interface, delivering bandwidth more than ten times that of standard DDR5.

So what HBM sells is not "capacity" but "bandwidth density." This change in product definition directly reshapes the competitive landscape: three or four companies can make standard DRAM, but only three can make HBM (Samsung, SK hynix, Micron). The difficulty in HBM lies in stacking, TSV, packaging and heat dissipation, not in the DRAM itself.

Value capture shifts accordingly: for the same bit capacity, HBM4 commands a unit price 5–8x that of DDR5 of the same capacity (industry estimate), at the cost of 2–3x higher wafer consumption. Netting it out, revenue per wafer is 2–3x that of standard DRAM, and profit per wafer is even higher. That is why all three are fighting for HBM share.

Micron's position in this layer: 18% share (2026Q2), ranked third, but its 12-high HBM4 ramp is twice as fast as the prior generation, with monthly capacity doubling to 10.0 million wafers by year-end. Its strategy is "don't chase first place on share, but lead on yield" — because HBM profit comes mainly from yield, not from share.

Layer three: certainty — what the SCA long-term contract is really selling

In June 2026, Micron did something no one in the memory industry had done: it signed five-year strategic customer agreements with 16 customers — take-or-pay, fixed volumes, price floors and ceilings. The 14 agreements at the floor price correspond to roughly USD 100 billion of revenue, and customers have already paid about USD 22 billion in cash deposits.

What these contracts sell is not bits, not bandwidth, but "certainty." Customers fear not high prices but being unable to get product — AI data center buildouts are scheduled by the quarter, and a one-week HBM supply interruption delays delivery of an entire rack. So customers are willing to pay upfront, lock in five years of volume and accept a price floor in exchange for a promise that "you will definitely supply me."

For Micron, the value of these contracts has three layers: the first is cash flow (USD 22 billion of interest-free working capital); the second is visibility (at least USD 100 billion of revenue over five years, a coverage ratio of about 20%); the third is pricing power (a price floor means prices will not fall below the cost line in a downturn).

But it also comes at a cost: a price ceiling means forgoing part of the upside. So the essence of the SCA is "trading upside elasticity for downside protection" — for a company that posted a net loss of USD 5.8 billion in FY23, this is a rational deal; for shareholders at the top of the cycle, it means "you earn less."

How much does each of the three business layers contribute

Rough calculation using FY26Q3 figures: the bit business (conventional DRAM + NAND) contributed about USD 30 billion in revenue and about USD 25 billion in gross profit; the bandwidth business (HBM4 revenue above USD 1 billion) is still small but growing fastest; the certainty business (SCA) does not appear as revenue in the current quarter's financials, only as contract liabilities of USD 22 billion.

This structure shows one thing: 90% of the money Micron earns today still comes from the oldest layer of the business — bits. HBM is the narrative, SCA is the option; what actually supports USD 41.4 billion in revenue and an 84.9% gross margin is price increases on conventional DRAM and NAND.

So judging this company requires looking beyond HBM share and long-term contract volume to "conventional DRAM pricing" — it is the main variable in current-period profit. HBM determines whether it is still alive after 2028; conventional DRAM determines how much it earns this year.

Conclusion

Business essence: Micron is a "bit company" trying to become a "bandwidth company + certainty company." The bit business contributes 90% of revenue, with cost leadership but a low ceiling; the bandwidth business (HBM) ranks third in share but leads in yield, and is the ticket for entry after 2028; the certainty business (SCA) has 20% coverage and is the only hope for a valuation re-rating. The core judgment in investing in it is: can the migration speed across these three business layers outpace the downturn speed of this cycle.
