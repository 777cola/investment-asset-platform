Core view

Most investors hold three biases about memory stocks, and each one makes you buy at the wrong time. The first: memory is a low-end cyclical commodity with no technology content. The second: cyclicals should be bought when the P/E is low. The third: AI will make cycles disappear. Of the three, the first is the most wrong, the second the most costly, and the third the most dangerous.

Bias 1: Memory has no technical content

This is the most widespread and most absurd bias. Memory's technical difficulty lies not in "design" but in "manufacturing" — packing more bits into the same area, running the same bits at lower power, achieving higher yield on the same wafer. Every generation is extreme engineering.

One concrete number: HBM4 requires drilling through 12 layers of DRAM chips, stacking them and connecting them with through-silicon vias (TSVs). Each layer is only tens of microns thick, stacking error must be controlled at the micron level, and not one of the 12 layers can be defective — any yield loss in a single layer scraps the entire 12-layer product. This is why HBM consumes 2–3x the capacity of ordinary DRAM, and why only three companies worldwide can make it.

Now the process node: Micron's 1γ process uses EUV lithography, a single wafer goes through more than 1,000 processing steps, and the cycle from wafer start to output takes 3–4 months. No more than five companies worldwide can do this. To say it has no technical content is to say TSMC has no technical content.

But two things must be distinguished here: high technical content does not equal stable profits. Memory's problem is that "a technology lead lasts only one to two generations," because rivals spend the same money and buy the same equipment. So it is a business where you must keep running just to stand still — technology is the ticket in, not the moat.

Bias 2: Buy when the PE is low

This is the most expensive bias of all. Cyclical stocks trade on their lowest P/E at the top and their highest at the bottom, because the denominator (EPS) swings far more than the numerator (share price). A 6.5x multiple usually marks the point of maximum frenzy in EPS.

Run the numbers on Micron's own data: FY23 was a loss, so P/E is meaningless; FY24 was marginally profitable, so P/E runs into the hundreds; FY26 consensus EPS of USD 73 puts the current price at 15x; FY27 consensus EPS of USD 166 puts the current price at 6.5x. Same company, same share price, and P/E goes from hundreds to 6.5x in just three years—that is not a change in valuation, it is the denominator climbing from trough to peak.

The right approach is the reverse: calculate P/E on "normalized earnings" and use the "position in the price cycle" to time entry. Normalized EPS is roughly USD 12–39 (depending on your assumption for mid-cycle gross margin), which puts the current price at 28–90x—not cheap at all.

The deeper cause of this bias: people treat "current profit" as "permanent profit." That is exactly where cyclical stocks are most dangerous—every signal it gives you at the top (low P/E, high growth, sell-side target upgrades, a coherent industry narrative) is a "buy" signal, and when these signals appear together, it is usually the top.

Bias 3: AI will make cycles disappear

This is the newest, most popular and most dangerous bias. Its logic: AI data centre demand for HBM is structural, HBM absorbs a large share of wafer capacity, so ordinary DRAM stays in chronic shortage and the cycle has been smoothed away. Half of that logic holds.

The half that holds: the demand centre has genuinely shifted up. HBM's share of DRAM wafer capacity has risen from under 5% to above 20% and may reach 35% in 2027, permanently carving out a block of ordinary DRAM supply. This is physical fact, not sentiment: the same wafer making HBM yields only one-third to one-half the bits of ordinary DRAM.

The half that does not: supply will shift up too. Micron's FY27 capex exceeds USD 40 billion, with Samsung and SK hynix expanding in parallel; this capacity comes online in a concentrated wave in 2028. The cycle does not disappear, it only changes shape — from "cyclical swings in demand" to "cyclical oversupply."

History offers a guide. In the 2018 round, some argued that "cloud computing makes the memory cycle disappear"; 2019 revenue then fell 23% and net profit 55%. In the 2022 round, the claim was that "5G + automotive electronics make the memory cycle disappear"; 2023 revenue halved and the company posted a net loss of RMB 5.8 billion. Every cycle brings a "this time is different" story, and every time the story is disproven within two years.

The reset framework: treat it as a "capacity option"

Clear away those three biases and a more practical framework emerges: a memory company is a "capacity option" — you pay the cost of building fabs and collect the spread between price and cost.

The defining feature of this option is that the cost side is fixed (fab construction, equipment, depreciation, labour) while the revenue side is uncertain (price set by supply and demand). That makes earnings elasticity enormous: a 60% price increase with costs unchanged lifts gross margin from 40% to 85%, while a 60% price decline drops it from 85% to 10%. Operating leverage of roughly 5-8x is the mathematical reason memory stocks swing so violently.

Through this lens, Micron's current position: the option's "strike price" (cost) is locked in at USD 67 billion of capex across FY26-FY27; the option's "underlying price" (DRAM prices) sits at a historic high; the option's "remaining term" (this shortage cycle) is roughly 4-6 quarters. Conclusion: this is an option whose underlying is at a high and whose remaining term is shrinking — the expected return on buying it is not high.

That does not make it worthless. If the SCA long-term agreements lock in 20% of revenue for five years, that effectively adds a "floor" to the option — less downside loss, but also less upside. This is a trade of elasticity for certainty, and the only genuine business-model innovation from Micron this cycle.

Conclusion

Three conclusions from the cognitive reset: ① Memory is a genuine technology business, but technology is a ticket to entry, not a moat; ② A low P/E often marks the top, so anchor on normalised earnings; ③ AI has lifted the central demand level, but supply will catch up too—cycles deform, they do not disappear. Through the "capacity option" lens, Micron is now an option whose underlying sits at a high while remaining tenor shortens—the case for holding it is not "cheap," it is "long-term contracts provide a floor."
