Part Four: The Financial Truth — From a RMB 5.8 billion loss to RMB 83 billion in profit — Micron Technology

Core view

Micron posted a net loss of USD 5.8 billion in FY23 as revenue halved to USD 15.5 billion; FY26 is expected to deliver net income of about USD 83 billion on revenue of about USD 129 billion. In three years, with the same fabs and the same engineers, profit swings from -5.8 billion to +83 billion. This is not operational improvement but operating leverage—grasp that, and you grasp everything about memory stocks.

The arithmetic of a three-year turnaround

Break the three years apart. FY23: revenue RMB 15.5 billion, gross margin below 10%, net loss RMB 5.8 billion, workforce cut by roughly 10%, capex trimmed to RMB 7.7 billion. FY24: revenue recovers to about RMB 25 billion, gross margin back to the low 20s, net profit marginal. FY25: revenue about RMB 37 billion, gross margin around 40%. FY26: revenue about RMB 129 billion, gross margin 84.9%, net profit about RMB 83 billion.

Revenue rises from RMB 15.5 billion to RMB 129 billion, 8.3x; net profit swings from -RMB 5.8 billion to +RMB 83 billion, "from negative to positive." The mathematics of that jump is called operating leverage: costs (depreciation, labor, R&D) are fixed in the short run, so every extra dollar of revenue falls almost entirely into gross profit.

Rough leverage math: FY23 revenue RMB 15.5 billion at about 8% gross margin gives gross profit of RMB 1.2 billion; FY26 revenue RMB 129 billion at 84.9% gives RMB 109.5 billion. Revenue grows RMB 113.5 billion and gross profit grows RMB 108.3 billion—an incremental gross margin of 95%. That is why memory stocks can multiply profit tenfold in two years, and also take it to zero in two years.

The cost: the lag between capex and depreciation

High leverage comes at a cost: you must keep building fabs. Capex is USD 27 billion in FY26, FY27 guidance exceeds USD 40 billion, and USD 250 billion will be invested in the US through 2035. This is not "growth investment" but an "entry fee to stay competitive" — don't invest and you fall behind on the next-generation process; invest and depreciation weighs on profits through the next downturn.

The depreciation lag is the most easily overlooked risk in memory stocks. Equipment is bought, installed and put into production, with depreciation spread over 5–7 years. The USD 67 billion invested in 2026–2027 will therefore generate USD 10–13 billion of depreciation each year in 2028–2032. If prices turn down in 2028, it is a double hit of "rising depreciation + falling prices."

The 2022–2023 script played out exactly this way: FY22 capex hit a record, FY23 revenue halved, depreciation remained, and the result was a net loss of USD 5.8 billion. History does not repeat itself, but it rhymes.

Cash flow quality: RMB 18.3 billion is real, but discounted

FY26Q3 operating cash flow was USD 25.39 billion, net capex USD 7.084 billion and free cash flow USD 18.304 billion, with cash and investments of USD 30.2 billion. These numbers are real; the money is on the books.

But the USD 18.3 billion of free cash flow rests on a capex ratio of 17%, versus 35%–40% in a normal year. Q4 capex is about USD 10 billion and FY27 full-year capex exceeds USD 40 billion—after normalizing the capex ratio, free cash flow is only about USD 6–8 billion per quarter, or USD 24–32 billion annualized. On the USD 1.22 trillion market cap, that implies 38–50x—hardly cheap.

One line item is easy to misread: USD 22 billion of customer deposits. On the balance sheet this sits in contract liabilities, not revenue and not cash flow. It signals that customers are locking in supply with real money, not that the company earned an extra USD 22 billion. Counting it in free cash flow is a classic definitional error.

Four accounting signals to watch
  • Inventory days: a leading indicator of a memory top. Slowing revenue growth alongside rising inventory is the earliest alarm (the company does not disclose days, so you have to calculate it from the balance sheet)
  • Receivables growth vs revenue growth: receivables growth persistently above revenue growth signals looser payment terms to buy revenue
  • Gross margin to capex ratio: gross margin peaking while capex keeps rising means "trading future depreciation for today's share"
  • GAAP vs Non-GAAP gap: currently 2.2%; if it widens above 10% in any quarter, it signals adjustments are being used to mask real costs

Of these four signals, the first two are demand-side and the last two are accounting-side. All four are green today — inventory grows in line with revenue, receivables match revenue, gross margin is still rising, and the GAAP gap is small.But cyclical stocks never turn red one at a time — they turn red all at once.

One detail you only notice after reading the full financials: Micron's assumed depreciation life has not changed in the past three years. That is normal in an expansion phase, but it also means that once utilization falls, depreciation will not fall with it — it becomes a direct deduction from gross margin.In the FY23 cycle, depreciation was the last straw that crushed gross margin — gross margin fell from 45% to below 10%, roughly half from price and half from higher depreciation per unit.

Conclusion

Financial reality: 95% marginal gross margin means profit can swing from -RMB 5.8 billion to +RMB 83 billion in two years, and back again in another two. The RMB 18.3 billion of free cash flow is real, but the capex ratio is understated; normalised, capex runs at RMB 24–32 billion a year, implying 38–50x. The RMB 22 billion of customer deposits is contract liability, not cash inflow. Conclusion: price the stock on normalised earnings, not current-period earnings.
