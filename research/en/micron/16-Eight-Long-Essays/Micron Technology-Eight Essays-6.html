Core view

Since taking over as CEO in May 2017, Sanjay Mehrotra has made three big bets in nine years: in 2018 he cut non-core businesses to focus on memory; in 2023 he invested counter-cyclically in HBM at the bottom of the cycle; and in 2026 he launched SCA long-term contracts. The first two bets paid off; the third is unproven.

First bet: focus (2018–2021)

When Mehrotra took over, Micron was a company that "did a bit of everything": DRAM, NAND, 3D XPoint, the consumer brand Crucial — it had even done solar. His first move was to contract: exit 3D XPoint in 2021, sell the Lehi fab in Utah to Texas Instruments, and concentrate resources on the two mainstream product lines, DRAM and NAND.

That decision was vindicated in 2026: precisely because no non-core business was dragging it down, DRAM price increases passed through 100% to profit. FY26Q3 gross margin was 84.9% and net margin 68% — impossible for a company that "does everything", whose gross margins get diluted by low-margin businesses.

This is also why Micron's "purity" deserves a premium: it was not pure by nature; management actively cut it into shape.

Second bet: HBM (2023)

2023 was the memory industry's worst year: revenue halved from USD 30.8 billion to USD 15.5 billion, net loss reached USD 5.8 billion, headcount fell by about 10% and capex was cut to USD 7.7 billion. Most companies responded by protecting cash flow and axing long-term investment.

Micron did not cut HBM. It kept investing in TSV lines, kept developing HBM3E and kept chasing Nvidia certification.Many analysts called the decision out of touch with reality at the time — the books were bleeding, yet the company was still funding a product with only a single-digit share.

The payoff: HBM3E won Nvidia certification in 2025, HBM4 12-layer entered mass production in sync with Vera Rubin in 2026, global HBM share reached 18% in Q2 2026 and HBM4 quarterly revenue exceeded USD 1 billion.This bet was the most valuable move of the cycle, and the hardest — it required expanding while everyone else was contracting.

Third bet: SCA long-term contract (2026)

In June 2026, Micron announced 16 strategic customer agreements: five-year terms, take-or-pay, committed volumes, price floors and ceilings. 14 of them, at the floor price, correspond to roughly USD 100 billion of revenue, and customers have paid about USD 22 billion in cash deposits.

This is the first time anyone in the memory industry has tried to change "prices set by the market" into "prices set by contract." Its significance is not how much extra money was made this quarter, but that it attempts to change the industry's pricing mechanism—if successful, Micron's revenue visibility would extend from 3 months to 5 years, and its valuation center would shift permanently higher.

The risks are equally clear: coverage is only 20%, the price floor has never been tested in reality, and take-or-pay contracts are usually renegotiated during industry downturns. So the current state of this bet is "right direction, unknown outcome."

Governance and succession: two variables to watch

On governance, Micron has a single-class share structure — one share, one vote — and no controlling family, which leaves it cleaner than many tech companies on this point. The CEO also serves as chairman, however, removing a layer of checks and balances that matters all the more for decisions of the "USD 250 billion investment" magnitude.

Succession is the more substantive issue. Mehrotra was born in 1959, turns 67 in 2026 and has served as CEO for nine years, with no succession plan disclosed. Three internal candidates are possible: CFO Mark Murphy (negotiator on capital structure and customer deposits), global operations head Manish Bhatia (capex executor) and chief business officer Sumit Sadana (SCA negotiator). All three helped build this playbook, but none has independently steered a full cycle.

One further detail: on August 21, 2026, Mehrotra sold 4.0 million shares under a 10b5-1 plan at an average price of USD 968.90, raising about USD 38.76 million and leaving him with 87.16 million shares (about 0.08%). The transaction is compliant, but the message it sends is that the CEO views USD 970 as a price at which to sell, not a price at which to buy.

Compensation structure: where he puts his money

Mehrotra's total FY25 compensation was about USD 30.94 million, of which base salary accounted for only USD 1.45 million, with stock awards making up almost all the remainder. That structure ties his wealth tightly to the share price — the good news is that "he is in the same boat as major shareholders"; the bad news is that "his optimal strategy is to get the share price as high as possible during his tenure."

This is a common flaw of all equity incentives, not a problem unique to Micron. But it explains one thing: why management is willing to give aggressive 2026 guidance such as "FY27 capex above USD 40 billion" — capacity expansion is a catalyst for the share price, while the cost of oversupply is borne by the next CEO.

Implication for investors: treat management guidance as "their intention," not an "objective forecast." The truly objective data are contract prices, inventory and market share, not management's optimism.

Conclusion

Management conclusion: three bets in 9 years, two right, one still unproven; capital-allocation discipline is sound; governance and succession carry two variables. Mehrotra ranks among the best CEOs of this memory cycle, but three facts together constitute risks that must be logged for the holding period: he is 67, has no named successor, and also serves as chairman. Score him 8/10: the deduction reflects structure, not ability.
