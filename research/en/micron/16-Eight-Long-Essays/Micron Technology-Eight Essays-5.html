Core view

Memory supply and demand in 2027 comes down to a simple subtraction: wafer supply +12%, half of which is absorbed by HBM, leaving the rest for conventional DRAM at 15% bit growth; demand growth 22%; a 7-percentage-point shortfall. The answer to this equation determines how much money Micron can still make in 2027, and when this cycle ends.

Supply and demand model: why HBM will "eat" conventional DRAM

The same 12-inch wafer yields 1 unit of bits when making standard DDR5, but only 0.3–0.5 units when making HBM4—because HBM dies require TSV drilling, must leave stacking gaps, and suffer greater yield loss. So for every 1 percentage point of wafer capacity HBM takes up, standard DRAM supply falls by 1 percentage point.

In 2026, HBM will occupy about 20% of DRAM wafer capacity, and by 2027 it could reach 35% (industry estimate). This means that even if total wafer capacity grows 12%, bit supply for standard DRAM can only grow about 15%—while combined demand growth from AI servers, phones, PCs and autos reaches 22%. The 7-percentage-point gap is what supports prices.

But this model has a flaw: it assumes demand growth stays constant. If AI capex slows in the second half of 2027 (for example, through model efficiency gains or greater specialization of inference chips), demand growth will drop from 22% to below 10%, and the gap instantly becomes a surplus. That is why "AI capex" is the most important external variable in this cycle—more important than Micron's own capacity.

Three-player game: who is expanding capacity, who is holding the line

Samsung: DRAM share 39.4%, HBM 33%, first globally to mass-produce HBM4 in February 2026. Its strategy is "do it all" — memory, foundry and devices together, using foundry and device cash flow to subsidize memory. This cycle it grabbed HBM share from 21% to 33% in a single quarter, proving its catch-up ability is stronger than the market expected.

SK hynix: HBM share 50%, the biggest winner this cycle. Its strategy is "hold the HBM high ground," supplying 18 billion Gb of HBM in 2026 and 24 billion Gb in 2027. But its risk is "over-concentration" — HBM accounts for too high a share of its revenue, and once HBM prices fall back it has no ordinary DRAM buffer.

Micron: DRAM 23.3%, HBM 18%, third in both. Its strategy is "yield leadership + long-term contract lock-in" — not chasing the No. 1 share, but achieving the best yield on 12-layer HBM4 and locking in 20% of revenue for five years via SCA long-term contracts. This is a strategy that "admits it is No. 3" — pragmatic but not sexy.

CXMT: share 9.5%, revenue up 99.3% QoQ, STAR Market IPO already approved. Its strategy is "trade localization orders for share," focusing on ordinary DRAM (DDR4/DDR5). It poses no threat to HBM, but it will cap the price ceiling for ordinary DRAM.

How the cycle ends: three possible scenarios

Scenario one (highest probability): Supply catches up. All three players expand capacity simultaneously in 2027, with capacity released in a concentrated wave in 2028; contract prices turn negative year-on-year by end-2027, and gross margins fall back to 40%–50% in 2028. This is the historical playbook, staged in both 2019 and 2023.

Scenario two: Demand air pocket. AI capex slows in the second half of 2027, cloud providers discover compute oversupply, server DRAM orders are halved, and prices fall 40% within 6.0 months. This scenario is faster and harsher, but less likely than scenario one, because AI capex is still being revised upward.

Scenario three: Technology substitution. HBM is partly displaced by new memory architectures (such as 3D DRAM, or deeper integration of HBM with logic chips), eroding Micron's capacity advantage. This is a slow variable spanning more than 5 years and does not affect the 2027 call.

What the three scenarios share: they all play out in 2027–2028, and the share price tops 3–6.0 months before the scenario is confirmed. That is why "hold but do not add" is the most reasonable action now—you cannot predict the scenario, but you can refrain from raising the bet at this point.

A structural call: a higher central range, unchanged volatility

Setting aside the cycle, there is one genuine structural change this time: AI has turned memory from a "cyclical commodity" into "a critical component of AI infrastructure." The effect of this change: the demand center has shifted up (HBM's share rising from 5% to 35%), industry concentration has increased (only three players can make HBM), and customer relationships have become long-term (SCA long-term agreements).

But what it has not changed: supply elasticity remains high. All three can double capacity within 18 months, provided there is capex. So the result of the "shifted-up center" is not "the cycle disappearing" but "a higher cycle floor and a more frenzied top."

In one sentence: over the past decade Micron's revenue swung between USD 15 billion and USD 31 billion; over the next decade it may swing between USD 40 billion and USD 150 billion. The center has indeed moved up, but the amplitude of the swings is larger—not good news for long-term holders, good news for cycle traders.

Conclusion

Industry conclusion: the 7-percentage-point gap in 2027 is real, but it is "supply arithmetic", not "demand faith". All three are expanding capacity: CXMT is chasing mainstream DRAM, Samsung is chasing HBM. The likely script is "supply catches up by end-2027, prices decline in 2028". Structural shifts (AI demand, industry concentration, long-term contracts) have raised the midpoint but have not eliminated volatility — over the next decade Micron's revenue range will be wider than in the past, not narrower.
