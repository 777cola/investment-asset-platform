## Micron has no pricing power, only a place in the queue

Memory makers sit downstream of the equipment and materials suppliers. ASML is the sole supplier of EUV lithography tools, without which advanced DRAM nodes below 1γ are unbuildable; etch, deposition and metrology are split among Applied Materials, Lam Research and KLA. These vendors run gross margins of 48%–60% year in, year out, above the mid-cycle gross margins of memory makers (35%–45%)—in normal years, equipment vendors therefore out-earn memory makers.

Micron has no bargaining power over them. Capex is USD 27 billion in FY26 and above USD 40 billion in FY27, most of it paid to equipment vendors at prices and on delivery schedules they set. As the industry adds capacity in 2026, equipment lead times stretch to 12–18 months, which directly means Micron "cannot expand fast even if it wants to"—a short-term positive (supply cannot come up) and a medium-term negative (everyone's capacity arriving together means 2028 oversupply).

## Midstream: HBM is the only place where prices can be raised

The midstream splits into two layers. The first is wafer fabrication — turning silicon wafers into DRAM dies. Products at this layer are commodities: DDR5 is DDR5, and customers shop on price. The second is HBM stacking — punching through 12 layers of DRAM chips with TSVs, stacking them and packaging them alongside the GPU. This layer carries a certification barrier: Nvidia validates for 6–12 months, and once passed, a product generation is locked in.

The value gap is enormous. For the same bit capacity, HBM4 commands a unit price 5–8x that of DDR5 (industry estimate), at the cost of 2–3x wafer consumption. This is why Micron says it will "strategically keep HBM share near its DRAM share": making more HBM cannibalizes supply of ordinary DRAM, and ordinary DRAM is also in shortage, with both sides profitable. The optimal solution is balance, not betting everything on HBM.

Micron's HBM share is 18% (Counterpoint 2026Q2), below SK hynix's 50% and Samsung's 33%. Its advantage lies not in share but in "ramping 12-layer HBM4 twice as fast as the prior generation" — a fast yield ramp means more qualified output from the same wafers.

## Downstream customers earn more than it does, giving them leverage to push prices down

Micron's downstream demand splits into four categories: AI servers (NVIDIA, AMD, the four major cloud providers), phones and PCs (Apple, Samsung, Xiaomi, Lenovo, Dell), automotive (Tier 1 suppliers and automakers), and industrial and embedded. In the 2026Q3 revenue mix, CDBU contributed 11.5 billion, CMBU 13.77 billion, MCBU 11.52 billion and AEBU 4.63 billion—with data center and cloud combined at roughly 61%, already the absolute mainstay.

Downstream customers' gross margins generally run above Micron's mid-cycle level—NVIDIA at 70%–75%, Apple at 45%—which means that in a normal year customers have both the ability and the incentive to squeeze memory prices; only in shortage years does bargaining power flip. 2026 is that year: Micron can raise ASP 60% because customers cannot get supply, not because it negotiates well.

To judge when bargaining power reverses, one indicator suffices: customers' inventory days. When cloud providers' server DRAM inventory goes from 4 weeks back to 8 weeks, price pressure begins. Public information now shows Samsung and SK hynix finished-goods inventory at under 10 days, with customer-side inventory also tight, so bargaining power still sits with the seller.

## Cycle position: late-cycle boom, neither peak nor mid-cycle

The memory cycle broadly divides into four phases: recovery (prices stop falling, utilisation rebounds), boom (prices accelerate, gross margin hits a record high), peak (gains narrow, inventory rebuilds) and downturn (price growth turns negative, gross margin collapses). Three indicators tell us where we are now.

- Gross margin: 84.9%, a historical high, with Q4 guidance of 86%, only +1pct QoQ — a classic late-boom signature (still rising, but no longer able to rise)

- Price gains: +90%–95% YoY in 2026Q1, +10%–15% QoQ in Q4 — gains narrowing quarter by quarter, but still positive growth

- Capacity: Micron's HBM monthly capacity rising from 40–50 thousand wafers to 100 thousand wafers by year-end, FY27 capex above USD 40 billion — expansion has started, but supply will only be released in concentrated form in 2028

Taken together, the three indicators show: the peak has not arrived (prices still rising, inventory still low), but we are already in the second half of the boom (gross margin can no longer rise, expansion has begun). On historical rhythm, that leaves roughly 2–4 quarters before QoQ price growth turns negative, and roughly 0–2 quarters before the share price peaks — because share prices always lead.
