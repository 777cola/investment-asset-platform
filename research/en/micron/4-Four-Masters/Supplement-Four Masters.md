## Duan Yongping: Are you buying the company, or the price?

Duan Yongping would first separate two things. At the company level, Micron is doing very well: mass production of 12-layer HBM4, design wins across Nvidia's entire Vera Rubin lineup, 16 take-or-pay long-term contracts locked in through 2030, and USD 6.165 billion in subsidies for US domestic capacity. These are "doing the right things." But at the price level, **buying Micron is essentially a bet on 2027 DRAM contract prices, and that falls outside his circle of competence**—even management says it cannot see clearly when supply will catch up with demand.

He would say, "I understand this, but I don't want to bet on it." What he understands is the business; what he doesn't want to bet on is the price. If he had to buy, the position would be very small, because the downside in this kind of business is not 20%—it is 60%. In the 2022 cycle, Micron fell more than 50% from its peak and revenue was halved. He would be more likely to buy the upstream shovel-sellers (ASML, Applied Materials) or downstream memory users rather than memory itself.

In one sentence, his attitude: **A good company is not the same as a good stock. Price is what you pay, value is what you get, and this company's value is anchored to a contract price that changes every quarter.**

## Buffett: first-rate management, second-rate business

Buffett has never been polite about the memory industry: products are undifferentiated, customers care only about price and lead times, and the money you make must go straight back into capacity or you are eliminated in the next cycle. Micron's FY26 capex of USD 27 billion, FY27 guidance above USD 40 billion, and USD 250 billion of US investment through 2035—**that money is not optional; it is the entry fee**. At the 2011 Berkshire annual meeting he said that in businesses like this, "there are crocodiles in the moat."

But he would give management credit: Mehrotra bucked the trend and bet on HBM in 2023, when the industry was at its worst—a textbook case of "investing in capacity when others are fearful"—and the bet paid off; the 2026 push for SCA long-term contracts is an attempt to turn a commodity business into a contract business, and the direction is right. Net cash at the top of the cycle, no share dilution in five years, and a 30% dividend raise—the capital-allocation discipline is there.

So his verdict would be: good management can take a second-rate business to its limit, but cannot make it a first-rate business. **You must buy only at a low enough price, and 6.5x is not low, because that E is fake**—in his words, "cheapness computed from the wrong denominator is the most expensive cheapness of all."

## Munger: What would make it fall 60%?

Munger does not ask how much it can rise; he first calculates how much it can fall. This cycle starts from a higher base than any in history: gross margin 84.9%, net margin 68%, single-quarter net profit USD 28.2 billion. The higher the starting point, the greater the absolute room for reversion. In the 2022 cycle, gross margin fell from 45% to below 10%; this cycle, a fall from 85% to 25% is the same story, amplified.

Three triggers, each of which can be written into the calendar: **① All three expand capacity simultaneously in 2027** — Micron's FY27 capital expenditure exceeds USD 40 billion, and Samsung and SK hynix are also expanding; if supply growth exceeds 20%, prices reverse immediately; **② AI capital expenditure peaks in 2028** — the current spending plans of North America's four major cloud providers assume "compute is always insufficient"; once model efficiency improves or inference chips become specialized, server DRAM demand will fall off a cliff; **③ Long-term contracts are renegotiated** — take-or-pay contracts typically soften during downturns through "extensions, specification changes, and revised price ranges"; the 2008 shipping long-term agreements and the 2020 shale gas long-term agreements are both precedents.

If any one of these occurs, quarterly profit of USD 28.2 billion becomes USD 5 billion within two years. He would add: **"Buying cyclical stocks at the top of the cycle is the mistake smart people make most often," because at the top the numbers look best, the logic is smoothest, and sell-side target prices are highest.** Late-September target prices range from USD 1,300 to 2,000; that distribution is itself a sentiment indicator.

## Li Lu: AI is a new demand curve, but it only guarantees three years

Li Lu would first concede that this cycle really is different. A buyer that does not count cost has appeared on the demand side: AI data centers. HBM's share of DRAM wafer capacity has risen from under 5% to over 20%, and may reach 35% in 2027—the same wafer making HBM yields only one-third to one-half the bits of ordinary DRAM, **which shifts the entire supply curve to the left**. This is structural, not sentiment.

But he would press on the endgame: is AI training demand one-off buildout or sustained consumption? If model efficiency improves sharply in 2028 and inference chips become specialized (ASIC share rises), incremental memory demand growth will fall; if China's ChangXin lifts its 9.5% share to 20%, the price center will drop another notch. In his framework, **only demand that can last twenty years deserves a long-term holding; Micron today qualifies only as "can last three years."**

He would also offer a positive observation: the SCA long-term contracts are the company's first attempt to turn a cyclical business into a business with contracted cash flow. If five years from now SCA covers more than 50% of revenue and the price floor has actually been enforced through one downcycle, Micron's valuation center will be permanently raised—**that is the evidence he is willing to wait for, not the current 6.5x.**

## Where we differ: how our view diverges from the sell side

The first disagreement is the denominator's year. UBS reiterated USD 1,625 on 24 September, using 8x 2029 expected earnings; BofA's USD 1,550 also rests on post-2028 profits. **The sell side is pricing "two years past the cycle top"; we are pricing "the next reversion"**—a methodological difference, not an arithmetic error on either side.

The second is the definition of "cheap." At 6.5x 2027 expected earnings it is among the lowest in the S&P 500, but a cyclical's PE is always lowest at the top. Our view: the multiple reflects the market's doubt about E, not a market mistake.

The third is SCA's execution. The market assumes long-term contracts can flatten the cycle; we assume they can only shave a third off the downside (coverage of roughly 20%, and the price floor has never been tested in practice). The fourth is ChangXin: TrendForce shows 9.5% share in 2026Q2, up 99.3% QoQ, its IPO has cleared the listing review, and the market broadly treats it as "not a threat"—we see it as the most underappreciated variable for 2027–2028.

## Odds: zero expected return, 63% downside

Weighting the three scenarios by probability: 0.25×400 + 0.50×1,120 + 0.25×1,620 = USD 1,065, 1.6% below the current price of 1,082.28. **The neutral scenario itself offers only +3% upside**—meaning even if the shortage persists through 2027 and EPS reaches USD 166, you earn just 3%, while the bear case loses 63%.

This payoff structure is not "the market being wrong"; it is "the market discounting an unsustainable E." Only two paths make this trade attractive: **the price falls below USD 800** (implying +40% in the neutral scenario and -50% in the bear case, the payoff only turns positive then); or **fundamentals push down the probability of the bear case**—SCA coverage above half, a price floor written into the financials, and three consecutive years of positive free cash flow.

For those already holding, the conclusion differs: if the cost basis is in the USD 400–600 range, selling would incur over 30% capital gains tax and forfeit the "long-term contract re-rating" option, so continuing to hold is reasonable. The final rating is therefore "Hold, do not add," not "Sell."
