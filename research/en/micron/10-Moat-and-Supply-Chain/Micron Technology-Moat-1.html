Core thesis

Score the moat across six components: cost 8, technology 8, scale 7, licenses 6, switching costs 5, brand 4, network effects 2 — a weighted 6.4/10. That score says one thing: Micron's moat is an "engineering moat" — it wins on cost and technology, not on customer stickiness and ecosystem lock-in.

An engineering moat only guarantees "last one to lose money" in a downturn, not "no losses." Samsung is the most dangerous rival: it holds 39.4% DRAM share and 33% HBM share, plus a foundry business that can subsidise memory — it is the only competitor that "can afford to bleed alongside you by not making money in memory."

Position in the supply chain (1): the "transformer" of the chain

The memory maker sits in the semiconductor chain like a transformer: capex from upstream equipment flows in, passes through its fabs to become standardised bits, and is output to downstream compute and end devices. What defines this position is that it carries the largest capital risk in the entire chain, yet earns only mid-cycle average returns.

Micron specifically buys EUV from ASML, deposition equipment from Applied Materials and wafers from Shin-Etsu — all of which earn higher mid-cycle gross margins than Micron. It sells to Nvidia, Apple and Xiaomi, whose gross margins also exceed Micron's mid-cycle level (Nvidia 70%+, Apple 45%). Squeezed in the middle, the memory maker's gross margin in a normal year is 35%–45%, one of the thinnest links in the chain. Its 84.9% in 2026 came from shortage, not from position.

Strategically, this position means Micron must keep investing in the most advanced process nodes or its cost curve shifts to the right; but investing does not guarantee profit, because price is set by supply and demand. Its capex is therefore not "growth investment" but "defensive investment."

Value-chain position (II): US capacity is a unique asset and a cost burden

Micron is the only one of the big three headquartered in the US, and the only one with advanced DRAM capacity on US soil. It will invest USD 250 billion across New York, Idaho and Virginia through 2035, targeting 40% of DRAM production in the US; the CHIPS Act provided USD 6.165 billion in subsidies.

The value of this asset lies in two places. First, a policy moat: the US government has a strategic need for domestic memory capacity, particularly as HBM becomes a critical component of AI infrastructure. Second, customer demand: US cloud providers and defense customers prefer localized supply chains, and these orders are stickier than ordinary commercial orders.

The costs are real too. US construction, labor and electricity costs all exceed Asia's, raising unit costs. Management says it will offset this through scale and process technology, but the investment community broadly expects US fabs to run gross margins 5–10 percentage points below Asian fabs. This is a trade of gross margin for strategic position, not a purely commercial decision.

The true width of the moat: survival, not pricing power

Taken together, the six scores tell a clear story: cost scores 8 and technology scores 8, enough to survive a price war; brand scores 4 and network scores 2, gaps that leave it with no pricing power. Pricing power comes from customers being unable to leave you—yet Micron's customers can switch suppliers at any time, provided the specs match and the price is lower.

The only sticky element is HBM qualification: Nvidia runs a 6–12 month validation, and once passed it locks in a single product generation (HBM4 corresponds to Vera Rubin). But qualification protects only one generation—HBM5 must be won again—and in June 2026 Jensen Huang confirmed that Samsung, SK hynix and Micron have all passed HBM4 qualification. The "exclusive qualification" dividend is over; what follows is a contest of yield and capacity.

The moat is therefore best framed as: "a company with engineering capability and no pricing power." Its excess profit comes from the supply-demand gap, not from a moat. The moat's role is to keep it alive and still profitable (on thin margins) once the gap closes, so that it does not go bankrupt like Elpida.

The most dangerous rival is Samsung, because it can ignore the math

Samsung is the only rival that can "keep going even when memory loses money." It holds a 39.4% DRAM share and a 33% HBM share (up from 21% in a single quarter), plus the world's largest foundry business and a consumer electronics business to cross-subsidise. In February 2026 it became the first globally to mass-produce HBM4, and in June it passed Nvidia qualification — catching up faster than anyone expected.

If Samsung lifts its HBM share to 40% in 2027, Micron's 18% gets squeezed below 15%. In years of tight supply that is not a problem (everyone eats), but in years of loose supply it means a price war — and in a price war, the largest and most diversified player withstands it best.

The second-most-dangerous rival is ChangXin. It held a 9.5% share in 2026Q2 with revenue up 99.3% QoQ, and its STAR Market IPO has cleared review. Its threat is not in HBM but in the price ceiling for commodity DRAM — Micron's MCBU (mobile and client) posted Q3 revenue of USD 11.5 billion, exactly ChangXin's main target.

SK hynix is instead the "least dangerous rival": its 50% HBM share has already captured the biggest gains of this cycle, and what comes next is defending share, not taking it; it is also overly dependent on HBM, with limited elasticity in commodity DRAM, so direct clashes with Micron are fewer.

Conclusion

Conclusion: moat 6.4/10, an "engineering moat". Cost and technology score 8, which keeps it alive even in the worst years; brand and network score 2–4, showing it has no pricing power and that all excess profit comes from the supply-demand gap. The most dangerous rival is Samsung—the only player able to subsidise memory with foundry and device businesses, and one that took 12 points of HBM share in a single quarter. The moat explains "why Micron will not die", not "why it deserves 15x".
