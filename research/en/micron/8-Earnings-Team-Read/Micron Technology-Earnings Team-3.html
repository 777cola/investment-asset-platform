Core view

Munger does not look at the aggregate; he looks at the segments. Gross margins across all four business units jumped simultaneously to 79%–87%, a phenomenon never seen before in the history of the memory industry—it shows the shortage is not localized but spans every product category; it also shows this quarter's profit is not "operational improvement" but "pricing power." Pricing power disappears; operational improvement does not.

What does all four segments jumping to 87% mean?

CDBU 38%→87%, MCBU 24%→87%, AEBU 26%→79%, with CMBU also at a high level. Four customer groups with entirely different businesses (data center, handset/PC, automotive/industrial) saw their gross margins converge into the same range in the same quarter — the only explanation is that "everything is priced for shortage."

A Munger-style inference: if this were operational improvement, the four segments' gross margins should have risen disparately, each driven by its own efficiency gains; instead they moved in lockstep, indicating a single external variable — price. External variables reverse, so this gross margin cannot be extrapolated.

Inventory and receivables: the early warning signs of a downturn hide here

Revenue rose 74% quarter on quarter, so receivables and inventory are bound to swell — basic accounting. The real metric to watch is inventory days. Micron does not disclose an explicit figure in its filings, but judging by working-capital absorption (net income of RMB 28.24 billion versus operating cash flow of RMB 25.39 billion, a gap of RMB 2.85 billion), the drag is not yet severe.

Munger would caution that the leading indicators of every memory-cycle peak are a rebound in inventory days plus narrowing contract-price increases; when both appear together, the financials still look good while the stock has already begun to fall. The first condition has not yet appeared; the second (narrowing price increases) has half appeared.

Capex: viewing "dividends" as "borrowing to expand capacity"

The dividend this quarter is only USD 0.15 per share (about USD 170 million in total), negligible against net profit of USD 28.2 billion. Where did the money go? FY26 capex of USD 27 billion, FY27 above USD 40 billion, and USD 250 billion of US investment before 2035.

Munger would translate it this way: "It takes shareholders' money to build fabs, betting someone will still be buying in 2028." That bet may be right, but it is not "shareholder return" — it is "reinvestment." The key to assessing management is not the payout ratio but the return on this reinvestment — and that return depends on 2028 DRAM prices, which nobody knows.

Conclusion

Munger-style conclusion: the pretty numbers mask a simple fact—all four segments earning 87% gross margins at once rests on shortage, not skill. The shortage ends in 2027–2028. Holding until then is fine, but do not treat this quarter's gross margin as the new normal.
