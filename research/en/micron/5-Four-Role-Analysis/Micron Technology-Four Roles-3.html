Core view

Munger asked only two questions about an industry: who takes the money, and what would reverse it. In 2026Q2 global DRAM industry revenue reached USD 154.73 billion, up 59.5% QoQ, split four ways: Samsung 39.4%, SK hynix 24.9%, Micron 23.3%, ChangXin 9.5%. The money went to the three oligopolists; the reversal switch sits on the supply side, and that switch flips in 2027.

How large is the supply gap: the arithmetic of 7 percentage points

SK hynix's CEO estimated in July that wafer supply would grow 12% in 2027, half of it absorbed by HBM, leaving conventional DRAM bit growth ex-HBM at just 15% against demand growth of 22%—a 7-percentage-point gap. Meanwhile, finished-goods inventory at Samsung and SK hynix stands below 10 days, the lowest on record.

The estimate carries high credibility: it comes from the sell side (the suppliers) themselves, and three independent phenomena corroborate its direction—HBM monthly capacity expansion (Micron from 40,000–50,000 wafers/month at end-2025 to 100,000 wafers/month by end-2026), narrowing quarter-on-quarter contract price gains (2026Q1 up 90%–95% year on year, Q4 down to just 10%–15% quarter on quarter), and customers signing five-year long-term agreements to lock in supply.

But the Munger-style retort is: suppliers always underestimate their own capacity to expand. Adding up the three players' 2027 plans—Micron FY27 capex above USD 40 billion, Samsung and SK hynix expanding in tandem—meaningful supply growth is "expected to materialize in 2028." The flip side of that statement: 2028 could bring a concentrated release.

Why the three oligopolists cannot hold prices: three lessons from history

The memory industry's structure looks beautiful: three players hold 88.7% of DRAM share and could in theory control output to defend prices like OPEC. But historically this cartel has never succeeded, for three reasons.

First, the product is homogeneous. DDR5 is DDR5; customers buy to spec and go with whoever quotes lower, with no brand premium. Second, cost curves cross. Today's cost leadership comes from process node, and tomorrow's process leadership may change hands — Samsung grabbed HBM share from 21% to 33% in 2026 by hard-charging on process and capacity. Third, the prisoner's dilemma. Any one player that adds 10% more capacity during a shortage can capture an extra year of excess profit; if all three think this way, they all bring capacity online in 2028 at once.

Munger would say: this is not an "oligopoly with a moat" but an "oligopoly forced to cooperate." Cooperation works during shortages and collapses the moment there is oversupply. So the industry's good times have a boundary — and that boundary is not demand, but the three players' capex discipline.
China variable: CXMT's 9.5% is no small number

CXMT 2026Q2 revenue USD 14.62 billion, +99.3% QoQ, share rising from 7.6% to 9.5%, fourth globally. The STAR Market IPO passed review on 27 May and has entered the registration process. The market widely treats it as "a low-end DDR4 supplement" — that is an underestimate.

Its threat is not in HBM (currently no share), but in "capping the price ceiling for ordinary DRAM." Chinese phone makers, PC makers and server makers have policy incentives for domestic procurement; every 1 point of share CXMT gains is 1 point less of import demand, and ordinary DRAM is precisely the core market of Micron's MCBU (Mobile and Client, Q3 revenue of 11.5 billion).

Conversely, CXMT is also a "buffer" for this round of price increases — if the three major suppliers undersupply in 2027, CXMT's incremental output can fill part of the gap, and prices will not rise to absurd levels. It is a "long-term negative, short-term neutral" variable for Micron; after the IPO, public financials will make tracking far cheaper.

Conclusion

Industry view: the shortage is real, but only 4–6 quarters remain. Excluding HBM, the DRAM bit supply gap in 2027 is about 7 percentage points, inventory covers less than 10 days, and contract price increases, though narrowing, remain positive—the upcycle should last at least through the first half of 2027. The reversal switch is 2027 capex at the three major suppliers, all of which are stepping up spending this year, so supply will hit the market in force in 2028. The Munger-style conclusion: this is an industry that is "great now, terrible in two years," and share prices typically peak 6–9 months before the industry turns.
