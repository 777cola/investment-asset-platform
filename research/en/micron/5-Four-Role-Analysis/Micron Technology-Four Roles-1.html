Core view

Strip Micron's business down to its foundations and it comes down to one sentence: use ever more expensive equipment to make ever cheaper (over the long term) memory chips, and sell them to customers who are ever more short of supply (right now). In 2026 this business is almost absurdly good — an 84.9% gross margin and a 68% net margin — but that is not a victory for the business model; it is a victory for the supply-demand gap. Duan Yongping would ask three questions: where does the money come from, can it last, and will it still be here in ten years.

Where the money comes from: three markups on a single wafer

The first layer of markup comes from process. On the same 12-inch wafer, the 1γ node yields 20%–30% more bits than 1β, diluting cost — this is Micron's own capability, and the reason its bit cost is the lowest of the three. The second layer comes from product mix: the same bits made into HBM4 12-high command several times the unit price of ordinary DDR5, at the cost of three times the wafer consumption. The third layer comes from timing: in 2026, DRAM contract prices rose 90%–95% year on year in the first quarter, and HBM rose more than 200% — this has nothing to do with Micron's operations, it is purely shortage.

Seventy percent of the money made in 2026 comes from the third layer. That is also why gross margin could jump from 39% in FY25Q3 to 84.9% — not because Micron got stronger, but because customers got more anxious. The Duan Yongping-style question is: if the third layer disappears, how much gross margin can the first and second layers still support? The answer is 35%–45%, and that is the true "underlying color" of this business.
Can it last: the substance of the three contracts

The SCA long-term agreements are the most substantive change to this company's business model: 16 contracts, five-year terms, take-or-pay, fixed volumes, price floors and ceilings; 14 at the floor price correspond to roughly USD 100 billion of revenue, and customers have already paid about USD 22 billion in cash deposits. In business terms: customers pay first, then lock in volume, and accept a price floor — effectively writing "I guarantee to buy" into the contract.

But its limits are clear. First, the USD 100 billion is "floor price × minimum volume," which spreads to USD 20 billion a year over five years, while FY26 single-quarter revenue alone is USD 41.4 billion — coverage of only about 20%. Second, the price ceiling means Micron does not capture the full upside when prices rise. Third, take-or-pay contracts are typically renegotiated in a downturn (spec changes, deferrals, new price ranges). So it is a peak-shaving tool, not a cycle-flattening one.

Duan Yongping would rate this move highly — it sells uncertainty to customers in exchange for cash and visibility. But he would also caution: a contract can lock in volume, but it cannot lock in whether customers will still need that much next year.

Will it still be here in ten years: demand is real, the position may not be

The good news on the demand side is structural. AI data centers' hunger for bandwidth is not a cyclical preference but architecture-driven: HBM's share of DRAM wafer capacity has risen from under 5% to over 20%, and could reach 35% by 2027, permanently carving out a chunk of ordinary DRAM supply. As long as AI capex does not collapse, the midpoint of memory shortages will sit higher than in the past decade.

The bad news is positioning. A decade from now Micron will most likely still be around, but it will most likely still be "one of three," not "the only one." Samsung has a foundry business that can subsidize memory, SK hynix leads in HBM, and ChangXin has used domestic-substitution orders to lift its China market share from 7.6% to 9.5%, with single-quarter revenue up 99.3% QoQ; its IPO has cleared the review.The endgame of this business is "an oligopoly plus a steadily growing Chinese variable," not "winner takes all."

So the ten-year conclusion is: the company will survive, and survive well; but its profits will not ratchet up year after year like a software company's — they will oscillate around a mid-cycle level. In Duan Yongping's words:"This is a good business, but not a business you can buy and hold without watching."

Conclusion

Business judgment: the business itself scores 7, 2026 profitability scores 10, sustainability scores 5. Seventy percent of Micron's money comes from shortage premiums, thirty percent from process and mix; the SCA long-term contracts turn 20% of revenue into contracted revenue, a real step forward, but coverage is not yet enough to change its cyclical nature. A decade from now it will still be one of the big three, but even then it will still be earning cyclical money. You pay for it as a "cyclical stock," not as a "contracted-revenue stock."
