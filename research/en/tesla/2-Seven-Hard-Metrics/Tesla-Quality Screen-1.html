Quality Screen — Tesla

Tesla (TSLA) | 02 · Quality Screen
Research date 2026/07/22  ·  Price $374.01 (-1.30%)  ·  Market cap Approximately USD 1.48 trillion  ·  PE(TTM) — (Margins too thin; PE no longer meaningful)

Core call: this is not a company that can be filtered using "first-class company" standards

Of seven hard metrics, Tesla passes only two: debt-to-asset ratio of about 40%, and operating cash flow at 4.2x net profit. The five it fails are ROE, gross margin, net margin, free cash flow and growth quality—each points to the same fact: its profits are thin, and getting thinner.

These metrics are designed to screen out the weak, filtering out companies that earn hard money. Tesla is exactly the type that gets filtered out: revenue of USD 28.2 billion, net profit of USD 1.1 billion, net margin of 3.9%—a company with hundreds of billions in revenue retains less than 4 points of net margin. Yet its market cap is USD 1.48 trillion—the metrics say it is not first-rate, the market says it is one of a kind, and both are right, because they are not measuring the same thing.

All three profit metrics fail: these are manufacturing readings, not those of a tech company

Gross margin 16.8%, net margin 3.9%, ROE about 5.2%—put the three numbers together and they paint a typical heavy-asset manufacturer, not the AI company the market talks about. For comparison: CATL's gross margin is around 25%, BYD 18%, Toyota 20% with a 10% net margin, Apple 46%. Tesla's gross margin is lower than Toyota's, and its net margin is half of Toyota's.

More critical is the direction. Gross margin was still 20.1% in Q4 2025 and 21.1% in Q1 2026 (including a one-off boost from tariffs and credits), then fell straight to 16.8% in Q2. A 4.3 percentage point drop in one quarter is not all price war—regulatory credits fell from USD 439 million to USD 146 million, and that line is almost pure profit.

The credits line is not coming back. The federal USD 7,500 EV subsidy expired on 30 September 2025, and federal law simultaneously eliminated penalties for automakers missing fuel-economy standards, removing the incentive to buy credits. Credit revenue peaked at USD 2.76 billion in 2024, was USD 1.99 billion in 2025, and only USD 146 million in Q2 2026. Over the past two years, 1–2 points of gross margin were propped up by credits; that cushion is now gone.

Two of the passing metrics are also weak: one is accounting structure, the other is scale effect

A debt-to-asset ratio of about 40% is manageable because Tesla's liabilities are mainly accounts payable and deferred revenue, with very little interest-bearing debt, and it holds USD 43.5 billion in cash and short-term investments. This is a real advantage: in a year of surging capital expenditure, it does not have to answer to the banks. But full-year 2026 capex guidance exceeds USD 25 billion, and the company has lined up a borrowing facility of up to USD 30 billion — its net cash position will most likely end this year.

Operating cash flow is 4.2x net profit, which looks impressive but is really propped up by depreciation and amortization. Quarterly D&A runs at around USD 1.5 billion, and together with share-based compensation, warranty accruals and other non-cash items, it inflates USD 1.1 billion of net profit into USD 4.7 billion of cash flow. A high ratio here does not signal good earnings quality; it only signals that this is a very asset-heavy company.

Taken together, the two points say one thing: Tesla will not die from a lack of money, but it will be expensive because it does not make money. The difference between these two things matters a great deal for valuation.

Are the five non-passing items fatal: fatal for DCF, not applicable to option pricing

Run a DCF on it and all five tests fail, each on a hard point: a 3.9% net margin means any price war wipes profit out to zero; negative free cash flow means expansion is funded by financing; poor growth quality means revenue growth creates no shareholder value. On a DCF basis, the RMB 1.48 trillion market cap requires free cash flow of more than USD 70 billion by 2035 — the combined total of Toyota, BYD and CATL today.

But the market is not buying a DCF. Tesla buyers are pricing three things: software revenue from FSD, capacity revenue from Robotaxi, and robot revenue from Optimus. None of the three sits in the income statement today, so none of the seven hard metrics can measure them. This is where "negative screening" breaks down on an option-type company — it can tell you this is not a first-tier manufacturer, but not what the option is worth.

The practical approach is to separate the two: use the seven tests to judge what the physical "auto + energy storage" business is worth (conclusion: RMB 500–600 billion), and use probability weighting to judge what the option is worth (conclusion: the current price has already prepaid most of the probability of success). Only adding the two together fully explains the USD 374 figure.

Conclusion

Conclusion: it passes two of seven criteria, so by the "first-class company" standard it fails. The failure is not operational but structural: low gross margin, asset-heavy, price wars, fading subsidies. The right way to use this scorecard is to value the physical business—at 20–25x PE, the auto business is worth RMB 450 billion, already a high multiple for manufacturing. The remaining RMB 910 billion must be judged by a different method, and that method offers no margin of safety.
