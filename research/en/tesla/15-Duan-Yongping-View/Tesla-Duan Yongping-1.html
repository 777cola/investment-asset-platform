Core call: a good car, an unintelligible business, and an even less intelligible price

Ask the six questions the way Duan Yongping would and the answers line up: this is a great company, but not one I can buy at USD 374. He asks only three things of a company — is it a good business, is the culture right, is the price cheap — and the first two can pass halfway while the third fails.

He would probably say: "I'm bullish on it, but I'm not buying it — the two aren't contradictory." Being bullish is a judgment; not buying is discipline. Judgment can be wrong; discipline cannot be broken.

Q: Is this a good business?

Answer: half good, half grind. Selling cars is a tough business: 16.8% gross margin, 1.4% operating margin, a production line that costs several billion dollars, new tooling for every model change, and prices that fall every year. Even the world's best automakers earn long-run net margins of just 8%. Energy storage is a good business — long-term contracts, predictable cash flow, clear scale effects — but gross margin is only 20.4% because the cells are bought in.

Robots and transport capacity are not yet businesses, only possibilities. Possibilities can be very big things, but they cannot be counted as profit.

So I often say: don't touch a business you don't understand. Not because it's bad, but because I can't put a price on it. Anything I can't price can go to the moon and it's none of my concern.

Q: Will Tesla still be around in ten years?

Answer: yes, most likely still there, and possibly bigger. Whether it will be number one, I am not sure.

A decade ago, who could have imagined BYD selling 4 million vehicles a year, or Xiaomi building a car in three years? In autos, a technology lead is worth only one product cycle. Tesla's 4680 cells, gigacasting and end-to-end autonomous driving could all be standard equipment three years from now.

When I bought Apple, I asked: will people still use the iPhone in ten years? The answer was yes. When I bought Moutai, I asked: will people still drink Moutai in ten years? The answer was yes. For Tesla the question is whether people will still buy its cars in ten years — and I cannot answer that, because there are too many substitutes.

Q: Can you understand it?

Answer: I understand the car business; I do not understand the valuation. These are two separate matters.

On the car side, the math is straightforward: 48.0 vehicles, RMB 28.2 billion revenue, RMB 4.75 billion gross profit, RMB 400 million operating profit. Every line is in the financials. Energy storage is equally tractable: 13.5GWh, RMB 3.14 billion revenue, 20.4% gross margin.

But of the RMB 1.48 trillion market cap, RMB 910 billion has no corresponding revenue. Robotaxi discloses no trip volumes, FSD discloses no dollar amounts, Optimus has not shipped. To believe these three together are worth RMB 910 billion is not investing — it is faith.

I have nothing against faith; I have believed in things myself. But faith should not be practiced under the name of margin of safety.

Q: At what price would you buy?

A: Below USD 300 is negotiable; below USD 250 I would look seriously. This is not a guess — it is calculated.

The auto business sells 1.9 million vehicles in 2027 at USD 950 net profit per vehicle; at 25x that is worth USD 450 billion. Energy storage adds USD 80 billion and net cash USD 40 billion. Together the three total USD 570 billion, or USD 144 per share. The rest is optionality.

At USD 300, optionality accounts for 55% — the market is paying half the price for "possibility." I accept that, because what I am buying is "half certainty, half possibility." At USD 374, optionality is 61% — possibility costs more than certainty, and that I do not accept.

Some will ask, what if it rises to USD 600? Then I do not buy. Missing out is no disgrace; buying wrong is.

Q: What are you most afraid of?

Answer: My biggest fear is that this becomes "the right company, the wrong stock." I have seen companies improve while shareholder returns deteriorate far too many times.

Railroads were right, aviation was right, solar was right, yet long-term returns for the vast majority of participants were close to zero. Because all the money they earned was ploughed back into capacity. Tesla's 2026 capex of USD 25 billion and free cash flow of -USD 1.09 billion mark the start of this pattern.

The second fear is the person. The company leans too heavily on one man. He is genuinely a genius, but geniuses have their own lives, their own interests, their own pile of companies. What you are buying is his attention, and his attention is finite.

The third fear is that "the story keeps getting bigger." Today it is Robotaxi, tomorrow it is Optimus, the day after it is Mars. The bigger the story, the more evidence it requires; the less evidence there is, the more expensive the price.

Q: Growth is this fast—why aren't you tempted?

A: Because I ran the numbers — and the growth was bought.Revenue +26%, net profit -5%, free cash flow -RMB 1.09 billion: put those three lines together and the story tells itself.

At my age, "growth rates" don't move me easily.Growth can be bought; profit cannot.If a company has to spend two times the capex to buy one time the revenue growth, that growth is worth nothing to shareholders.

Of course, I could be wrong.If Optimus really works out, if Robotaxi really scales, then looking back ten years from now there will be no difference between USD 300 and USD 374.But investing is not about buying "possible"; it is about buying "certain." I can only earn the money I understand.

Conclusion

Conclusion: a good car, a good company, not a good stock. Duan Yongping would rate this company very highly and then not buy a single share — the price does not embed the margin of safety he insists on. His discipline is simple: if you don't understand it, don't touch it; if it's expensive, don't buy it; if you miss it, don't regret it. Applied to Tesla, that discipline would cost him a potentially big opportunity — and spare him a potential halving. He would probably say: worth it.
