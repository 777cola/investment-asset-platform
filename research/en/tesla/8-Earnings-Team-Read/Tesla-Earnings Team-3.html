Earnings Deep Dive (Munger) — Tesla

Valuation: Tesla (TSLA) Look at the accounting first, then incentives, then common sense
Research date 2026/07/22  ·  Price $374.01 (-1.30%)  ·  Market cap Approximately USD 1.48 trillion  ·  PE(TTM) — (Margins too thin; PE no longer meaningful)

Core view

Of the RMB 1.114 billion net profit, RMB 590 million came from other income. The 47% rise in operating expenses includes share-based compensation, and the RMB 25 billion in capex was funded by debt. Together these three form a loop: accounting and narrative holding up the valuation.
Accounting quality: three red flags
First, other income of RMB 590 million. This comprises Bitcoin fair value changes and FX gains. Bitcoin rose this quarter, flattering profit; when it falls, profit suffers. A car company's income statement containing a line item that swings with cryptocurrency is itself evidence that the core business is not strong enough. Second, the reflexivity of equity incentives. SBC tied to the 2025 compensation plan is booked in operating expenses and rises with the share price. The higher the share price, the higher the expense and the lower the profit—a self-offsetting structure. The market awards a high valuation, the company books more expense, and few notice this loop. Third, warranty provisions. RMB 240 million in the energy segment cut gross margin from 39.5% to 20.4%. Provisions are a good thing (they show accounting prudence), but consecutive provisions point to a product problem. Watch next quarter to see whether they recur.
Incentive mechanism: what USD 1 trillion buys

In November 2025, shareholders approved a pay package worth up to USD 1 trillion with more than 75% of the vote. The award is tied to 12 tiers of market-cap and operating milestones, the highest of which requires a market cap of USD 8.5 trillion. That figure exceeds Apple, Microsoft and Nvidia combined today.

On incentive design, it got one thing right: it aligns management and shareholder long-term interests, and pays out only when milestones are met. On corporate governance, it got another thing wrong: to retain one person, it staked all shareholders' dilution rights.

Munger would say: "Show me the incentive and I'll show you the outcome." When the CEO's pay depends on market cap and robot shipments, it should come as no surprise that the company shifts resources from cars to robots. This is not betrayal; it is incentives at work.

Common-sense check: three numbers together don't add up

Revenue of RMB 28.2 billion, operating profit of RMB 400 million, market cap of RMB 1.48 trillion. Put those three numbers on one page and anyone who has never studied finance will feel uneasy: how does a company earning RMB 4 billion a year deserve to be worth RMB 1.48 trillion?

There is only one answer: the market believes it will earn RMB 50 billion, then RMB 100 billion, next year, the year after and the year after that. That is not impossible — Amazon and Nvidia both walked this path. But theirs was "profits grow first, valuation catches up later"; Tesla's is "valuation gets there first, profits are nowhere in sight."

What Munger hated most was exactly this order. He said: "If you can't explain in simple language why it's worth the money, then it isn't worth the money." Making the bull case for Tesla requires three scenarios, five assumptions and a ten-year horizon — which is itself the answer.

Conclusion

Munger-style conclusion: accounting flaws, misaligned incentives and a business that defies common sense. Taken together, these three do not mean "dig deeper" — they mean "stay away." I may miss it, but missing it won't break me; buying wrong will.
