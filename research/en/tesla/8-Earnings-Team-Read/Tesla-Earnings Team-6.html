Earnings review (publishable version) — Tesla

Selling 480,000 vehicles generates just USD 400 million in profit

Core view

Tesla just posted record revenue and its worst income statement in three years. That both happened at once is no accident.
I. 480,000 vehicles and USD 400 million

Tesla delivered 480,126 vehicles in 2026Q2, up 25% YoY and its best second quarter on record. Revenue reached USD 28.236 billion, up 26% YoY, the fastest growth in three years. On those two lines alone, this is a flawless earnings report.

Now the next three lines: operating profit of USD 398 million, down 57% YoY; operating margin of 1.4% versus 4.1% a year earlier; free cash flow of -USD 1.092 billion, negative for the first time in two years. On quarterly revenue of USD 28.2 billion, the core business earned less than USD 400 million — a ratio that would be dangerous in any industry.

Where did the money go? Operating expenses were USD 4.353 billion, up 47% YoY. The company cites four drivers: AI R&D, investment in Optimus and Robotaxi, stock-based compensation tied to the CEO pay package, and G&A. The first three are not one-off items; they will rise alongside capex.

II. Gross-margin safety net: regulatory credits gone

Many investors see a 16.8% gross margin, down just 0.4pp YoY, and call it fine. The trap: regulatory credit revenue fell from USD 439 million a year ago to USD 146 million.

These credits are emissions allowances that combustion-vehicle makers buy from Tesla to meet standards. They cost Tesla almost nothing, so every dollar of revenue is pure gross profit. Credit revenue was USD 2.76 billion in 2024, USD 1.99 billion in 2025 and just USD 146 million in 2026Q2. The reason is simple: the US federal USD 7,500 EV subsidy expired on 30 September 2025 and penalties for automakers missing targets were scrapped at the same time, so the incentive to buy credits vanished.

Strip out credits and Tesla's underlying gross margin this quarter is 16.2%, versus 15.2% a year ago. It actually improved YoY, evidence that 4680 cells, single-piece casting and supply-chain localisation are genuinely cutting costs. But part of the savings went to consumers and part went into AI.

That is where the company stands now: able to cut costs, unable to raise prices.

III. The USD 25 billion bet

Capex this quarter was USD 5.789 billion, up 142% YoY. Management guided full-year capex above USD 25 billion, three times the USD 8.5 billion spent in 2025, and is also arranging a borrowing facility of up to USD 30 billion.

Where is the money going? AI training compute in Texas (Cortex 1/2 combined exceed 200MW), the Cybercab line (employee trial operations began in July), the first-generation Optimus mass-production line at Fremont (the Model S/X line has been dismantled), a captive semiconductor fab in Austin, and energy-storage capacity in California, Shanghai and Texas. Not a single dollar is going into "selling more cars next year."

This is a wholesale pivot: from a car-selling company to a company selling transport capacity and robots. If it works, today's choice will be written up as foresight; if not, the USD 25 billion is sunk cost.

IV. What does USD 374 buy?

By segment: auto sells 1.9 million vehicles in 2027 at USD 950 net profit per vehicle and 25x P/E, worth USD 450 billion; energy storage USD 80 billion; net cash USD 40 billion. Together that totals USD 570 billion, or USD 144 per share.

Against a current market cap of USD 1.48 trillion and a share price of USD 374, the USD 910 billion gap—USD 230 per share—buys options on Robotaxi, Optimus and FSD.

Options are not a bad thing. The problem is that options offer no margin of safety—only room for imagination. When 61% of market cap rests on businesses that have yet to generate revenue, you are not buying a company, you are buying a lottery ticket. Lottery tickets can be bought, but only at lottery-ticket prices.

So the conclusion: a good company, not a good price. Watch, do not chase. Wait until the stock is below USD 300, or until FSD revenue first appears as a separate line in the financials, before discussing a purchase.
