Earnings Deep Dive (Li Lu) — Tesla

Look at the capital cycle, not single-quarter profit

Core view

Li Lu reads financial reports by first asking where capital is invested, for how long, and at what return. Tesla invested RMB 5.789 billion this quarter, and the return will not be known for three to five years—the earliest and most dangerous stage of the capital cycle.
Capital cycle: from asset-light to asset-heavy

In 2019–2023 the Tesla story was "asset-light, software-defined vehicles, 25% gross margin." In 2026 it is "AI infrastructure, robot factories, self-built fabs," with capex jumping from USD 8.5 billion to USD 25 billion. That is a fundamental shift in business model, not a quarterly fluctuation.

In Li Lu's capital cycle framework, the key question is: once this round of investment ends, will the industry be better or worse? If every player is investing in AI and robotics and three years from now capacity is in surplus and returns are declining, today's investment is value destruction.

What sets Tesla apart is that it is investing in things others cannot: training compute, proprietary chips, vehicle manufacturing and an energy network — the only company in the world doing all four at once. That uniqueness is a moat, and also a black hole for capital efficiency — each one requires its own production line.

Unit economics: Robotaxi's numbers must add up before anyone believes the story

The earnings report disclosed no unit economics for Robotaxi — only a single line: "covers seven major U.S. metropolitan areas." To assess this business, you have to build the model yourself: a Cybercab costs about USD 30,000, runs 20 trips a day at USD 8 per trip for USD 58,000 of annual revenue; after electricity, insurance, maintenance and platform commission, gross margin is 40%, or USD 23,000 a year, implying a two-year payback.

The model hinges on three variables: utilization (can it hit 20 trips/day), regulation (can it run without a safety driver) and accident costs (insurance and litigation). Waymo has operated for eight years across 10 cities at 500,000 trips a week and has yet to disclose per-vehicle profitability — which is itself the answer: if the unit economics were good, it would have disclosed them long ago.

Robotaxi should therefore be valued as an option, not a business: assign a probability of success, then multiply by the value if successful. The market does the opposite — it starts with the value if successful, then assumes a probability of 100%.

Long-term view: three trends, one winner's position

Electrification, autonomous driving and embodied intelligence each represent a civilisation-scale efficiency leap, and Tesla holds real assets in all three — a combination no other company globally can match. Li Lu would acknowledge this, and would also acknowledge it is not enough to justify the current price.

Because the long-term view has a second half: the correctness of a trend will be seen by all capital, which ultimately erases excess profit. Solar, aviation and railways were all correct trends, yet long-run returns were close to zero because capex swallowed all the profit.

Tesla has only one path to avoid this fate: build something others cannot replicate before capital flattens the trend. The supercharger network, in-house chips and fleet data are candidates, but none has yet established pricing power.

Conclusion

Li Lu-style conclusion: right direction, good position, early timing. Early means you pay today in full for something that has not happened yet, while carrying the risk of a capital downcycle. The right move is to wait—for unit economics to be disclosed, for capex to peak, for valuation to return to a range where probabilities can be priced.
