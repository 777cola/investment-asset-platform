Tesla posts its best sales ever, then earns just USD 400 million

After the close on July 22, 2026, Tesla reported second-quarter results. The next day, its shares fell 4% after hours.

Many found it hard to understand: 480,000 vehicles delivered, USD 28.2 billion in revenue, both record highs — so why the drop?

The answer sits on the third line of the income statement: operating profit of USD 398 million, down 57% year on year.

1. 480,000 vehicles, just USD 400 million of profit

Start with the numbers. Tesla sold 480,126 vehicles in the second quarter, up 25% year on year, its best second quarter on record; revenue was USD 28.236 billion, up 26%, the fastest in three years.

But look further down: gross profit of USD 4.751 billion, gross margin 16.8%, down 0.4 percentage points from a year earlier; operating expenses of USD 4.353 billion, up 47% year on year; operating profit down to just USD 398 million, an operating margin of 1.4% — versus 4.1% a year earlier.

What does 1.4% mean? A company with USD 28.2 billion of revenue earned less than USD 400 million from its core business. In any industry, that ratio is dangerous.

Where did the money go? The company itself cites four reasons: AI R&D, investment in Optimus and Robotaxi, equity incentives tied to the CEO pay package, and general and administrative expenses. The first three are not one-offs; they will rise alongside capital expenditure.

2. Net profit fell only 5% because of a windfall

Many saw "net profit attributable to shareholders of USD 1.114 billion, down only 5%" and thought it was fine. But there was a detail this quarter: other income of USD 590 million, which includes fair-value changes on bitcoin holdings and foreign-exchange gains.

In other words, half of this quarter's net profit did not come from selling cars.

Non-GAAP net profit better reflects the real trend: USD 1.153 billion, down 17% year on year. The market expected USD 0.51–0.53 per share; the actual figure was just USD 0.33 — a 35% gap. That is the real reason the shares fell after hours.

3. The naked truth on gross margin: regulatory credits are gone

Another number few mention: regulatory credit revenue of USD 146 million, versus USD 439 million a year earlier and USD 2.76 billion for all of 2024.

What are credits? They are "emissions allowances" that combustion-car makers buy from Tesla to meet standards. They cost almost nothing, every sale is pure profit, and they flow straight into gross margin.

Why are they gone? On September 30, 2025, the US federal USD 7,500 EV subsidy expired; at the same time, the law eliminated fines on automakers that miss standards — the incentive to buy credits vanished overnight.

Last year credits contributed about 2 percentage points to gross margin; this year just 0.5. Strip out credits and Tesla's true gross margin this quarter was 16.2%, versus 15.2% a year earlier.

Interestingly, it actually improved year on year. That shows 4680 cells, single-piece casting and supply-chain localization really are cutting costs. But part of the savings went to consumers and part went into AI.

That is where the company stands now: able to cut costs, unable to raise prices.

4. A USD 25 billion bet

Capital expenditure this quarter was USD 5.789 billion, up 142% year on year. Full-year guidance is more than USD 25 billion — versus USD 8.5 billion for all of 2025. It has also lined up a borrowing facility of up to USD 30 billion.

Where is the money going? AI training compute in Texas, the Cybercab line, the first-generation Optimus production line at Fremont (the Model S/X line has already been dismantled), the in-house semiconductor fab in Austin, and energy storage capacity across three sites.

Not a single dollar is invested in "selling more cars next year."

This is a complete pivot: from a "car-selling company" to a "company selling capacity and robots." If it works, today's choice will be written up as foresight; if not, the USD 25 billion is sunk cost.

V. At USD 374, what are you buying

Break the company down: the auto business, selling 1.9 million vehicles in 2027 at USD 950 net profit per vehicle, at 25x, is worth USD 450 billion; energy storage USD 80 billion; net cash on the books USD 40 billion.The three together come to USD 570 billion, or USD 144 per share.

The current market cap is USD 1.48 trillion, the share price USD 374.The USD 910 billion gap, USD 230 per share, buys the options on Robotaxi, Optimus and FSD.

Options are not a bad thing. The problem is that options do not offer a margin of safety—they only offer room for imagination. When 61% of the market cap rests on businesses that have yet to generate revenue, you are not buying a company, you are buying a lottery ticket.

Lottery tickets can be bought, but only at lottery-ticket prices.

VI. So what is the conclusion

A good company, not a good price.

This company deserves respect: it took the EV from a toy to the mainstream, rolled out 70,000 superchargers, and turned a company on the brink of bankruptcy into the world's most valuable automaker.But respecting a company and buying its stock at this price are two entirely different things.

Our conclusion: watch, do not chase.

Two conditions, and we revisit if either is met: the share price falls back below USD 300 (at which point the options share drops to 55%), or FSD and Robotaxi revenue appears as a separate line in the financials for the first time.

Until then, let the financials speak, not the story.
