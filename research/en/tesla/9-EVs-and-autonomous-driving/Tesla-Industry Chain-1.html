Industry Chain — Tesla

Core call: vehicle assembly is the least valuable link in the chain, and Tesla sits on exactly that link

Profit distribution along the EV supply chain is barbell-shaped: fat at both ends, thin in the middle. Upstream lithium salts earn 40% gross margins at cycle peaks, downstream intelligent-driving chips run 60–75%, and automakers in the middle get only 15–20%. Tesla sits in the thinnest link yet carries the fattest valuation.

Vertical integration is its one exception: batteries, chips, energy storage and charging networks all done in-house, retaining part of the upstream and midstream profit inside the company. This is where it outpaces peers, and why its capex is twice theirs. Supply-chain position caps its gross margin, and gross margin determines what the physical business is worth.

Upstream: lithium prices flatline, resources no longer the bottleneck

Lithium carbonate spiked to RMB 600,000/tonne in 2022; by mid-2026 it trades in the RMB 80,000–100,000/tonne range. Lithium is no longer scarce, and upstream bargaining power has gone with it. Good news for automakers, as battery costs have fallen; bad news for lithium miners, whose gross margins have slid from 40% to around 15%.

Tesla's response in this link is to build its own: a Texas lithium refinery planned at 30GWh equivalent, 10GWh of cathode material and 7GWh of LFP cells in Nevada. The point of this capacity is not cost savings but supply security — ensuring it is not choked off when supply chains tighten. Returns on capital are modest, however, and a considerable portion of 2026 capex of USD 25 billion goes into these "defensive assets."

Midstream: batteries are the real profit pool, chips the real toll booth

Batteries account for 30–40% of a vehicle's bill of materials. CATL runs gross margins of 20–25% and holds more than a third of global share. For every car an automaker sells, it pays a toll to the battery maker. Tesla's response has been to build its own 4680 lines (over 40GWh in Texas) and diversify sourcing across Panasonic, LG and CATL, driving battery cost down to among the lowest in the industry.

The real toll booth, though, is intelligent-driving chips. Nvidia's automotive Orin/Thor carries gross margins of 60–75%, and the profit of this one company exceeds that of most automakers. Tesla's response is to develop AI4/AI5 in-house and build its own semiconductor fab in Austin—on the 2026 earnings call, Musk put it bluntly: a supply bottleneck will emerge in the next three to four years, so it must build its own.

This is the most interesting move in the supply chain: if the in-house chip succeeds, Tesla goes from "the one paying the toll" to "the one collecting it"; if it fails, a large chunk of the USD 25 billion in capex goes down the drain.

Automakers sit furthest downstream, with the thinnest margins and the most capacity—capacity that is not yet open for business

Vehicle manufacturing carries a 15–20% gross margin, the lowest in the chain, for a simple reason: overcapacity, product homogenisation and consumers who compete on price alone. BYD sold 1.809 million vehicles in the first half of 2026, down 15.7% YoY, and even the leader is cutting prices to defend share — evidence that this segment has no pricing power.

Mobility (Robotaxi) is theoretically the fattest pool: a car running 20 trips a day at USD 10 each generates USD 70,000 of annual revenue, and after depreciation, electricity, insurance and platform commission, gross profit per vehicle can exceed USD 20,000. But that is theory — Waymo has run for eight years without disclosing per-vehicle profitability data.

Energy storage is the other high-value segment, and Tesla sits in the global first tier: 13.5GWh per quarter, with 40GWh in California + 40GWh in Shanghai + 50GWh planned in Texas. The moat lies in system integration and Autobidder software rather than cells, which are purchased, so gross margin only reaches the low 20s.

Tesla's position in the chain: the only player spanning all four segments

Map the chain and Tesla is the only company occupying four positions at once: batteries (4680/LFP), chips (AI4/AI5/Dojo), vehicles and the energy network (Supercharging plus storage). That vertical integration is a moat when capacity is tight and a burden when demand is weak—because every link needs its own production line, fixed costs run far above peers.

Against BYD: BYD is vertically integrated too, but around batteries, motors, electronic controls and vehicles—no chips, no transport capacity. Its strength is cost; its weakness is moving the brand upmarket. Tesla's integration is deeper; BYD's is broader.

Against Waymo: Waymo does transport capacity only, not manufacturing, leaning on Chrysler/Jaguar for contract production. Its asset base is light, expansion is slow and per-vehicle cost is high, but it leads by at least two years in the hardest link—driverless operations. Which route wins remains unclear in 2026.

Cycle position: mid-downcycle for vehicle OEMs, first year of autonomous-driving commercialisation, mid-stage growth for energy storage
Automotive: mid-downturn. Global capacity utilisation is below 70%, China's price war has yet to clear, European tariff barriers are rising and US subsidies are phasing out (the federal USD 7,500 credit expires on 2025/09/30). All three markets face headwinds simultaneously, with no systemic improvement in sight before 2027. Autonomous driving: first year of commercialisation. Waymo covers 10 cities at 50.0 million rides per week and targets 1 million by year-end; Tesla covers 7 metro areas, with no safety driver in some cities. The 0-to-1 is done; the 1-to-10 has only just begun, but the valuation prices in 1-to-100. Energy storage: mid-growth. Global installation growth has slowed from 100% to 30–40%; absolute incremental volume remains large and the price war has already begun. This is the healthiest of the three segments and the only Tesla business that can be valued on a growth-stock framework.

Three segments, three cycles, yet Tesla's valuation uses only one cycle—this is its biggest pricing mismatch.

Conclusion

Conclusion: first-tier position in the value chain, third-tier position in profit. Tesla spans four links and has the deepest technical depth in the chain, but both of its main battlegrounds—vehicles and energy storage—are mired in price wars, and one (smart driving) has yet to open for business. The result: revenue can grow, but gross margin can only hold at 16–17%, and the ceiling for the physical business sits at USD 500–600 billion. Breaking through that ceiling depends on two links—chips and compute capacity—delivering, and both are currently works in progress.
