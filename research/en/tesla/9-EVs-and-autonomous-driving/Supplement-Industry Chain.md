## Upstream: lithium prices flatline, resources no longer the bottleneck

In 2022 lithium carbonate once spiked to RMB 600,000/tonne; by mid-2026 it oscillates in the RMB 80,000–100,000/tonne range. Lithium is no longer a scarce resource, and upstream bargaining power has disappeared with it. That is good news for automakers — battery costs have come down; it is bad news for lithium miners — gross margins have fallen from 40% to around 15%.

Tesla's play in this link is to build its own: the Texas lithium refinery planned at 30GWh equivalent, 10GWh of cathode material, and 7GWh of LFP cells in Nevada. The significance of this capacity is not saving money but securing supply — so it is not choked off when supply chains tighten. But returns on capital are not high, and a considerable portion of 2026 capex of USD 25 billion goes into these "defensive assets."

## Midstream: batteries are the real profit pool, chips are the real toll booth

Batteries account for 30–40% of a vehicle's bill of materials. CATL runs gross margins of 20–25% and holds more than a third of global share. For every car an automaker sells, it pays a toll to the battery maker. Tesla's response: build its own 4680 lines (over 40GWh in Texas) and diversify sourcing (Panasonic, LG, CATL), driving battery cost down to among the lowest in the industry.

The real toll booth is intelligent-driving chips. Nvidia's automotive Orin/Thor carries gross margins of 60–75%, and the profit of this one company exceeds that of most automakers. Tesla's response is to develop AI4/AI5 in-house and build its own semiconductor fab in Austin—on the 2026 earnings call, Musk put it bluntly: a supply bottleneck will emerge in the next three to four years, so it must build its own.

This is the most interesting move in the supply chain: if the in-house chip succeeds, Tesla goes from "the one paying the toll" to "the one collecting it"; if it fails, a large chunk of the USD 25 billion in capex goes down the drain.

## Downstream: vehicle makers thinnest, capacity fattest but not yet open for business

Vehicle manufacturing carries a gross margin of 15–20%, the lowest in the chain. The reason is simple: overcapacity, product homogenization, and consumers who look only at price. BYD sold 1.809 million vehicles in the first half of 2026, down 15.7% year on year, and even the leader is cutting prices to defend share, which shows this segment has no pricing power.

The mobility segment (Robotaxi) is theoretically the fattest: a car running 20 trips a day at USD 10 each generates USD 70,000 of annual revenue, and after depreciation, electricity, insurance and platform commission, gross profit per vehicle can exceed USD 20,000. But that is theoretical — Waymo has run for eight years and has yet to disclose per-vehicle profitability data.

Energy storage is another high-value segment, and Tesla sits in the global first tier here: 13.5GWh per quarter, with 40GWh in California + 40GWh in Shanghai + 50GWh planned in Texas. Its moat lies in system integration and Autobidder software, not in cells — the cells are purchased, so gross margin only reaches the low 20s.

## Tesla's position in the chain: the only player spanning all four segments

Lay out the chain and Tesla is the only company standing simultaneously in four positions: batteries (4680/LFP), chips (AI4/AI5/Dojo), vehicles, and the energy network (Supercharging + storage). This vertical integration is a moat when capacity is tight and a burden when demand is weak—because every link requires its own production line, fixed costs run far higher than peers.

Versus BYD: BYD is also vertically integrated, but its integration centers on batteries, motors, electronic controls and vehicles, with no chips and no transport capacity; its strength is cost, its weakness is moving the brand upmarket. Tesla's integration is deeper; BYD's is broader.

Versus Waymo: Waymo only does transport capacity, not manufacturing, relying on Chrysler/Jaguar for contract production. Its assets are light, expansion is slow, and per-vehicle cost is high, but it leads by at least two years in the hardest link—driverless operations. Which route wins is still unclear in 2026.

## Cycle position: mid-downcycle for vehicle OEMs, first year of autonomous-driving commercialization, mid-stage growth for energy storage

Automotive: mid-downturn. Global capacity utilization is below 70%, China's price war has not cleared, European tariff barriers are rising, and US subsidies are phasing out (the federal USD 7,500 credit expires on 2025/09/30). Headwinds blow across all three markets at once, with no systemic improvement in sight before 2027. Autonomous driving: first year of commercialization. Waymo covers 10 cities at 50.0 million rides per week, targeting 1 million by year-end; Tesla covers 7 metro areas, with no safety driver in some cities. The 0-to-1 is done; the 1-to-10 has only just begun, but the valuation prices in 1-to-100. Energy storage: mid-growth. Global installation growth has slowed from 100% to 30–40%; absolute incremental volume remains large, and the price war has already begun. This is the healthiest of the three segments and the only Tesla business that can be valued on a growth-stock framework.Three segments, three cycles, yet Tesla's valuation uses only one cycle—this is its biggest pricing mismatch.
