Core view

Tesla's business is, in essence, selling a steel shell stuffed with batteries, aluminium, chips and software for USD 42,700. That earns USD 2,320 per vehicle, of which car manufacturing accounts for only about USD 900.
I. Revenue: the weight of four pockets

2026Q2 revenue split: automotive RMB 20.516 billion (72.7%), services RMB 4.581 billion (16.2%), energy RMB 3.139 billion (11.1%), credits RMB 146 million (0.5%). Automotive is the core, services is the fastest-growing (+50.4%), energy carries the highest strategic value, and credits is dying.

Revenue per vehicle is USD 42,700 (RMB 20.516 billion ÷ 480,100 vehicles), versus around USD 45,000 in 2023 — down 5% over two years. The price cuts were deliberate, aimed at defending volume; volume held, but profit did not.

The RMB 4.581 billion in service revenue spans supercharging, insurance, used cars, parts and FSD subscriptions. What these five share is "invest once, collect many times" — the key to Tesla's shift from manufacturing to services. The company does not disclose segment data, unfortunately.

II. Costs: why gross margin caps out at 16.9%

A USD 42,700 car carries roughly USD 31,000 in material cost (battery 12,000, electric drive and electronics 6,000, body and interior 9,000, other 4,000), about 4,500 in labour and manufacturing, and about 7,200 in gross profit—equal to a 16.9% automotive gross margin.

Where does the cost advantage come from? Giga-casting consolidates 70 parts into 1 and halves the welding line; in-house 4680 cells cost 10–15% less per kWh than purchased cells; the wiring harness is one-third the length of Model 3's. These are real capabilities, holding per-vehicle cost about 15% below traditional automakers.

But the cost advantage has a ceiling: batteries are 40% of material cost, and cell prices are set by lithium prices and CATL, leaving Tesla able to influence only part of it; tariffs, freight and FX are external variables. Its gross margin therefore floors near 15%, with the price war capping it below 20%.

III. Marginal cost: software is near zero, capacity is not

The marginal cost of an FSD subscription is close to zero: each additional user consumes only a sliver of compute and bandwidth.That is the most tantalising part of Tesla's valuation—if 30% of 10 million vehicles subscribe at USD 99 a month, that is USD 35.6 billion of high-margin revenue a year.

Robotaxi's marginal cost is not zero: every additional vehicle adds depreciation, insurance, cleaning, charging and remote assistance.Waymo has run for eight years and completes 500,000 rides a week without disclosing per-vehicle profitability, which shows this business has a heavier cost structure than imagined.

So "software company" and "capacity company" are two entirely different stories:the former has 80% gross margins, is asset-light and can scale without limit; the latter has 30–40% gross margins, is asset-heavy and is capped by the number of cities.The market is pricing it as the former while it actually operates as the latter.

IV. Where is the ceiling of the business?

The ceiling for the auto business is "global share × net profit per vehicle." On 70 million global new energy vehicle units a year (2030 estimate), a 15% Tesla share implies 10.5 million vehicles; at USD 950 net profit per vehicle that is USD 10 billion of net profit, or USD 250 billion at 25x.That is the most optimistic auto story, and it is only USD 250 billion.

The ceiling for energy storage is "global installations × unit price × gross margin." Global storage installations could reach 1TWh in 2030; a 10% Tesla share implies 100GWh, or USD 23 billion of revenue at USD 23 million per GWh, and USD 138 billion at a 20% net margin and 30x.Bigger than the auto story, but it takes five years.

What truly has no ceiling is "transport capacity + robots."If Robotaxi replaces 10% of urban travel and Optimus enters 1% of households, that is a trillion-dollar market.The problem: neither has a single revenue line today.

V. Unit economics sensitivity: three variables decide survival

Start with a simplified per-vehicle model: revenue per vehicle of USD 42,700, materials plus manufacturing cost of 35,500, gross profit of 7,200. A 5% move in each of the three variables produces very different outcomes:

  • Average price down 5% (USD 2,135): gross profit falls from 7,200 to 5,100 and gross margin from 16.9% to 12.1%;
  • Material cost up 5% (USD 1,775): gross profit falls to 5,400, gross margin 12.8%;
  • Both at once: gross profit left at just 3,300, gross margin 8.1%.
This is why price wars are so brutal — the leverage cuts both ways. A 5% price cut needs 40% volume growth to recover total gross profit, while 2026Q2 volume growth was only 25%. Mathematically, this round of price cuts has not paid back yet.

Conclusion

Business in one sentence: a construct of "manufacturing cash flow + software narrative + capacity optionality." The cash-flow piece is worth RMB 570 billion, the narrative piece is worth nothing, and the optionality piece is probability-weighted. Together they get you to USD 374.
