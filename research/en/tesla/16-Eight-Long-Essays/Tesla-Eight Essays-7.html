Risk list: six risks, two that could kill the thesis — Tesla

The risk isn't a falling share price — it's the disappearance of the reason to own the stock

I. Logic-level risk one: regulation of FSD and Robotaxi

Supervised FSD has long been under NHTSA investigation in the US; in China it only launched on May 21, 2026, after executives conceded on the April earnings call that they had "not received broader approval." The entire Robotaxi valuation rests on one assumption: that "regulators permit large-scale operation without safety drivers."

Should a major market mandate safety drivers, cap the operating scope, or should a serious accident force a suspension, the RMB 910 billion option would halve immediately or worse, sending the stock toward USD 250. This is not a probability question but a tail question—unlikely to materialise, but when it does, the loss cannot be stopped.

II. Logic-level risk two: key-person risk

Musk's attention is split across xAI, SpaceX and politics. On the 2026Q2 earnings call he discussed Cybercab, Optimus and building his own wafer fab; the auto business got ten minutes. The pay package locks him in until 2035; it does not lock in his time.

If in some year he shifts his focus away entirely, Tesla's product cadence would stall immediately—because no second person at the company can "define the product." This is not a risk that can be diversified away; it sits inside the same stock.

III. Price-level risks: competition, financing, technology roadmaps, valuation
Competition: BYD is using cost to capture incremental market share, exporting 789,000 vehicles in the first half of 2026, up 70.5% year on year; Xiaomi is siphoning off the RMB 200,000–300,000 price band in China; Waymo leads in driverless operations. Competition will shave another 1–2 points off gross margin, but will not kill the company. Financing: RMB 25 billion capex, RMB 30 billion borrowing capacity, RMB 43.5 billion cash. Rising rates or tighter credit would slow expansion, but would not trigger a solvency crisis. Technology route: if pure vision proves to have a ceiling at L4 and the company has no lidar backup, the option value goes to zero. Low probability, high impact — a semi-structural risk. Valuation: 389x PE, options accounting for 61%. This is a risk that has already materialized — down 17% year to date, 23.7% below its high, yet the valuation has not gotten cheaper because earnings are falling too.
Four, three more easily overlooked
Tariffs and geopolitics: Shanghai accounts for nearly 60% of global deliveries, so US-China tariffs and the European anti-subsidy probe feed straight through to gross margin. Supply chain: Batteries depend on Panasonic, LG and CATL, and cells are bought in—as the RMB 240 million energy-storage warranty provision illustrates. Litigation and recalls: FSD-related accident lawsuits are a recurring cost in the US: small individually, not small cumulatively.

Together these fall into the "won't kill the thesis, but will keep eroding profit" bucket. The valuation impact is 1–2 points of gross margin a year; the sentiment impact is a selloff on every accident.

V. How to price the risk: working through expected loss

A rough calculation of the two logic-level risks: a 25% probability of tighter FSD regulation, halving option value if it occurs (-RMB 455 billion), for an expected loss of RMB 113.8 billion; and a 15% probability of Musk exit, a 40% valuation haircut if it occurs (-RMB 592 billion), for an expected loss of RMB 88.8 billion. Combined, the two total RMB 202.6 billion, or 13.7% of market cap.

Price-level risks (competition, financing, valuation) add a further 5–8% in expected loss. In other words, the expected cost of risk alone consumes 20% of market cap.

Meanwhile, upside in the base case is only 2%. One equation makes it clear: 2% expected return against 20% expected risk cost — this is not a trade worth making.

Six: how the six risks interrelate, in one sentence

The six risks form a single causal chain: a price war compresses gross margin → gross margin falls short → capex-funded bets on new businesses → capex requires financing → financing depends on the story → the story depends on regulators and Musk.

Every link holds, so the risk is not that the chain "may" break but that it is already interconnected. If any link breaks, the shock transmits through the entire chain to valuation.

The most fragile link is the last: the story depends on regulators and Musk—two variables the company itself cannot control.

Conclusion

Risk assessment: Of the six risks, two are thesis-level and four are price-level, plus three slow-burn items. A thesis-level risk is defined by the fact that once it materialises, the valuation framework itself changes — from RMB 1.48 trillion to RMB 600 billion. With risk/reward already unfavourable (base case +2%, bear case -33%), adding tail risk on top argues for zero exposure.
