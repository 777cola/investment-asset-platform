Industry and competition: three cycles, one valuation — Tesla

Whole vehicles are in recession, smart driving is only just starting out and energy storage is growing — yet the market applies a single multiple
Research date 2026/07/22  ·  Price $374.01 (-1.30%)  ·  Market cap Approximately USD 1.48 trillion  ·  P/E (TTM) — (Margins too thin; PE no longer meaningful)

Core view

Within their own cycles, the three business lines are clearly out of sync: vehicles are mid-downcycle, autonomous driving is in its first year of commercialisation and energy storage is mid-growth — yet the market prices the whole business on an "autonomous driving endgame" multiple.

I. Complete vehicles: global overcapacity, no winners in the price war

China's new-energy passenger vehicle market slowed to single-digit growth in 1H26. BYD sold 1.809 million units, down 15.7% YoY — the steepest decline among automakers that have disclosed figures — while exports reached 789,000 units, up 70.5% YoY, overseas sales offsetting weakness at home. Even the leader is shrinking, a sign the industry has moved from chasing growth to fighting over share.

Global capacity utilisation is below 70%. Europe has built a tariff wall, and the US cancelled its USD 7,500 federal subsidy on September 30, 2025. With all three major markets facing headwinds at once, no systemic improvement is in sight before 2027.

Tesla's position: pricing power in North America on brand premium; competition on value for money in China, where the Shanghai plant delivered 213,000 units in Q1, nearly 60% of global deliveries; and a squeeze in Europe from tariffs and localisation requirements. Three markets: one for profit, one for volume, one on the defensive.

II. Autonomous driving: the route debate has a provisional answer

Waymo: 10 cities, 500,000 paid rides per week, a fleet of about 3,000 vehicles and a target of 1 million rides per week by end-2026, using lidar plus high-definition mapping. Its strengths are its safety record and regulatory trust; its weaknesses are high per-vehicle cost and slow expansion.

Tesla: 7 major US metropolitan areas, no safety monitor in Dallas/Houston from April 2026, expansion to the entire Austin metro area in June, Miami, Orlando and Tampa added in July, and the Cybercab already in production in Texas. Its strengths are cost and generalisation capability; its weaknesses are safety record and regulatory trust.

Key difference: Waymo sells "guaranteed driverless"; Tesla sells "cheap driverless." Waymo's customers are regulators and passengers; Tesla's are shareholders — because its cost structure can support a larger fleet. Only 2028 will tell which model wins.

III. Energy storage: the only healthy growth business

Global energy storage installations will still grow more than 30% in 2026, driven by US data-center co-located storage, European grid peak-shaving, and utility-scale storage in the Middle East and Australia. Tesla deployed 13.5GWh in Q2, up 41% YoY, with capacity of 40GWh in California, 40GWh in Shanghai and a planned 50GWh in Texas.

Price is the problem: revenue rose only 12.6% on 41% volume growth, implying unit prices fell by a fifth. Chinese vendors (CATL, Sungrow, BYD) undercut utility-scale storage quotes by 20–30%, and Tesla defends the high end on brand, Autobidder software and delivery capability, with gross margin pushed down to 20.4%.

This is the only one of the three businesses that can be valued on growth-stock logic: predictable, long-cycle, with obvious scale effects. But it is too small — RMB 3.139 billion a quarter, 11% of total revenue.

IV. Endgame projection for the competitive landscape
Most likely scenario (2028): The vehicle market settles into a three-way split — Chinese makers dominating mid- and low-end, Tesla dominating North American high-end, and localised European production. Autonomous driving becomes a duopoly: Waymo leading on operations, Tesla leading on cost. Energy storage becomes a three-way race between Tesla, CATL and Sungrow. Tesla is top three in every battlefield, first in none.

This landscape implies a valuation of RMB 400–500 billion for vehicles, RMB 100–150 billion for energy storage and RMB 200–300 billion of option value in autonomous driving — RMB 700–950 billion in total. That is 35–50% below today's RMB 1.48 trillion.

For the valuation to hold, Tesla must become number one in one battlefield — and the kind of number one that "defines the standard." The closest today is Optimus, because no one has yet defined the standard. That is also why Musk tore out the Fremont production line and converted it to robots.

V. Three ways the price war ends
Path one: clearing (least likely). Weak brands exit, capacity contracts and prices recover. China has more than 50 NEV makers and a batch had already halted production by the first half of 2026, but the leaders are still adding capacity, so clearing is running far slower than expected. Path two: stalemate (most likely). Leaders lean on their cost advantage to hold prices near the break-even line; nobody makes money but nobody exits. BYD's first-half 2026 sales fell 15.7% while exports rose 70.5% — a portrait of "stalemate at home, seek a way out overseas." In this state, Tesla's gross margin will sit around 16% for a long time. Path three: technology substitution (the variable). If Robotaxi or intelligent driving opens a generational gap, the price war shifts from "competing on cost" to "competing on experience," and gross margin reopens. This is Tesla's only way out, and the entire hope behind the RMB 910 billion option.

Of the three endings, stalemate is the most probable, while clearing and technology substitution both take time. Implication for valuation: do not expect a big gross-margin recovery over the next two years; holding 16% would count as a win.

Conclusion

Industry view: Three business lines, three cycles, three positions: third in vehicles, second in autonomous driving, second in energy storage. A "top three everywhere, first nowhere" profile cannot support a "sole winner" valuation. Either Optimus delivers, or the price comes down.
