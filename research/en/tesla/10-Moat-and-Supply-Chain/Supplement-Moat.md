## Position in the value chain (1): upstream in-house, midstream in-house, downstream also in-house

Tesla is the most vertically integrated of the automakers. Upstream: 30GWh lithium refining in Texas, 10GWh cathode material, 7GWh LFP cells in Nevada; midstream: over 40GWh of 4680 cells, in-house AI4/AI5 chips, a semiconductor plant under construction in Austin; downstream: direct-sales stores, 70,000 superchargers, insurance, used cars, Robotaxi operations. Across a car's value chain, it wants to capture almost every segment itself, except mining and some cells.

This model is a huge advantage when capacity is tight—during the 2021–2022 chip and battery shortages, Tesla was the only EV maker that did not halt production. It is a huge burden when demand is weak—every segment must carry capacity, fixed costs run higher than peers, and once utilization slips, gross margin collapses.Gross margin of 16.8% and operating margin of 1.4% in Q2 2026 are the result of this structure.

## Industry-chain position (II): bargaining power over suppliers, none over customers

Facing its supply chain, Tesla is the stronger party: three battery suppliers bid against each other (Panasonic, LG, CATL), and seats, glass and die-cast parts are all multi-sourced; with its own capacity as a bargaining chip, it can squeeze supplier gross margins below 10%. This is why its per-vehicle cost runs a notch below traditional automakers'.

Facing consumers, it is the weaker party. It cut prices in all major markets in 2026, delivering a record 480,000 vehicles in Q2, at the cost of a continued slide in average selling price. Its upstream bargaining power ultimately passes through to consumers via price cuts—which is why cost reductions have not turned into profit growth.

The sole exception is the Supercharger network. After opening to other brands, it went from "exclusive asset" to "toll infrastructure," giving it the right to charge third-party automakers. This is its only business with downstream pricing power, and the highest score in our moat assessment at 9.

## Moat item by item: brand and network are hard assets; licences and switching costs are weak points

Brand 8 points: The default EV option in North America; Model Y has been the global best-seller for three consecutive years. Points deducted in China — 2025 China sales 626,000 units, -4.8% YoY, share diverted by BYD and Xiaomi; Europe 238,700 units, -27% YoY. The brand remains; the premium is falling. Cost 7 points: 4680 batteries, single-piece die-casting and streamlined wiring harnesses keep per-vehicle cost roughly 15% below traditional automakers. But BYD's vertical integration is more thorough (over 80% battery self-supply), giving it a lower cost base. It is one of the best, not the only one. Network 9 points: 70,000 Superchargers — location, grid capacity and land contracts are a decade of accumulation that money cannot buy. After NACS became the North American standard, it went from "exclusive" to "collecting rent" — the moat shallows, but cash flow steadies. Switching cost 5 points: The infotainment system, FSD and Supercharging experience have stickiness, but the physical cost of changing cars is low; user loyalty is sustained by product strength, not ecosystem lock-in. This is the biggest difference from Apple. Licence 4 points: Robotaxi requires fresh approval in every city it enters — Texas and Florida are relatively permissive, California strict. Waymo already covers 10 cities at 500,000 orders per week; Tesla is the challenger on this river, not the incumbent.

## The most dangerous rival: BYD in the short term, Waymo in the long term

Short term (1–3 years): BYD. It uses vertical integration to push costs below Tesla's. In the first half of 2026 it exported 789,000 vehicles, up 70.5% year on year, taking incremental market share directly from Tesla in Southeast Asia, Latin America and the Middle East. What it is taking is not Tesla's technology narrative but Tesla's current cash flow. Sell 50,000 fewer cars in a quarter and gross margin drops a point. Long term (3–5 years): Waymo. It already handles 500,000 paid rides a week across 10 cities with a fleet of about 3,000 vehicles, targeting 1 million rides a week by the end of 2026. Its significance is not taking market share but proving that driverless operation can be run safely and commercially — once that proof holds, the core of Tesla's USD 910 billion option value must be repriced on Waymo's cadence. Xiaomi is a variable, not the main line. It diverts customers in China's RMB 200,000–300,000 price band, but its battlefield is China, not the globe; its valuation logic is the ecosystem, not transport capacity. The threat to Tesla is "a few tens of thousands fewer cars sold," not "the business model being replaced."In one sentence: BYD makes Tesla earn less; Waymo makes Tesla's story cheaper. The former hits the income statement, the latter hits the valuation.
