## Position in the value chain (1): in-house at every stage — upstream, midstream and downstream

Tesla is the most vertically integrated of the automakers. Upstream, it runs 30GWh of lithium refining in Texas, 10GWh of cathode material and 7GWh of LFP cells in Nevada; midstream, more than 40GWh of 4680 cells, in-house AI4/AI5 chips and a semiconductor plant under construction in Austin; downstream, direct-sales stores, 70,000 superchargers, insurance, used cars and Robotaxi operations. Across a car's value chain, it aims to capture almost every segment itself, except mining and some cells.

That model is a huge advantage when capacity is tight—during the 2021–2022 chip and battery shortages, Tesla was the only EV maker that did not halt production. It is a huge burden when demand is weak: every segment must carry capacity, fixed costs run higher than peers, and once utilization slips, gross margin collapses. The 16.8% gross margin and 1.4% operating margin in Q2 2026 are the product of this structure.

## Industry-chain position (II): bargaining power over suppliers, none over customers

Tesla holds the stronger hand with suppliers: three battery makers (Panasonic, LG, CATL) bid against one another, and seats, glass and die-cast parts are all multi-sourced. With its own capacity as a bargaining chip, it can squeeze supplier gross margins below 10% — which is why its per-vehicle cost runs a notch below traditional automakers'.

With consumers, it holds the weaker hand. It cut prices across all major markets in 2026 and delivered a record 480,000 vehicles in Q2, at the cost of a continued slide in average selling price. Its upstream bargaining power ultimately passes through to consumers via price cuts—which is why cost reductions have not turned into profit growth.

The sole exception is the Supercharger network. After opening to other brands it went from "exclusive asset" to "toll infrastructure," giving Tesla the right to charge third-party automakers. This is its only business with downstream pricing power, and the highest score in our moat assessment at 9.

## Moat, item by item: brand and network are hard assets; licences and switching costs are the weak points

Brand 8 points: The default EV in North America; Model Y has been the global best-seller for three consecutive years. Marks come off in China — 2025 China sales of 626,000 units, -4.8% YoY, with share lost to BYD and Xiaomi; Europe 238,700 units, -27% YoY. The brand endures; the premium is eroding. Cost 7 points: 4680 batteries, single-piece die-casting and simplified wiring harnesses hold per-vehicle cost roughly 15% below traditional automakers. BYD's vertical integration is deeper, though (over 80% battery self-supply), giving it a lower cost base. Tesla is one of the best, not the only one. Network 9 points: 70,000 Superchargers — site selection, grid capacity and land contracts represent a decade of accumulation that money cannot buy. After NACS became the North American standard, the network went from "exclusive" to "collecting rent" — the moat shallows, but cash flow steadies. Switching cost 5 points: The infotainment system, FSD and Supercharging experience have stickiness, but the physical cost of switching cars is low; loyalty rests on product strength, not ecosystem lock-in. This is the biggest difference from Apple. Licence 4 points: Robotaxi needs fresh approval in every city it enters — Texas and Florida are relatively permissive, California strict. Waymo already covers 10 cities at 500,000 orders per week; on this river Tesla is the challenger, not the incumbent.

## The most dangerous rival: BYD near term, Waymo long term

Short term (1–3 years): BYD. Vertical integration pushes its costs below Tesla's. In 1H26 it exported 789,000 vehicles, up 70.5% YoY, taking incremental share directly from Tesla in Southeast Asia, Latin America and the Middle East. What it is taking is not Tesla's technology narrative but Tesla's current cash flow. Sell 50,000 fewer cars in a quarter and gross margin drops a point. Long term (3–5 years): Waymo. It already handles 500,000 paid rides a week across 10 cities with a fleet of about 3,000 vehicles, targeting 1 million rides a week by the end of 2026. Its significance is not taking market share but proving that driverless operation can be run safely and commercially — once that proof holds, the core of Tesla's USD 910 billion option value must be repriced on Waymo's cadence. Xiaomi is a variable, not the main line. It diverts customers in China's RMB 200,000–300,000 price band, but its battlefield is China, not the globe; its valuation logic is the ecosystem, not transport capacity. The threat to Tesla is "a few tens of thousands fewer cars sold," not "the business model being replaced."In one sentence: BYD makes Tesla earn less; Waymo makes Tesla's story cheaper. The former hits the income statement, the latter hits the valuation.
