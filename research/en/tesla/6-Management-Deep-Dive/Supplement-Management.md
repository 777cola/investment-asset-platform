## Key people and power structure: companies without a second-in-command

Musk's power comes from three places: his 13% stake, his founder status, and the market's reliance on his personal narrative. Tesla has no COO and no clear president; major product decisions (the Cybertruck's styling, cancelling the Model 2, dismantling the S/X lines) are all his to make alone. This structure is an advantage in execution — 480,000 vehicles delivered in 2026Q2 and the industry's fastest capacity ramp rest on a short decision chain.

The risk sits in the same place. In his public schedule for the first half of 2026, xAI fundraising, SpaceX Starship test flights and political donations took up most of it; on Tesla's earnings call he spoke only about the Cybercab, Optimus and building its own wafer fab. Shareholders are buying his attention, and he has only one.

Zhu Xiaotong is the only person the market treats as the "operational successor": the Shanghai plant went into production in 10 months and reached annual capacity of 950,000 vehicles at a single site, the ceiling of Tesla's manufacturing system. But he runs execution, not strategy; with Musk absent, the company can operate but cannot change direction.

## Capital allocation record: right on three of four big calls; the fifth has yet to reach its proving period

2019 Shanghai plant: Built in 10 months with Chinese loans and a Chinese supply chain, it cut Model 3 per-unit cost by 30% and was the key step that took Tesla from the brink of bankruptcy to global leadership. A full-marks decision. 2023 global price cuts: Trading gross margin for share, volumes went from 1.81 million units in 2023 to 1.79 million in 2024, then fell to 1.636 million in 2025. It grabbed volume in the short term, failed to hold share in the long term, and shaved a layer off brand premium along the way. This one scores only 5. 2024–2026 energy storage expansion: 40GWh in California, 40GWh in Shanghai Lingang (production starts 2026, output exported to Europe), and 50GWh under construction in Texas. Energy storage went from a supporting act in 2023 to USD 3.14 billion of quarterly revenue in 2026Q2 — an underappreciated correct bet. 2026 capex of USD 25 billion: Directed at AI compute (Cortex 1/2 already exceed 200MW), the Cybercab line, the first-generation Optimus mass-production line, and a self-built semiconductor fab in Austin. Free cash flow of -USD 1.09 billion, with USD 30 billion of borrowing capacity lined up. This is a transformation of the company from "manufacturing cash flow" to "AI infrastructure," a bet ten times larger than the Shanghai plant, with validation only after 2028.

## Integrity and governance events: three controversies, one pattern

2018 "privatization funding secured": The stock swung violently after the tweet, the SEC sued for securities fraud, and the case ended in a settlement — USD 20 million each for the company and the individual, Musk stepping down as chairman for three years, and tweets requiring pre-clearance by lawyers. This was the most serious trust crisis in Tesla's governance history, and the pattern was "substituting public statements for formal disclosure." 2022 share sales: Musk sold more than USD 20 billion of stock cumulatively to fund the Twitter acquisition, with no advance warning. Shareholders could not stop it and simply had to absorb it. After this, the market developed muscle memory around "Musk will treat Tesla stock as an ATM." 2025 USD 1 trillion pay package: Both ISS and Glass Lewis recommended against it, citing excessive dilution and overly loose milestones (the top market-cap tier being USD 8.5 trillion). Shareholders approved it with more than 75% support. The supporters' logic: without keeping him, the company is nothing; the opponents' logic: the price is too high. Both are right.One more point that is easily overlooked: xAI's related-party transactions with Tesla. Tesla has supplied Megapack units to xAI and shared some AI talent, with transactions settled at market prices, but who reviews the fairness of that pricing has never had a clear answer. These transactions do not make headlines, but they steadily erode confidence that "minority shareholders are treated fairly."

## Succession: the pay package kicked the problem down the road for a decade and locked in the answer

Without this pay package, Tesla's biggest governance risk was "what happens if Musk leaves." With the package approved, that question is pushed out to 2035, at the cost of up to 12% potential dilution. In other words, shareholders bought a ten-year "key-man insurance" policy with equity.

The internal succession ladder is clearly ordered: Tom Zhu runs manufacturing and operations, Ashok runs AI and autonomy, Taneja runs the money. Together the three can keep the company running, but none can replace Musk's ability to "define the product" — every leap Tesla has made over the past decade came from a product decision no one else dared to make.

The implication for investors: this company's management score should not be graded on "whether there is a successor," but on "whether the machine can still run without the founder." The answer is that it can run, but cannot change direction, so 6 points — 9 for capability, 4 for governance, averaging 6.
