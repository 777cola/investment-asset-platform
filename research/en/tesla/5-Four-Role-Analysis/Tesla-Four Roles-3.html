Industry Research (Munger's Lens) — Tesla

Profit pools are shifting from OEMs to chips and capacity
Research date 2026/07/22  ·  Price $374.01 (-1.30%)  ·  Market cap Approximately USD 1.48 trillion  ·  PE(TTM) — (Margins too thin; PE no longer meaningful)

Core view

The EV industry is no longer a growth industry but a zero-sum game over existing demand; the profit pool for autonomous driving has yet to form; energy storage is the only segment still in its expansion phase. These three statements define Tesla's position.
Whole vehicles: global overcapacity, with China at the epicenter of the price war

In the first half of 2026, China's new-energy passenger vehicle sales growth fell back to single digits, with BYD at 1.809 million units, down 15.7% year on year—the leader is declining, showing the industry has moved from "competing for growth" to "competing for share." Over the same period, BYD exported 789,000 vehicles, up 70.5% year on year, as Chinese automakers dump excess capacity overseas, igniting price wars across Europe, Southeast Asia and Latin America.

Tesla's position is awkward: in North America it is a premium brand with pricing power; in China it must fight BYD, Xiaomi and Li Auto head-on on value for money; in Europe it faces pressure from both tariffs and localised production. In 2025 its European sales were 238,700 units, down 27% year on year, and China sales 626,000 units, down 4.8% year on year.Of its three main markets, two are losing share.

Munger would ask: has this industry cleared its excess capacity? The answer is no. Global vehicle capacity utilisation is below 70%, and China is worse.In an industry with overcapacity, even the best company can only earn average profits.

Autonomous driving: the route debate has a provisional answer

Pure vision versus LiDAR: this debate has run for eight years. As of July 2026, the interim answer is: Waymo's LiDAR plus HD-map route leads on "driverless commercial operation," while Tesla's pure vision plus end-to-end approach leads on "cost and generalization."

Waymo runs 50.0 thousand paid trips per week across 10 cities with a fleet of about 3,000 vehicles, targeting 1 million trips per week by the end of 2026. Tesla Robotaxi covers seven major US metropolitan areas, entering Dallas and Houston in April 2026, expanding to the entire Austin metro in June, and adding Miami, Orlando and Tampa in July, but its trip volume and fleet size are far smaller than Waymo's, and some cities still require safety drivers.

The key difference lies in cost structure:Waymo's per-vehicle retrofit cost is high, expansion is slow, and every city requires HD maps; Tesla uses its existing fleet and expands at near-zero marginal cost, but its safety record and regulatory trust are weak points.Munger would say: these are two different businesses, not two players on one track.
Energy storage: the only segment still in an expansion phase, but Chinese manufacturers are driving prices down

Global energy storage installations will still grow more than 30% in 2026, driven by US data-center storage, European grid peak-shaving, and large-scale storage projects in the Middle East and Australia. Tesla deployed 13.5GWh in Q2, up 41% year on year, with 40GWh in California, 40GWh in Shanghai Lingang and a planned 50GWh in Texas — a global manufacturing footprint.

But prices are falling. Energy segment revenue rose only 12.6% year on year against a 41% increase in deployments, implying unit prices fell by a fifth. Chinese manufacturers (CATL, Sungrow, BYD) quote 20–30% below Megapack in the large-scale storage market, and Tesla defends the high end on brand, software (Autobidder) and delivery capability.The 20.4% segment gross margin is the outcome of this price war.

Munger liked this kind of business: predictable, long-cycle, with obvious scale effects. But he would also caution: manufacturing margins are ultimately compressed by competition toward the cost of capital unless you have a genuine technical moat. Megapack's moat lies in system integration and software, not in cells — the cells are bought in.

Cycle position: three segments, three different clocks
Whole vehicles: Mid-cycle downturn. Global overcapacity, an unresolved price war, and fading subsidies leave no systemic improvement in sight before 2027. Autonomous driving: First year of commercialization; the 0-to-1 phase is complete, the 1-to-10 phase has not begun, yet the valuation prices in 1-to-100. Energy storage: Mid-cycle growth, with growth slowing from 100% to 30–40%, though absolute incremental volume remains large.

Tesla's valuation treats these three clocks as one: it applies energy-storage growth to the whole-vehicle business and prices the present at the autonomous-driving endgame. This is the error Munger hated most—lumping different businesses together into a single total.

The right approach is to value them separately: whole vehicles at a manufacturing multiple (15–20x), energy storage at a growth-stock multiple (25–30x), and autonomous driving as an option (probability-weighted). Summed separately, the three parts come to RMB 570 billion, not RMB 1.48 trillion.

Conclusion

Industry view: Tesla sits in a gap where "the old industry is declining and the new industry has yet to take shape." In vehicles it is no longer the fastest-growing player, in autonomous driving it is not the player with the most orders, and in energy storage it is one of the most profitable players but its moat is shallow. Its industry position is not bad, but it does not justify a valuation of USD 1.48 trillion.
