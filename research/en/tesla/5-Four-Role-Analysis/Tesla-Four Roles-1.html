Business analysis (Duan Yongping's perspective) — Tesla

What actually makes Tesla money, and where the money leaks out
Research date 2026/07/22  ·  Price $374.01 (-1.30%)  ·  Market cap Approximately USD 1.48 trillion  ·  PE(TTM) — (Margins too thin; PE no longer meaningful)

Core view

In one sentence: it makes money selling cars and gets its valuation from selling stories. Four pockets—vehicles, energy storage, services, credits—only the first two have real scale and gross profit, and both saw gross margin decline this quarter.
Four pockets, two that actually make money

Breaking down 2026Q2 revenue: automotive RMB 20.516 billion (73%), services and other RMB 4.581 billion (16%), energy RMB 3.139 billion (11%), regulatory credits RMB 146 million (0.5%). Automotive is the dominant piece, but it also carries the lowest gross margin—16.9%, and still declining year over year.

Services and other grew 50% year over year, the fastest-growing segment, comprising supercharging fees, insurance, used cars, parts and FSD subscriptions. The upside is low capex and high customer stickiness; the downside is that the company does not disclose this segment's gross margin or FSD's specific revenue, so you see only a RMB 4.581 billion black box growing fast, without visibility into what inside is making money.

Energy is the second leg: 13.5GWh, up 41% year over year, with revenue of RMB 3.139 billion, up only 12.6%—volume up, price down, implying unit price fell about 20% from last year. Segment gross margin was 20.4%, roughly halved from 39.5% a year earlier, including RMB 240 million in warranty provisions for battery suppliers.

The credits line is essentially over: RMB 146 million versus RMB 439 million a year earlier. Policy has changed, and this profit will not come back.

Unit economics: USD 2,320 profit per vehicle, USD 900 of it from manufacturing

Spread across 480,100 vehicles, company-wide net profit per vehicle comes to USD 2,320 (USD 1.114 billion ÷ 480,100). That looks decent — Toyota earns about USD 2,500 per vehicle and BYD about USD 1,000. But break it down and half of that USD 2,320 is not earned from selling cars:

  • Automotive business contributes about USD 900 per vehicle (a rough calculation from segment gross profit after deducting expenses);
  • Energy storage and services contribute about USD 700 per vehicle;
  • Other income (bitcoin fair-value changes, foreign exchange) contributes about USD 1,200 per vehicle — this item is unsustainable and its direction is random.

In other words, looking at carmaking alone, Tesla's per-vehicle profit is already close to that of China's leading automakers, not to that of a "software company." This is the number Duan Yongping would fixate on: not how big revenue is, but how much a single vehicle actually earns.

Pricing power: from "queue up unless you pay more" to "volume only with price cuts"

In 2021, Model Y buyers in China waited 4.0 months, and paying a premium to jump the queue was the norm. By 2026 the situation has reversed: Q2 deliveries hit a record 48.0 million units, but the cost was a continued slide in average selling price, incentive revenue falling to zero, and lower per-vehicle manufacturing cost failing to close the gross margin gap. Pricing power means raising prices without losing volume; Tesla now has to cut prices just to hold volume.

Compare BYD, which is also trading price for volume: H1 2026 sales of 180.9 million units, down 15.7% year on year, with exports of 78.9 million units, up 70.5%. The leader is bleeding at home and seeking a way out overseas, a sign that the price war in China, its largest market, is not over. The Shanghai plant delivered 21.3 million units in Q1, nearly 60% of global volume; when China sneezes, Tesla's gross margin catches a cold.

What makes this business good, and where it falls short
The good side: Demand is real. In a global passenger-car market of 70 million units a year, EV penetration is still climbing, and Tesla's brand remains the default choice in North America and Europe; energy storage is a utility-style long-cycle business with better order visibility than cars; and once software subscriptions scale, marginal cost is close to zero. The bad side: Carmaking combines capital intensity, deep cyclicality and price war—money earned must be plowed straight back into capacity or you fall behind. Capex above USD 25 billion and free cash flow of -USD 1.09 billion in 2026 are the mathematical expression of that model.

Duan Yongping would say: "A good business is one an idiot can run." Tesla clearly is not—it needs someone continually making the right product judgments to sustain its edge. Such a business can be great, but it is not the kind that "makes money while you sleep."

Conclusion

Business judgment: This is half of a good business plus a business that has yet to take shape. The vehicle half is real, but is turning into ordinary manufacturing; the energy storage half is growing, but gross margins are unstable; the software and mobility half is still on PowerPoint. Together, the three can support a valuation of USD 500–600 billion, not USD 1.48 trillion.
