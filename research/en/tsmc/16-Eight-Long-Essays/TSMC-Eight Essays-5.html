Industry and competition: only one switch on the entire chain — TSMC

70% share, two generations ahead, a curve no one can catch

Core view

Global wafer foundry is an extremely concentrated business: TSMC holds about 70% share and second-place Samsung Foundry about 8%. That structure owes nothing to regulation; it is the inevitable product of economies of scale and the learning curve. Understand this, and you understand why the industry calls not for "stock picking" but for "price picking".

I. Market-share map: a landslide lead

The 2026 Q2 landscape: TSMC about 70%, Samsung Foundry about 8%, UMC about 6%, SMIC about 5%, GlobalFoundries about 5%, Intel Foundry under 3%. The leader is nearly nine times the runner-up—extremely rare in manufacturing.

More critical is that advanced-node share is even more extreme: below 7nm sits almost entirely in TSMC's hands (Samsung holds a small amount, Intel is just starting). Mature nodes (28nm and above) are the battleground for UMC, GlobalFoundries and SMIC, where price competition is fierce and gross margins run 20%–35%.

The "70% foundry share" figure therefore understates TSMC's position—by profit, it takes more than 80% of the industry's profits.

II. Samsung: the only rival keeping pace, but losing on yield

Samsung is the only rival that does not lag on node: its 2nm mass-production timing trails TSMC by only a little, its EUV tool count is sufficient, and the group has memory and handset cash flow to support it. The gap is yield — on SRAM test structures TSMC is near 90%, while Samsung is estimated at around 70% (market estimate).

A 20-percentage-point yield gap implies unit costs more than 30% higher. That is why Samsung Foundry has lost money for years, and why it cannot win Nvidia's main orders.

A structural barrier also stands in the way: Samsung builds its own handsets, memory and chip design. A customer handing it a flagship chip is handing its product roadmap to a competitor. That barrier has stood for a decade and will be hard to remove.

III. Intel: policy backing, but missing two things

Intel 18A ramps in 2026 with US government backing — a government equity stake plus subsidies — making it the only competitor capable of changing the landscape after 2028. It lacks two things: customer trust and scale.

The trust problem mirrors Samsung's: Intel makes its own CPUs, and AMD, Nvidia and Qualcomm are all competitors. Scale is the more concrete constraint. Foundry requires more than USD 20 billion in annual capex, Intel's own cash flow cannot sustain it, and it must lean on government and external investment.

The Intel threat is therefore a post-2028 story, not a 2026 one. But it is the only competitor backed by "national will", and cannot be entirely ignored.

IV. SMIC and mature-node processes: another war

Equipment controls lock SMIC at the 7nm-class node (DUV multi-patterning), more than three generations behind TSMC. SMIC revenue was USD 3 billion in 2026Q2, +36% YoY, with net profit of USD 479 million, +261.7% YoY—rapid growth, but off a low base, and gross margin is only around 22%.

SMIC does not compete in TSMC's market: it serves domestic Chinese design companies, concentrated in mature nodes and specialty processes. Chinese capacity expansion is driving prices down in that market, with global mature-node prices falling 10%–20% in 2026 (industry estimate).

The impact on TSMC is indirect: profit at mature nodes (23% of revenue) comes under pressure, while advanced nodes (77%) are unaffected. That is the benefit of walking on two legs—if one leg is stepped on, the other can still run.

V. Cycle position: three signs this upcycle is in its late stage

First, capex is at an all-time high of USD 60–64 billion; historically every capex peak has arrived one to two quarters before the demand peak. Second, gross margin is at an all-time high of 67.7%, and management itself guides it lower. Third, customers have begun discussing a "second supplier" — the demand side's standard move to prepare for a downturn.

All three point to the same conclusion: the cycle is still in the top zone, but the slope has flattened. That does not mean a fall is imminent; it means "there is not much good news left."

Judging by the historical rhythm of the foundry industry (three years up, two years down), the risk window runs from the second half of 2026 to the first half of 2027. TSMC's own guidance hints at this: revenue will still hit new highs, but gross margin starts to decline.

Conclusion

Industry and competitive conclusion: excellent structure, strong cyclicality, no near-term threat from rivals. TSMC's position is irreplaceable — but "irreplaceable" does not mean "worth buying at any time." We are now in the late stage of the upcycle.
