The nature of the business: it does not sell wafers, it sells certainty — TSMC

Why customers pay 50% more and wire the money a year in advance

Core view

A 2nm wafer is quoted at about USD 30,000, more than 10 times that of China's mature-node processes. Customers are no fools—what they buy is not silicon but the assurance that "the product ships on time." TSMC's business, at its core, is converting uncertainty into certainty and charging a fee for that conversion.

I. What exactly are customers paying for?

An AI accelerator chip costs about USD 500 million to design and USD 30 million per tape-out. If the first tape-out fails, the loss is not just money but 6–9 months of time. In the AI market, being 9 months late means losing an entire product generation.

What TSMC sells is the probability of getting it right the first time. Its 2nm yield reaches above 80% in early mass production (close to 90% on SRAM test structures), meaning customers' designs ship on schedule. What is that probability worth? Customers have done the math themselves: paying 50% more for wafers is far cheaper than launching 9 months late.

So pricing power comes not from low cost but from "no one else can do it." This is the most counterintuitive thing about the business—it is not the scale logic of manufacturing but the logic of scarcity.

II. The three layers of revenue

The first layer is wafer foundry, charged per wafer, accounting for more than 90% of revenue. Pricing at this layer tracks the process node: 2nm costs 30%–50% more than 3nm, and 3nm costs about 20% more than 5nm.

The second layer is advanced packaging (CoWoS, SoIC, InFO), charged per unit. This layer is more profitable because it is the second bottleneck for AI chips—GPUs and HBM must be stacked together, and only TSMC's CoWoS-L can handle large package sizes. The market estimates gross margin on this segment at above 50%.

The third layer is "co-development": customers engage TSMC at the chip definition stage, with costs folded into the development project rather than priced separately. This layer contributes no revenue but locks in orders for the next three to five years. Stack the three layers together, and you have the complete business.

III. Cost structure: depreciation is the biggest variable

A 2nm fab costs USD 20 billion and up, with equipment accounting for over 70%. Over five-year depreciation, that is USD 4 billion a year, or 40%–50% of wafer cost. This means two things: utilization must stay high (below 80% and the fab loses money), and gross margin in the early stage of every new process node gets squeezed by depreciation.

2026 capex is USD 60–64 billion. On five-year depreciation, that adds about USD 13 billion (roughly TWD 410 billion) of annual depreciation from 2027. This is one reason Q3 gross margin guidance fell from 67.7% to 66%, and a structural drag on gross margin in 2027–2028.

TSMC's response is to set a shorter depreciation life than peers — more conservative reported profit, and a "depreciation roll-off" tailwind down the road. This is accounting conservatism, not financial engineering.

IV. Why this business can only have one winner

Economies of scale plus the learning curve. The larger the output, the lower the cost; the lower the cost, the more competitive the quote; the more competitive the quote, the more orders won. Run that positive feedback for a decade and the gap between first and second place widens beyond reach.

The data: TSMC holds about 70% share, second-place Samsung Foundry about 8%. More telling is the gross margin gap — TSMC at 67.7%, Samsung Foundry an estimated 30%–40%. Same equipment, same customer demand, yet a twofold gross margin gap — the difference lies in yield.

That is why this industry does not require "stock picking" — it only requires "price picking."

One more layer: this positive feedback also reinforces the customer structure itself. The more customers design around TSMC's process, the more complete its process-parameter library becomes, and the faster the next generation climbs the yield curve. This is a "data flywheel," similar in form but different in substance from the network effects of internet companies — it runs on physical data, not user scale.

Five: Where is the ceiling for this business?

The constraint is not demand; it is physics. After 2nm comes A16 (1.6nm, risk production in the second half of 2026) and A14 (2028), with each generation's performance gain falling from 30% to around 15%. Customers have less incentive to upgrade, and unit prices can still rise (because no one else can make them), but shipment growth will slow.

The other ceiling is customer willingness. Nvidia, Apple and cloud providers are all assessing whether the premium is worth paying indefinitely. If one day the performance gain from advanced processes becomes too small for customers to want to switch, TSMC's pricing power will loosen.

Neither ceiling arrives within three years, but they determine what PE multiple to assign a decade out. My judgment: it will still be here in ten years, but the valuation center will revert to 18–20x.

Conclusion

Conclusion on the nature of the business: it sells certainty, earns money from scarcity, costs are dominated by depreciation, and structurally there is only one winner. This is a 9-out-of-10 business, with points deducted for "having to keep investing capital" and a "visible ceiling."
