Management: a team of engineers and a tradition of "no bragging" — TSMC

Thirty years spent on one thing; eight years without a single bad loan

Core view

TSMC's management is the least "storytelling" group in the semiconductor industry. C.C. Wei, Y.J. Mii and Y.P. Chin all come from process and manufacturing backgrounds, and they speak in "data + conditions + boundaries." That style is unpopular in a bull market and lifesaving in a cycle.

1. C.C. Wei: Treating conservatism as a capability

C.C. Wei joined TSMC in 1998, after holding technical management roles at Texas Instruments and STMicroelectronics in the United States. He became president in 2018 and chairman in 2024, the first person in TSMC's history to hold both roles at once.

His public style can be summed up as "never promise what you can't deliver." In 2023, with the industry in a downturn, he refused to give full-year growth guidance; in 2024, as AI demand first emerged, he said "the demand is real, but we need to see whether customers can get paid"; in 2026, when raising capex, he took the initiative to stress that "depreciation will pressure gross margin, and we want to make that clear upfront."

This communication style can be verified quantitatively: over the past eight years, more than 90% of the guidance given at earnings calls has been delivered. That figure is rare in the semiconductor industry — both Samsung and Intel have repeatedly pushed back timelines.

II. Dual COOs: one runs technology, one runs capacity

In 2024, Y.J. Mii and Y.P. Chin were both promoted to Executive Vice President and Co-Chief Operating Officer. Mii came up through R&D and led the 7/5/3/2nm technology roadmap; Chin came up through manufacturing and fab operations and oversees global capacity buildout.

This division of labor maps to TSMC's two lifelines: the technology roadmap (can it build the next generation) and capacity execution (can it deliver on time). Managing the two lines separately brings depth of expertise but blurs accountability — when something goes wrong, who is responsible?

Judging by execution from 2024–2026, the arrangement has worked well: 2nm volume production is on schedule, and Arizona, Kumamoto and Dresden are advancing simultaneously. This is the transition period of the succession plan, and also the observation window.

III. Capital allocation: all three major decisions were right

TSMC invested counter-cyclically in 5nm in 2019–2021, which was seen as aggressive at the time; it ended up winning all of Apple's and Nvidia's orders. In 2022–2024 it invested in 3nm and CoWoS, so capacity was already in place when AI demand surged. For 2026 it raised capex to USD 60–64 billion, with customers prepaying and booking capacity.

All three times it moved half a step ahead of demand. This is not luck—the basis for its judgment is customers' product roadmaps; TSMC participates at the chip definition stage and knows what customers want two years before the market does.

There is counter-evidence too: costs at the Arizona fab are far higher than in Taiwan, and in 2024 it was delayed by labor and cultural differences. But this is an "insurance premium that must be paid," not a misjudgment.

IV. Shareholder returns: modest dividends, but every dollar reinvested wisely

2026 dividend per share of RMB 24, up 33% year on year, payout ratio about 23%, dividend yield 1.0%. It looks stingy, but do the math: money kept in the company has historically earned an ROE of 25%–33%; paid out to shareholders, they would only get 8% buying an index. Not paying a dividend is the right call.

No large acquisitions — a plus. The semiconductor industry's M&A history is almost entirely value destruction. For thirty years TSMC has done one thing: build fabs, ramp yields, win the next generation of orders.

Share count 25.93 billion shares, average growth within 0.5% a year over five years (restricted stock), no large-scale dilution. For long-term shareholders, this is the most tangible form of respect.

V. Governance and succession: the only remaining question

TSMC has no actual controlling shareholder. Its largest shareholder is the Executive Yuan's National Development Fund (about 6%), and foreign investors hold roughly 70%. The nine-seat board includes independent directors. The advantage is that professional managers run the company; the drawback is the absence of a strong major shareholder to correct course.

Succession is the only open question. C.C. Wei was born in 1952 and will be 74 in 2026. TSMC's previous succession (Morris Chang's retirement in 2018) used a "chairman + president" power-sharing model that ran for six years. If one of the two co-COOs succeeds this time, watch who takes charge of customer relations and who takes charge of government relations—over the next five years, these two matter more than process technology.

Conclusion

Management verdict: 9/10. The capital-allocation record is among the best in the industry, and the honesty of communication is verifiable. Points deducted only for succession planning and governance concentration. This is a company where "you don't need to worry about management," and that in itself is very valuable.
