Moat anatomy: four legs, one crack — TSMC

Technology, switching costs, scale and trust: four pillars underpin a 67.7% gross margin

Core view

TSMC's moat rests on four pillars: technology leadership, customer switching costs, scale and capital barriers, and customer trust. Each reinforces the others, forming a structure a challenger "must close all at once." Only one crack exists—geopolitics—and overseas expansion is slowly repairing it, at the cost of a permanent 3–4 percentage point decline in gross margin.

I. First leg: technological leadership — an outcome, not a cause

2nm enters its first full year of mass production in 2026, with A16 (1.6nm) slated for risk production in 2H26 and A14 in 2028. Samsung mass-produces 2nm on a similar timeline but trails on yield, while Intel's 18A is ramping.

The technology lead is the outcome; three factors explain it: R&D and capex of about USD 40 billion a year, data accumulated along the yield learning curve, and the trust that lets customers hand over their most expensive chips. All three compound, and a challenger must close all three gaps at once. Note the "win again every year" feature — Intel once led by two generations, and a five-year 10nm delay wiped out the entire lead.

II. The Second Leg: Switching Costs, the Hardest One

An advanced-node chip takes 12–18 months from design to volume production. Design rules (PDK), IP libraries, yield models and packaging are all tied to a specific foundry's process, so switching foundries means redoing the entire validation chain.

The constraint is time and risk, not money. A customer that switches fabs during ramp-up is betting its product launch on an unproven yield curve, and no CEO makes that call unless the incumbent supplier lacks capacity.

Competition therefore turns not on whose technology is better but on who can deliver capacity when the customer needs it. TSMC raising 2026 capex to USD 60–64 billion is essentially buying an extension on this moat.

III. The Third Leg: Scale, a USD 60 billion Entry Fee

2026 capex of USD 60–64 billion, against USD 20 billion for a single 2nm fab. That number is itself the barrier to entry: globally, only a handful of companies can put USD 60 billion a year into semiconductors (TSMC, Samsung, Intel).

Scale economies show up in two places: procurement bargaining power (annual purchases from ASML and Applied Materials run several times those of peers) and capacity allocation (more than ten fab sites worldwide support one another, so yield problems at one site can be offset elsewhere).

But scale is also a shackle—once demand turns down, the USD 60 billion of depreciation remains and gross margins come under heavy pressure. This is the key risk to watch in 2027–2028.

Four, the fourth leg: trust, the hardest one to quantify

Apple's A-series and Nvidia's flagship GPUs all debut at TSMC. That is not merely a commercial choice but a relationship in which process and product are co-defined: customers participate in process development and TSMC tunes parameters to their requirements. Switching costs here are measured in years, and switching may not even deliver comparable results.

Trust also works in reverse. TSMC pledges that it does not compete with its customers: it does no chip design and builds no brands, the most fundamental difference between it and Samsung. Samsung runs memory, phones and foundry at once, so a customer handing it flagship chips is handing its roadmap to a competitor.

V. The Fracture: Geopolitics and Going Global

Taiwan capacity concentration is the one crack in this moat. Arizona, Kumamoto and Dresden fabs patch it, at the cost of a permanent 3–4 percentage point decline in gross margin (company guidance).

Why build despite the known dilution: customer requirements (US customers need supply assurance from "non-Taiwan" sources) and policy requirements (tariffs tied to subsidies). This is the cost of buying insurance, not an operational misstep. But it permanently lowers the ROE ceiling—from 33% to around 28% (rough calculation).

The moat therefore scores 41/50 overall: three of the four legs at full marks, with 7 points deducted for the crack.The probability of being breached head-on within ten years is low; the probability of being gradually circumvented is moderate.

Conclusion

Moat score: 41/50. All four legs—technology, switching costs, scale and trust—are solid; only geopolitics shows a crack that is leaking. That crack will not bring the company down, but it will permanently hold the valuation multiple one notch lower.
