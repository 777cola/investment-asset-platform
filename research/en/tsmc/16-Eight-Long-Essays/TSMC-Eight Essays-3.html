Moat anatomy: four legs, one crack — TSMC

Technology, switching costs, scale, trust: four pillars underpin a 67.7% gross margin
Research date 2026/09/11  ·  Price NT$2,410 (-1.63%)  ·  Market cap Approximately TWD 62.5 trillion (about USD 1.95 trillion)  ·  PE(TTM) Approximately 27.9x

Core view

TSMC's moat is not one but four: technology leadership, customer switching costs, scale and capital barriers, and customer trust. The four reinforce one another, forming a structure that "a challenger must close all at once." There is only one crack—geopolitics—and overseas expansion is slowly repairing it, at the cost of a permanent 3–4 percentage point decline in gross margin.

I. First leg: technological leadership, but it is an outcome, not a cause

2nm enters its first full year of mass production in 2026; A16 (1.6nm) is slated for risk production in the second half of 2026, and A14 for 2028. Rival Samsung mass-produces 2nm on a similar timeline but trails on yield, while Intel's 18A is ramping.

The technology lead is the outcome; three things explain it: R&D and capex of about USD 40 billion a year, data accumulated along the yield learning curve, and the trust that lets customers hand it their most expensive chips. All three compound, and a challenger must close all three gaps at once. Note the "win again every year" feature — Intel once led by two generations, and a five-year 10nm delay wiped out the entire lead.

II. The Second Leg: Switching Costs, the Hardest One

An advanced-node chip takes 12–18 months from design to volume production. Design rules (PDK), IP libraries, yield models and packaging solutions are all tied to a specific foundry's process. Switching to another foundry means redoing the entire validation chain.

This is not a question of money but of time and risk. A customer that switches fabs during ramp-up is betting its product launch on an unproven yield curve. No CEO would make that call unless the incumbent supplier lacks capacity.

So the real competition is not over whose technology is better, but over who can deliver capacity when the customer needs it. TSMC raising 2026 capex to USD 60–64 billion is essentially buying an extension on this moat.

III. The Third Leg: Scale, a USD 60 billion Entry Fee

2026 capex of USD 60–64 billion; a single 2nm fab starts at USD 20 billion. That number is itself the barrier to entry—globally, the companies that can put USD 60 billion a year into semiconductors can be counted on one hand (TSMC, Samsung, Intel).

The economics of scale show up in two places: procurement bargaining power (annual purchases from ASML and Applied Materials are several times those of peers), and capacity allocation (more than ten fab sites worldwide support one another, so yield problems can be offset elsewhere).

But scale is also a shackle—once demand turns down, the USD 60 billion of depreciation remains, and gross margins will be squeezed hard. This is the thing most worth guarding against in 2027–2028.

Four, the fourth leg: trust, the hardest one to quantify

Apple's A-series and Nvidia's flagship GPUs all debut at TSMC. This is not merely a commercial choice; it is a "co-defining the process" relationship — customers participate during process development, and TSMC adjusts parameters to customer requirements. The switching cost of such a relationship is measured in years, and switching may not even deliver comparable results.

Trust also has a reverse use: TSMC pledges "we do not compete with our customers." It does no chip design and builds no brands — the most fundamental difference between it and Samsung. Samsung makes memory, phones and foundry all at once; a customer handing it flagship chips is handing its roadmap to a competitor.

V. The Fracture: Geopolitics and Going Global

Concentrating capacity in Taiwan is the only crack in this moat. Building fabs in Arizona, Kumamoto and Dresden patches that crack, at the cost of a permanent 3–4 percentage point decline in gross margin (company guidance).

Why build despite the known dilution: customer requirements (US customers need supply assurance from "non-Taiwan" sources) and policy requirements (tariffs tied to subsidies). This is the cost of buying insurance, not an operational misstep. But it permanently lowers the ROE ceiling—from 33% to around 28% (rough calculation).

So the moat scores 41/50 overall: three of the four legs at full marks, with 7 points deducted for the crack.The probability of being breached head-on within ten years is low; the probability of being gradually circumvented is moderate.

Conclusion

Moat conclusion: 41/50. All four legs—technology, switching costs, scale and trust—are solid; only the geopolitical leg has a crack that is leaking. That crack will not bring the company down, but it will permanently hold its valuation multiple one notch lower.
