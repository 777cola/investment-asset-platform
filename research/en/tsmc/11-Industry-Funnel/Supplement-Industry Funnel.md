## I. Initial screening: three hard criteria eliminate four companies

First, whether advanced process nodes are in mass production with commercially viable yields. TSMC (2nm), Samsung (2nm ramp) and Intel (18A ramp) pass; SMIC (7nm-class), UMC (14nm) and GlobalFoundries (14nm) are out outright.

Second, whether the foundry business is profitable. Samsung Foundry is estimated to be loss-making (yield problems drive up costs) and Intel Foundry is clearly loss-making (still in its investment phase in 2026), so both are out. Third, whether customers are willing to entrust it with flagship products. Samsung is excluded because it competes with its customers, and Intel lacks trust. After these three tests, only TSMC remains.

## II. Comparing the remaining two: TSMC vs Samsung

Samsung is the only rival keeping pace at the process node: its 2nm volume production lags TSMC by only a little, and it has enough EUV tools. The gap is yield — on SRAM test structures TSMC is near 90%, while Samsung is estimated at around 70% (market estimate). A 20 percentage point yield gap means unit costs more than 30% higher, which is why Samsung's foundry business loses money.

The bigger problem is trust: Samsung makes its own phones, memory and chip designs, so a customer handing it a flagship chip is handing its product roadmap to a competitor. That structural barrier has gone unresolved for a decade and will be hard to fix.

A note on capacity: Samsung's 12-inch monthly capacity is close to TSMC's, but it is split between memory and logic and is hard to switch; TSMC's capacity serves logic foundry exclusively, giving it higher scheduling efficiency. This is the advantage of focus over diversification.

## III. Why It: In One Word, Yield</p>

Not "most advanced technology," because Samsung's node numbers are the same; not "cheapest," because its quotes are double UMC's; not "largest capacity," because Samsung's 12-inch capacity is not much smaller. It is yield — from the same wafer, it can cut more qualified chips.

Yield is a composite function of data, process and experience; it cannot be bought with equipment, nor poached with people. TSMC's thirty years of accumulated process-parameter libraries, the historical data of every production line, and the post-mortem of every failure form an asset that cannot be replicated. This is its true moat, and the starting point for every judgment in this report.

## IV. The conclusion from this funnel is a bit dull

The normal outcome of an industry funnel is "screen ten down to three, then pick one." In foundry, the outcome is "screen six down to one," because economies of scale and the learning curve push the industry toward extreme concentration. That is good for investors—no need to pick stocks, only to pick the price.

The downside: there is no substitute to hedge with. To invest in the manufacturing leg of AI compute, you can only buy TSMC, or buy its upstream (ASML, Applied Materials) and downstream (NVIDIA). That also explains why its valuation has always carried a premium—scarcity itself is part of the valuation.

## V. The valuation gap: why it trades at 28x and Samsung at 10x

In foundry, TSMC trades at 27.9x TTM PE, Samsung Electronics at about 10–12x, UMC at about 14x, and SMIC at about 80x (A-share premium). The gap is not market bias; it prices three things: certainty (order visibility through 2027), capital returns (ROE 33% vs 12%), and scarcity (no substitute).

SMIC's 80x follows a different logic—an A-share liquidity premium plus the domestic substitution narrative, with little connection to fundamentals. Excluding it, the reasonable PE range for foundry is 12–28x, with TSMC at the top.

This means: buying TSMC is buying "the most expensive certainty"; buying Samsung is buying "cheap cyclicality." Neither is wrong, but you need to know what you are buying.
