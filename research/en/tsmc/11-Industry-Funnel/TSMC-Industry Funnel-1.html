Core call: no need to pick a winner in this industry, because only one company is running.

Line up the world's six major foundries in a single table and screen them on five metrics: market cap, growth, gross margin, P/E and ROE. Samsung Foundry is loss-making and customers do not trust it; Intel Foundry is burning cash through its ramp; SMIC is boxed in by equipment controls; UMC and GlobalFoundries have exited advanced nodes. After this rough screen only TSMC remains — not because it was "selected", but because there is no alternative.

I. Initial screening: three hard criteria eliminate four companies

First, are the advanced nodes in mass production at commercially viable yields? TSMC (2nm), Samsung (2nm ramp) and Intel (18A ramp) pass; SMIC (7nm-class), UMC (14nm) and GlobalFoundries (14nm) fail outright.

Second, is the foundry business profitable? Samsung Foundry is estimated to be loss-making, with yield problems pushing up costs, and Intel Foundry is clearly loss-making as it remains in its investment phase in 2026; both fail. Third, are customers willing to entrust it with flagship products? Samsung is excluded because it competes with its customers, and Intel lacks trust. After these three tests, only TSMC remains.

II. Comparing the remaining two: TSMC vs Samsung

Samsung is the only rival keeping pace at the process node: its 2nm volume production lags TSMC by only a little, and it has sufficient EUV tools. The gap is yield — TSMC runs near 90% on SRAM test structures versus an estimated ~70% for Samsung (market estimate). A 20pp yield gap implies unit costs more than 30% higher, which is why Samsung's foundry business loses money.

Trust is the bigger problem: Samsung makes its own phones, memory and chip designs, so a customer handing it a flagship chip is handing its product roadmap to a competitor. That structural barrier has gone unresolved for a decade and will be hard to fix.

On capacity: Samsung's 12-inch monthly capacity is close to TSMC's, but it is split between memory and logic and is hard to switch; TSMC's capacity serves logic foundry exclusively, giving it higher scheduling efficiency. This is the advantage of focus over diversification.

III. Why It: In One Word, Yield</p>

Not "most advanced technology" — Samsung's node numbers match. Not "cheapest" — its quotes are double UMC's. Not "largest capacity" — Samsung's 12-inch capacity is not much smaller. It is yield: from the same wafer, TSMC extracts more qualified chips.

Yield is a composite of data, process and experience. It cannot be bought with equipment, nor poached with people. Thirty years of accumulated process-parameter libraries, historical data from every production line and the post-mortem of every failure form an asset that cannot be replicated. That is the true moat, and the starting point for every judgment in this report.

IV. The conclusion from this funnel is a bit dull

An industry funnel normally screens ten candidates down to three, then picks one. In foundry it screens six down to one: economies of scale and the learning curve drive the industry toward extreme concentration. That suits investors—no need to pick stocks, only to pick the price.

The downside is that there is no substitute to hedge with. To own the manufacturing leg of AI compute, you can only buy TSMC, or buy its upstream (ASML, Applied Materials) and downstream (NVIDIA). That also explains the persistent valuation premium: scarcity itself is part of the valuation.

V. The valuation gap: why TSMC trades at 28x and Samsung at 10x

In foundry, TSMC trades at 27.9x P/E (TTM), versus roughly 10–12x for Samsung Electronics, about 14x for UMC and about 80x for SMIC (A-share premium). That gap is not market bias: it prices three things — certainty (order visibility through 2027), capital returns (ROE of 33% vs 12%) and scarcity (no substitute).

SMIC's 80x follows a different logic — an A-share liquidity premium plus the domestic substitution narrative, with little connection to fundamentals. Excluding it, the reasonable P/E range for foundry is 12–28x, with TSMC at the top.

The implication: buying TSMC is buying "the most expensive certainty"; buying Samsung is buying "cheap cyclicality." Neither is wrong, but you need to know what you are buying.

Conclusion

Conclusion: TSMC wins on one ground alone—yield. Of the five peers, some have exited advanced nodes, some are climbing the ramp at a loss, and some are locked down by export controls. Scarcity earns a valuation premium—and brings one risk: when TSMC itself stumbles, there is no substitute. Cap the position size accordingly.
