Masterclass Close Read: Duan Yongping — TSMC

Will this business still be making money ten years from now?
Research date 2026/09/11  ·  Price NT$2,410 (-1.63%)  ·  Market cap Approximately TWD 62.5 trillion (about USD 1.95 trillion)  ·  PE(TTM) Approximately 27.9x
← Back to the hub

Core view

Duan Yongping never reads quarterly reports when he studies financial statements; he asks whether "this business can keep making money." In TSMC's 2026Q2 table he sees three numbers: gross margin 67.7%, net margin 55.6%, and capex at 39% of revenue. The first two show pricing power; the last shows it must keep spending. Conclusion: a good business, but value it as an asset-heavy one.

Where does he start looking?

Not revenue growth. Revenue +36% is the result; the real question is why it could grow 36%. The answer lies in the revenue mix: HPC at 66%, up +20% quarter-on-quarter. Growth is coming from customers building data centres, not from consumers replacing phones. The distinction matters — data centres are capex, with budgets, plans and contracts; phones are consumption, driven by mood.

He would then look at how the 67.7% gross margin came about. Price increases, full utilisation and product mix — all three are gifts of the cycle. He would not treat 67.7% as the norm, but as evidence of how much can be collected now, and then ask how much can be collected at the worst point. The answer is 46% in 2019 — a year when the industry was in a downturn and TSMC still did not lose money.

Where will he stop?

That line is capex: USD 60–64 billion in 2026, about 45% of revenue. Duan Yongping likes businesses that can raise prices without further investment (Moutai, Apple); TSMC is the opposite — no investment means falling behind, and investment compresses gross margin.

But he also knows heavy assets do not equal a bad business. The test is "how much you earn back per dollar invested": over the past five years TSMC has turned every USD 1 of capex into more than about USD 1.5 of incremental annual revenue, a conversion rate he accepts. So he would not reject the business, only demand a cheaper price — because he knows the ceiling of this model is that the money earned has to go back into the machines.

His conclusion

In one sentence: Understandable, holdable, but not a must-buy. He would not buy at RMB 2,410; he would buy when the market panics and the P/E falls to around 15x. He has said as much himself: good companies rarely fall, and when they do you must dare to buy. TSMC's problem is that it is too closely watched — it is hard for it to fall to the point where nobody wants it.

Put another way: if TSMC's share price did not move for five years, what would you think? Duan Yongping would probably say — that is just fine; I collect my 1% dividend each year, the company earns 8% more each year, after five years its intrinsic value is up 40%, and the market will recognize it sooner or later. This mindset is the only workable one for holding asset-heavy companies. If you cannot do this, it means what you bought is not this business but the price action of this stock.

Conclusion

Duan Yongping-style verdict: business 9 out of 10, price 5 out of 10. Wait for 15x P/E, around RMB 1,900, before revisiting; alternatively hold a very small long-term position and do not churn.
