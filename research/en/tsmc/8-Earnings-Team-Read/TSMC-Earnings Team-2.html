Masterclass Close Read: Buffett — TSMC

Moat, return on capital, management — all three must be right
Research date 2026/09/11  ·  Price NT$2,410 (-1.63%)  ·  Market cap Approximately TWD 62.5 trillion (about USD 1.95 trillion)  ·  PE(TTM) Approximately 27.9x

Core view

Reading this earnings report, Buffett would look at ROE first, then capex, and finally shareholder returns. All three pass: ROE of about 33%, capex underpinned by customer prepayments, and dividends raised year after year. His hesitation would be only about price—a 22.7x forward PE for a company that must keep reinvesting heavily sits at the top of the range, not the bottom.

Moat: the second-hardest he has ever seen

He would compare TSMC with Coca-Cola and See's Candies, then spot a key difference: consumer moats rest on mindshare, TSMC's moat rests on physics. A physical moat is harder — consumers can switch to another soda, but customers cannot casually replace a foundry that can hit 90% yield.

He bought TSMC ADRs in 2022 and sold within a quarter, on the grounds that "the geopolitical location doesn't fit." That reason still holds today, and costs more — because moving capacity overseas takes over USD 100 billion and permanently dilutes gross margin by 3–4 percentage points. So if he bought again today, he would factor in the geopolitical discount and demand a lower PE.

Return on capital: the number he actually cares about

ROE of 33% is an outlier in manufacturing. He would press: can this number hold? The answer depends on gross margin, gross margin depends on process leadership, and process leadership depends on capex. Chained together, these three form a loop that "must keep running."

He likes the leader inside such a loop (GEICO in auto insurance, BNSF in rail), because the leader's return on capital is high enough to fund itself. TSMC qualifies — Q2 operating cash flow of roughly RMB 95 billion (rough calculation) and capex of roughly RMB 49.5 billion; it earns enough to cover its own spending. This is the key: a capital-intensive company that needs no financing is the only good capital-intensive company.

How would he value it?

He would not run a precise DCF; he would use "a reasonable PE range × normalized earnings." How to derive normalized earnings? Not the RMB 106 for 2026 (cycle peak), nor the 2019 low, but a mid-cycle assumption of "58% gross margin, 10% annual revenue growth," corresponding to EPS of about RMB 85–90.

At 20x, fair value is RMB 1,700–1,800; at 22x, RMB 1,870–1,980. The current RMB 2,410 is more than 20% above the upper end of his "fair value range." So he would say: good company, wait for the price.

Conclusion

Buffett-style conclusion: a perfect moat, but the price is 20% too expensive. His entry point is below RMB 1,800 (20x normalized EPS), at which level it would be a very comfortable position.
