Edited and polished: a publish-ready earnings analysis — TSMC

Cut the jargon and explain it to a friend who understands investing but not semiconductors

Core view

TSMC just delivered its best quarterly report ever, then said itself that "gross margin will decline next quarter." Put those two things together and you have the entire message of this earnings report. The full analysis follows.

Look at three numbers first.

First number: revenue of TWD 1.27 trillion, up 36% year on year. Second number: gross margin of 67.7%, up 9 percentage points from a year earlier. Third number: net profit of TWD 706.56 billion, up 77% year on year, with earnings per share of TWD 27.25.

Net profit growth is twice revenue growth — that is operating leverage: revenue rose 36%, costs barely moved, and the difference flowed straight to profit. This happens only when capacity is fully loaded, and only at the top of the cycle.

Where did the growth come from

Not phones. The phone business actually fell 4% quarter on quarter and now accounts for only 22%. All the growth came from HPC (high-performance computing), which accounts for 66% and rose 20% quarter on quarter—that is Nvidia, AMD, and the cloud providers building their own chips.

This shift matters. Phone orders run on a one-year cycle and get cut fast; compute orders are planned three years out and require prepaid capacity. As the customer base shifts from "consumer" to "infrastructure," the business becomes more stable, but its dependence on AI capex rises too.

Why gross margin has peaked

The company's own guidance for the third quarter is 65%–67%, a midpoint of 66%, 1.7 percentage points below the second quarter. The reasons are clear: the new 2-nanometer process is ramping, initial yields are imperfect, diluting 3–4 percentage points; and the high costs of new overseas fabs dilute a further 2–3 percentage points.

Both reasons amount to "trading short-term margin for long-term capacity," which is not a bad thing. But the market needs to get used to one thing: revenue will keep hitting new highs, while profit growth will fall first. History shows this every time—profit peaks one to two quarters before revenue does.

Two more figures to look at

One is capex, raised from USD 52–56 billion to USD 60–64 billion for 2026. Customers are pushing for it, not the company choosing to expand—a near-term positive. But depreciation will hit concentrated in 2027–2028, adding about USD 13 billion a year, and that is the source of gross margin pressure next year and the year after.

The other is August revenue of RMB 514.806 billion, up 53.3% year on year, breaking RMB 500 billion for the first time and marking a fourth consecutive month of record highs. The number shows the third quarter will not be weak. What really matters is the month-on-month trend in September and October—whether it can hold above RMB 500 billion says more about whether the AI orders are real than the year-on-year growth rate does.

So what should be done now

Run the numbers. 2026 EPS is roughly RMB 106; at the current share price of RMB 2,410, that is 22.7x. Assume 2027 EPS of RMB 125 on 21x, and you get RMB 2,625, up 9%. Assume AI capex stalls, 2027 EPS of just RMB 95 on 18x, and you get RMB 1,710, down 29%.

Up 9%, down 29% — that risk/reward does not justify a new position. But nor should you sell — because the bear case requires AI capex to brake abruptly, and all current order data contradict that. So the answer is: hold what you already own; wait for below RMB 2,000 to buy what you don't.

RMB 2,000 corresponds to 19x 2026 and 16x 2027. That price would mean the market has begun discounting cyclical risk, and only then does a margin of safety emerge.

Two more numbers. Dividend: RMB 24 per share in 2026, a 1.0% yield and 23% payout, and management says it will rise again in 2027. That dividend cannot support the valuation, but it does prove the cash flow is real. Share count: 25.93 billion shares, barely changed in five years, with no dilution through share issuance. For a company that spends USD 60 billion a year, neither is easy.

So the conclusion can be put more bluntly: if what you want is an asset that is still here in ten years and earns more each year, TSMC fits; if what you want is to buy now and double in three years, it does not. The difference is not the company — it is your time horizon.

Conclusion

In one sentence: this is the world's most certain AI business, and also the most expensive certainty. A good company is not the same as a good price; TSMC at 2,410 is suited to holding, not chasing.
