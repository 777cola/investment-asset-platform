# TSMC（2330.TW）— Duan Yongping Supplement

- Research date：2026/09/11
- Price：2410.0 TWD
- Market cap：Approximately TWD 62.5 trillion (about USD 1.95 trillion)

## Notes

Rough-estimate method: based on 2031 net profit of TWD 4.4 trillion (TWD 3.24 trillion in 2027, then growing 8% annually), and an exit PE of 15x, that gives a market cap of TWD 66 trillion ÷ 25.93 billion shares = TWD 2,545 per share. This is a rough retail calculation, not a precise forecast. For the definitional issue with the "TWD 1.5 trillion over five years" figure in the working papers, see m03.

## Question 1: Is this a good business?

Good. Good because of pricing power. Gross margin was 67.7% in Q2 2026; no other foundry can reach 50%. Customers queue up to prepay for capacity; the company does not need to chase orders. This is the highest form of a business — not begging others to buy, but others begging you to sell.

But there is one demerit: the money it earns must be continuously reinvested in machines. 2026 capex is USD 60–64 billion, 45% of revenue. This kind of business is called "must keep running" — slow down and you fall behind. One notch below Moutai, three notches above most manufacturing.

## Question 2: Will it still be around in ten years?

It will. As long as humanity wants faster computing, someone will need to make transistors smaller. That demand will not disappear; it will only fluctuate with cycles. A decade from now, TSMC will most likely still be global number one, though its share may fall from 70% to 60% and gross margin from 67.7% back to 55%.

The real uncertainty is not whether it survives, but whether it still makes money. If customers all learn to build their own chips, or technology roadmaps bypass advanced logic processes, its toll-booth position will depreciate. That probability is not high, but it cannot be treated as zero.

## Question 3: Can you understand it?

Yes, I understand it. The revenue model in one sentence: build fabs, ramp yield, charge per wafer. The cost structure is simple too: equipment depreciation + materials + electricity + labor. There are only three metrics to judge: monthly revenue, gross margin, capex. These three numbers are published every month and every quarter, so there is no information asymmetry.

What I don't understand is the technical detail — GAA, backside power delivery, the A16 yield curve. But none of that affects the investment judgment, because you can understand "whether customers are willing to pay," and that is enough. Duan Yongping said that understanding a business is not about understanding the technology, it is about understanding the business.

He would add his own standard: understanding means "being able to judge whether it will earn more money ten years from now than it does today." For TSMC, my answer is "more, but growth will slow from 30% to around 10%." That judgment does not require understanding GAA process technology, only two things — compute demand is still growing, and there is no substitute. All the remaining detail is just a way to give yourself peace of mind.

## Question 4: At what price to buy?

Rough estimate: if TSMC earns TWD 4.4 trillion in 2031 (TWD 3.24 trillion in 2027, then 8% annual growth), at 15x that is a TWD 66 trillion market cap, divided by 25.93 billion shares, TWD 2,545 per share. It is now TWD 2,410 — a 5.6% gain over five years, plus 1% dividend, only 2% annualized.

That is not the return he wants. His standard is roughly "double in five years" (15% annualized), which implies a purchase price of TWD 1,270, or higher earnings in 2031. So he will not buy; he will wait. He waits for market panic to push PE down to 12–15x, that is, TWD 1,500–1,900.

## Question 5: What is the biggest fear?

The biggest fear is not Samsung catching up, nor Intel turning itself around—both would take a decade and would most likely fail. The biggest fear is "buying the best company at the highest point."

The cost of this kind of mistake is not losing money, it is losing time. Buy at 2,410 yuan, and if it returns to 2,000 yuan three years later, you have not only lost 17%, you have also missed other opportunities. Duan Yongping often says the biggest cost of investing is opportunity cost, not paper losses.

Another of his old sayings sums it up: do the right thing, and do things right. Buying TSMC is "the right thing"; buying at 2,410 yuan is "getting the price wrong." Look at the two separately, and you will not ignore price because you are bullish on the company.

## Question 6: What if I can't hold on?

If you can't hold it, don't buy too much. His approach is to "buy to a position size you can sleep with" — if you lose sleep over stock price swings, the position is too large, not the thesis wrong. TSMC's volatility runs above 30% annualized; with a 10% position, a 30% drawdown within a year is a normal event.

Another approach is to shift attention from the share price to the business: look at monthly revenue, quarterly gross margin. Whether the business is getting better or worse matters a hundred times more than whether the stock goes up or down.If you can answer "is this business better this year than last year," you can hold it.

## Question 7: If you could buy only one company in the entire semiconductor industry, which would it be?

Buy TSMC. The reason is not that it is the cheapest, but that it is the hardest to replace. Every other company in this industry has a clear rival: Nvidia has AMD and in-house chips, ASML has Canon and Nikon (though they cannot catch up), Samsung has SK hynix and Micron, Intel has AMD and the ARM camp. TSMC's rivals are all a decade or more behind.

But "hardest to replace" does not equal "most worth buying." If you could only buy one and had to buy now, Duan Yongping would probably say: then wait, wait until the price is right. He does not mind holding cash; he minds buying at the wrong price. "Better to miss out than to get it wrong" fits perfectly here.
