Duan Yongping — TSMC

TSMC (2330.TW) | Duan Yongping
Research date 2026/09/11  ·  Price NT$2,410 (-1.63%)  ·  Market cap Approximately TWD 62.5 trillion (about USD 1.95 trillion)  ·  P/E (TTM) Approximately 27.9x
← Back to the hub

Core call: a ten-year hold, but not a buy today.

Ask the six questions the way Duan Yongping would: Is it a good business? Will it still be here in ten years? Do I understand it? At what price do I buy? What do I fear most? What if I can't hold on? The first three answers are all positive; the fourth is a letdown — on his rough estimate, the five-year annualised return is only 2%–5%, below his threshold. Conclusion: a good company; wait for the price, or hold a small position and leave it alone.

Question 1: Is this a good business?

Good — and good because of pricing power. Gross margin reached 67.7% in Q2 2026, a level no other foundry can approach, let alone exceed 50%. Customers queue up to prepay for capacity, so the company does not need to chase orders. That is the highest form of a business: not begging others to buy, but having others beg you to sell.

One demerit, though: the money it earns must be continuously reinvested in machines. 2026 capex is USD 60–64 billion, or 45% of revenue. Call it a "must keep running" business — slow down and you fall behind. One notch below Moutai, three notches above most manufacturing.

Question 2: Will it still be around in ten years?

It will. So long as humanity wants faster computing, someone will need to make transistors smaller. That demand will not disappear; it will only fluctuate with cycles. A decade from now, TSMC will most likely still be global number one, though its share may fall from 70% to 60% and gross margin from 67.7% back to 55%.

The real uncertainty is not whether it survives, but whether it still makes money. If customers all learn to build their own chips, or technology roadmaps bypass advanced logic processes, its toll-booth position will depreciate. That probability is not high, but it cannot be treated as zero.

Question 3: Can you understand it?

Yes, I understand it. The revenue model in one sentence: build fabs, ramp yield, charge per wafer. The cost structure is equally simple: equipment depreciation, materials, electricity, labour. Only three metrics matter: monthly revenue, gross margin and capex. All three are published monthly and quarterly, so there is no information asymmetry.

What I don't understand is the technical detail — GAA, backside power delivery, the A16 yield curve. None of it affects the investment judgment, because you can understand "whether customers are willing to pay," and that is enough. As Duan Yongping said, understanding a business is not about understanding the technology, it is about understanding the business.

He would add his own standard: understanding means "being able to judge whether it will earn more money ten years from now than it does today." For TSMC, my answer is "more, but growth will slow from 30% to around 10%." That judgment does not require understanding GAA process technology, only two things — compute demand is still growing, and there is no substitute. All the remaining detail is just a way to give yourself peace of mind.

Question 4: At what price to buy?

Rough math: TWD 4.4 trillion of earnings in 2031 (TWD 3.24 trillion in 2027, then 8% annual growth) at 15x gives a TWD 66 trillion market cap; on 25.93 billion shares that is TWD 2,545 per share. Against the current TWD 2,410, that is 5.6% upside over five years, or 2% annualised including a 1% dividend.

Not the return he wants. His hurdle is roughly "double in five years" (15% annualised), which implies either a TWD 1,270 entry price or higher 2031 earnings. So he will not buy; he will wait. He waits for market panic to compress the P/E to 12–15x, i.e. TWD 1,500–1,900.

Question 5: What is the biggest fear?

The biggest fear is not Samsung catching up, nor Intel turning itself around—both would take a decade and would most likely fail. It is buying the best company at the highest point.

The cost of this kind of mistake is not losing money, it is losing time. Buy at 2,410 yuan, and if it returns to 2,000 yuan three years later, you have not only lost 17%, you have also missed other opportunities. Duan Yongping often says the biggest cost of investing is opportunity cost, not paper losses.

Another of his old sayings sums it up: do the right thing, and do things right. Buying TSMC is the right thing; buying at 2,410 yuan is getting the price wrong. Look at the two separately, and you will not ignore price because you are bullish on the company.

Question 6: What if I can't hold on?

If you can't hold it, don't buy too much. His rule is to "buy to a position size you can sleep with": if share price swings keep you awake, the position is too large — the thesis isn't wrong. TSMC's volatility runs above 30% annualised, so with a 10% position a 30% drawdown within a year is a normal event.

Alternatively, shift your attention from the share price to the business: monthly revenue, quarterly gross margin. Whether the business is getting better or worse matters a hundred times more than whether the stock goes up or down.If you can answer "is this business better this year than last year," you can hold it.

Question 7: If you could buy only one company in the entire semiconductor industry, which would it be?

Buy TSMC — not because it is the cheapest, but because it is the hardest to replace. Every other company in this industry has a clear rival: Nvidia has AMD and in-house chips, ASML has Canon and Nikon (though neither can catch up), Samsung has SK hynix and Micron, Intel has AMD and the ARM camp. TSMC's rivals are all a decade or more behind.

But "hardest to replace" does not equal "most worth buying." If you could only buy one and had to buy now, Duan Yongping would probably say: then wait, wait until the price is right. He does not mind holding cash; he minds buying at the wrong price. "Better to miss out than to get it wrong" fits perfectly here.

Conclusion

Conclusion: a good business, easy to understand, still here in ten years — but the price offers no opportunity. Rough estimate of 2%–5% annualised over five years, below his hurdle. Action: don't buy; wait for RMB 1,500–1,900; or hold a small 3%–5% position for "participation" without affecting the portfolio.
