## I. Position in the Industry Chain: The Pricing Benchmark for the Chain

Upstream equipment suppliers quote prices to it; downstream design houses accept its quotes. It is the pricing benchmark for the entire chain, and that position is not bought with scale—it is bought by having no substitute. ASML sells EUV to others at the same price, but only TSMC can run it at 90% yield; Nvidia's chips can be made elsewhere too, but a year later and 30% more expensive.

Its role in the chain is therefore that of bottleneck owner: the bottleneck sits with it, so profit flows to it. That fully explains the 67.7% gross margin in 2026Q2, and also why Q3 guidance steps back to 66%—during the 2nm ramp, the bottleneck temporarily becomes its own execution problem.

## II. Supply chain: equipment and materials are the real vulnerability

TSMC's supply chain has three single points: EUV lithography tools (ASML is the sole supplier), interposer materials for advanced packaging (Japanese suppliers), and specialty gases and photoresists (mostly Japanese). A cutoff in any one of them would halt advanced-node production. This is not a theoretical risk: when Japan restricted photoresist exports to South Korea in 2019, Samsung's lines were strained for six months.

TSMC's response has been multi-sourcing, inventory building and cultivating local suppliers. But there is no second source for EUV—that constraint is structural. It is also why TSMC sits on ASML's shareholder register (a small, strategic stake bought in 2024): the move shows the company knows exactly where the risk lies.

## III. Moat scoring: why network effects score only 3

Foundry has no platform effect. A customer using TSMC gains nothing extra from the presence of other customers, unlike an operating system or a payment network. That moat is worth 10 points for an internet company and close to zero for a manufacturer. The 3 points reflect the ecosystem that does exist: IP vendors, EDA tools and packaging and testing houses all adapt to TSMC's processes, creating a degree of "supporting inertia."

Conversely, lacking this moat is not fatal, because the other four are solid enough. TSMC's moat rests on four legs: technology, switching costs, scale and trust. It can stand on three.

## Four, the most dangerous rival: Intel, but not now

Samsung's 2nm still trails by one to two years on yield, and equipment controls lock SMIC at the 7nm-class node; neither is a near-term threat. Intel, backed by the US government and ramping 18A, is the only rival that could reshape the landscape after 2028—but it lacks customer trust and scale, and both take time to earn.

The real worry is not rivals but customers building their own capacity: Nvidia and Apple are both evaluating moving some mature-node orders elsewhere. That will not shake the leading-edge position, but it will thin the buffer in capacity utilization.The moat's biggest fear is not being breached, but being bypassed.

One more point: what reshapes the landscape may not be any single company but "alternative routes in advanced packaging." If glass substrates or panel-level packaging mature by 2028, CoWoS's exclusivity will erode—a risk that deserves closer watching than either Samsung or Intel.

## V. Three concrete pieces of evidence on bargaining power

Evidence 1: 2026 2nm quotes are 30%–50% higher than 3nm, and customers are accepting them. That is rare in foundry history: a new node normally launches on low yields and high prices, and customers push back; this time they are paying up front to lock in capacity.

Evidence 2: Customers prepay capacity deposits, and contract liabilities grow. Payment on delivery is the foundry norm, so prepayment signals that scarcity sits with the seller.

Evidence 3: Price increases do not cost share. TSMC's 2026 share is about 70%, above 2024 levels; conventional commercial logic says price increases lose share, yet here they did not. Taken together, the three pieces of evidence show its pricing power is structural, not a temporary gift from the cycle.

## VI. Quantifying supply-chain risk

Three single points of failure: EUV lithography tools (ASML is the sole supplier, with an 18-month lead time), advanced packaging interposer materials (mainly Japanese suppliers) and specialty gases and photoresists (mainly Japanese suppliers). Disruption at any one of them halts advanced-node production. This is not a theoretical risk—when Japan restricted photoresist exports to Korea in 2019, Samsung's lines ran tight for six months.

TSMC's response is "multi-sourcing + safety stock + cultivating local suppliers," alongside strategic investments in key equipment vendors. But there is no second supplier of EUV—this is structural. For investors, that makes "supply chain disruption" a risk diversification cannot eliminate; only position sizing can.
