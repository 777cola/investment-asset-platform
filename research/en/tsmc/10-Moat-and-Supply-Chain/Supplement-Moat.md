## I. Position in the Industry Chain: It Is the Pricing Benchmark for the Chain

Upstream equipment suppliers quote prices to it; downstream design companies accept its quotes—it is the pricing benchmark for the entire chain. That position is not bought with scale; it is bought by having no substitute. ASML sells EUV to others at the same price, but only TSMC can run it to 90% yield; Nvidia's chips can be made by others too, but a year later and 30% more expensive.

Its role in the chain is therefore "owner of the bottleneck." The bottleneck sits with it, so profit flows to it. That fully explains the 67.7% gross margin in 2026Q2, and also why Q3 guidance falls back to 66%—during the 2nm ramp, the bottleneck temporarily becomes its own execution problem.

## II. Supply chain: equipment and materials are the real vulnerability

TSMC's supply chain has three single points: EUV lithography machines (ASML exclusive), interposer materials for advanced packaging (Japanese), and specialty gases and photoresists (mostly Japanese). A cutoff in any one would halt advanced-node production. This is not a theoretical risk—when Japan restricted photoresist exports to South Korea in 2019, Samsung's lines were strained for six months.

TSMC's response is "multi-sourcing + inventory + cultivating local suppliers." But there is no second source for EUV—that is structural. This is also why it appears on ASML's shareholder register (it bought a small stake in 2024, strategic in nature); the move shows it knows exactly where the risk lies.

## III. Moat scoring: why network effects score only 3

Foundry has no platform effect. A customer using TSMC does not gain extra benefit because other customers also use it—unlike an operating system or a payment network. This moat is worth 10 points for an internet company and close to zero for a manufacturer. The 3 points reflect that the ecosystem does exist: IP vendors, EDA tools and packaging and testing houses all adapt to TSMC's processes, creating a degree of "supporting inertia."

Conversely, lacking this moat is not fatal—because the other four are solid enough. TSMC's moat rests on four legs: technology, switching costs, scale and trust. It can stand on three.

## Four, the most dangerous rival: Intel, but not now

Samsung's 2nm still trails by one to two years on yield, and SMIC is locked at the 7nm-class node by equipment controls; neither is a threat near term. Intel, with US government backing and 18A ramping, is the only rival that could reshape the landscape after 2028—but it lacks customer trust and scale, and both take time to earn.

The real worry is not rivals but customers building their own capacity: Nvidia and Apple are both evaluating moving some mature-node orders elsewhere. That will not shake the leading-edge position, but it will thin the buffer in capacity utilization.The moat's biggest fear is not being breached, but being bypassed.

One more point: what reshapes the landscape may not be any single company but "alternative routes in advanced packaging." If glass substrates or panel-level packaging mature by 2028, CoWoS's exclusivity will erode—this risk deserves closer watching than either Samsung or Intel.

## V. Three concrete pieces of evidence on bargaining power

Evidence 1: 2026 2nm quotes are 30%–50% higher than 3nm, and customers accept them. This is rare in foundry history—normally a new node starts with low yields and high prices, and customers push back on price; this time customers are proactively paying up to lock in capacity.

Evidence 2: Customers prepay capacity deposits (contract liabilities grow). The foundry norm is payment on delivery; prepayment shows scarcity sits with the seller.

Evidence 3: Price increases do not affect share. TSMC's 2026 share is about 70%, higher than in 2024—normal commercial logic says price increases lose share; it did not. Taken together, the three pieces of evidence show its pricing power is not a temporary gift from the cycle but structural.

## VI. Quantifying supply-chain risk

Three single points of failure: EUV lithography tools (ASML sole supplier, 18-month lead time), advanced packaging interposer materials (mainly Japanese suppliers), and specialty gases and photoresists (mainly Japanese suppliers). Disruption at any one halts advanced-node production. This is not a theoretical risk—when Japan restricted photoresist exports to Korea in 2019, Samsung's lines ran tight for six months.

TSMC's response is "multi-sourcing + safety stock + cultivating local suppliers," alongside strategic investments in key equipment vendors. But there is no second supplier of EUV—this is structural. For investors, this means "supply chain disruption" is a risk that diversification cannot eliminate; only position sizing can.
