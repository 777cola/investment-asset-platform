## Rule 1 ROE: 25% ten-year average, showing it does not rely on a single boom year

The 25% ten-year average ROE is the single most informative line in this table. Semiconductors is a deeply cyclical industry, and most companies average 10%–15% ROE over ten years because they lose money in down years. TSMC's worst year, 2019 (industry inventory correction, falling 7nm utilization), still saw ROE of around 20%, rising to 33% in 2026.

This shows its high returns are not a one-off gift from the cycle but structural: once a fab is built, depreciation is fixed, and as utilization rises the marginal cost of incremental revenue is extremely low. So in a downturn it holds up on cost, and in an upturn it amplifies through pricing power.The 8% threshold is completely irrelevant here—it is not a pass, it is three times over the bar.

Compare with peers: Samsung Electronics averages about 12% ROE over ten years, Intel about 15% (lower including the loss-making years of 2024–2026), UMC about 12%, SMIC about 3%–5%. TSMC's 25% ten-year average is more than double this group. The gap is not leverage—TSMC's equity multiplier is only about 1.1x, the lowest among peers—but net margin. On the same capital it earns more, because its prices are higher and its yields better.This is the only metric in the entire table that the cycle cannot explain.

## The second free cash flow: the only line you need to watch

Only this one of the seven criteria is debatable. TSMC's operating cash flow is strong (five-year average is 1.4–1.7x net income, given the large depreciation base), but capex is equally aggressive: USD 60–64 billion in 2026, about TWD 1.9–2.0 trillion at an exchange rate of 32, equivalent to 70% of full-year net income.

The result is free cash flow that is consistently positive but thin. Cumulative free cash flow over the five years 2022–2026 is roughly TWD 300 billion, enough to pay dividends (about TWD 620 billion distributed in 2026) and retain cash, but not enough to support large-scale buybacks. This is not a financial problem but the nature of the business: TSMC's cash must go back into the machines to sustain its lead. Treating this as a "pass" is fine; treating it as "excellent" is self-deception.

## Article 4 Gross margin: look at the floor, not the ceiling

67.7% is the historical peak, but this test screens for pricing power, so the number to watch is the 46% floor—even in 2019, the industry's worst year, it still held 46%, versus about 15% at UMC, about 20% at SMIC and about 15% at GlobalFoundries over the same period. In the same cycle, peers' gross margins could fall to single digits, while TSMC's floor stayed locked above 45%.

This is the hierarchy of the foundry industry: not "TSMC has high gross margins" but "TSMC's gross margins have a floor of their own." So this test passes without dispute—but a caveat is in order: extrapolating from 67.7% is dangerous; extrapolating from 55% is conservative.

## Where it falls short, and what it means when it exceeds

None that it fails. But "passing all seven" carries less information than it appears: this scorecard is a Graham-style exclusion method, built to screen out financial-engineering companies, cyclical traps and share-dilution machines. TSMC's problem is that it is too good, not too weak; this sieve has no discriminating power for it.

What actually needs judging (whether 22.7x forward PE is expensive, how many quarters a 67.7% gross margin can hold) is not in these seven criteria. So the role of this report is: confirm that TSMC will not spring a financial surprise, then put all attention on price and cyclical position. All seven passed but the price is wrong, and it is still "watch"; five of seven passed but the price is extremely cheap, and it may be a good opportunity — screening out the bad is never a reason to buy.
