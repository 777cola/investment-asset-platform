## I. C.C. Wei: Treating "conservatism" as a capability

C.C. Wei's public style can be summed up in one sentence: never promise what you cannot deliver. In the 2023 semiconductor downturn, he refused to give full-year growth guidance on the earnings call, saying only that "customers are adjusting inventory." In 2024, as AI demand first emerged, he said "the demand is real, but we need to see whether customers can get paid." When raising capex in 2026, he stressed that "depreciation will weigh on gross margin, and we need to make that clear in advance."

This communication style is rare for investors. Semiconductor management teams generally like to tell stories — Samsung talks about "overtaking," Intel talks about "returning to leadership," and both end in delays. In TSMC's earnings call records, the guidance delivery rate is above 90%, and this can be quantitatively verified.

## II. Capital allocation: eight years without a single misallocated dollar

Three major decisions since 2019 merit review. The contrarian 5nm bet of 2019–2021 looked aggressive at the time but ended up capturing all of Apple's and Nvidia's orders. The 3nm and CoWoS investment of 2022–2024 meant capacity was already in place when AI demand surged. The 2026 upward revision to USD 60–64 billion reflects customer prepayments and capacity pre-booked. All three landed half a step ahead of demand.

Counter-evidence exists: the Arizona fab's costs far exceed Taiwan's on labour, supply chain and regulation, and labour and cultural differences delayed it in 2024. But this is an "insurance premium that must be paid," not a misjudgment—customers demand non-Taiwan capacity, policy demands local manufacturing, and not building means losing orders.

## III. Integrity and governance: two stress tests

The first was the 2024–2025 overseas fab controversy: US unions accused TSMC of favouring Taiwanese employees and of falling behind schedule at its Arizona plant. TSMC responded by subcontracting part of the engineering work to local firms and hiring more locally, averting a direct confrontation.

The second was the 2025 export-control controversy over customer flows, with reports that the US was investigating whether certain chips reached restricted customers via third parties. TSMC cooperated with the investigation and tightened customer screening. Such incidents have limited substantive impact on TSMC, but they make one thing clear: geopolitics has entered the company's day-to-day operations; it is no longer a tail risk.

## IV. Governance: dispersed shareholding, no controlling shareholder

TSMC has no controlling shareholder. Its largest holder is Taiwan's National Development Fund under the Executive Yuan (roughly 6%), while foreign investors own about 70%. The nine-seat board includes independent directors. The strength of this structure is that professional managers run the company without interference from a single major shareholder; the weakness is that when management errs, no powerful shareholder exists to correct it.

In practice, checks and balances come from customers and the market—customers vote with orders, the market votes with the share price. Historically this mechanism has worked well; when C.C. Wei took over as chairman in 2024, the market showed no governance concerns.

## V. Succession: the only remaining question

C.C. Wei was born in 1952 and turns 74 in 2026. TSMC's succession path is already laid out: co-COOs Y.J. Mii (R&D) and Y.P. Chyn (manufacturing), both promoted in 2024, form a classic dual-leader arrangement—one runs the technology roadmap, the other runs global capacity.

The upside is complementarity; the downside is blurred lines of authority. TSMC's own precedent—Morris Chang's 2018 retirement—was a split model, with C.C. Wei as president and Mark Liu as chairman, which ran for six years. If the company again runs dual COOs and then promotes one of them, two things bear watching: who owns the customer relationships (Apple, Nvidia), and who owns the government relationships (US, Japan, Germany). Over the next five years, these two matter more than process technology.

Compensation and incentives deserve separate mention. Total remuneration to directors and managers is an extremely low share of after-tax net profit (under 0.1%); the chairman's annual pay is in the range of several hundred million New Taiwan dollars—against net profit of TWD 2.75 trillion, incentive intensity rests on industry reputation and long-term shareholding, not cash. The advantage is that management won't do foolish things for a short-term bonus; the drawback is that equity can't bind the core team especially tightly—in 2021–2022, some process executives were indeed poached by competitors.

One further governance detail: TSMC discloses related-party transactions in fine detail, listing dealings with the TSMC Education and Culture Foundation and with suppliers separately—a high standard among listed companies in Taiwan. For long-term investors, predictability of governance matters more than perfection of governance.
