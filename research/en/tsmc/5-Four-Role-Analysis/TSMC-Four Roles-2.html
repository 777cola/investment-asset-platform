Financial analysis (Buffett's perspective) — TSMC

ROE, cash flow and capital allocation: how money turns into more money

Core view

TSMC's financial profile is "three highs and one low": high ROE (about 33%), high gross margin (67.7%), high cash-flow conversion (OCF at 1.4–1.7x net profit) and low shareholder returns (payout ratio 23%, dividend yield 1.0%). This is not a financial flaw but the standard shape of an asset-heavy growth stock—capital retained in the company earns a higher return than distributing it to shareholders.

I. How ROE reached 33%

A DuPont breakdown gives net margin of 55.6% × asset turnover of about 0.55 × equity multiplier of about 1.1. None of the three factors is extreme; net margin carries ROE—rare in manufacturing, where high ROE usually comes from leverage or turnover. TSMC's comes from pricing power.

That is also its weakest point: net margin sits at the top of the ROE tree, and gross margin sits at the top of the net margin tree. A 10-percentage-point drop in gross margin takes ROE from 33% to 24% (rough calculation), dragging the valuation centre down with it. For TSMC, gross margin therefore matters more than revenue.

II. Cash flow: the earnings are real money

Operating cash flow has consistently run at 1.4–1.7x net income, for a simple reason: heavy depreciation. A USD 20 billion fab depreciated over five years carries USD 4 billion a year that never leaves the bank but is deducted from net income. TSMC's reported profit is therefore conservative, and cash looks better than earnings.

Free cash flow is another matter: capex of USD 60–64 billion in 2026 consumes 70% of operating cash flow, leaving only a thin layer of FCF. Buffett would notice this structure—it resembles a railroad (constantly buying locomotives) more than See's Candies (which spends almost nothing).So the P/E ceiling he would assign is 20–25x.

III. Capital allocation: low dividends, but every dollar is well spent

Dividend per share of RMB 24 for 2026, up 33% YoY, implies a payout ratio of about 23% and a dividend yield of 1.0%. That looks stingy, but do the math: cash retained in the business has historically earned an ROE of 25%–30% a year, whereas paid out to shareholders it would go into an index and earn only 8%. Not paying a dividend is the right call.

No large acquisitions — that is a plus. In the semiconductor industry's history, M&A has almost always destroyed value (Lenovo buying IBM's PC unit and Broadcom buying software assets are separate cases). For thirty years TSMC has done only one thing: build fabs, ramp yields, win the next generation of orders.

IV. Two hidden assets on the balance sheet

First, advance receipts and contract liabilities: cash prepaid by customers to lock in capacity sits on the balance sheet as interest-free funding and a leading indicator of demand intensity. Second, equipment depreciation lives — TSMC depreciates advanced-node equipment over shorter lives than some peers, which makes reported profit more conservative and implies a depreciation roll-off tailwind for future gross margin.

The liability side is simple: interest-bearing debt of about TWD 1 trillion against cash of about TWD 3.5 trillion, for net cash of TWD 2.5 trillion. Interest coverage exceeds 100x. The financial risk box can be ticked outright.

Conclusion

Financial conclusion: top-tier quality, with risks off the balance sheet. The real financial risk is the direction of gross margin, not debt or cash flow. One metric is enough to watch—the gap between quarterly gross margin and company guidance.
