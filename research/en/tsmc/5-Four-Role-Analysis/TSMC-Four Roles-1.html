Business analysis (Duan Yongping's perspective) — TSMC

TSMC: what the business does, why it makes money, and whether it will still be around in ten years

Core view

In one sentence: TSMC is the toll booth for global advanced computing. Customers hand over their designs, it turns silicon wafers into chips, and it charges by the wafer, with prices rising at each process node. The essence of the business is not manufacturing but "certainty that no one else can deliver." The business is understandable and will most likely still be here in ten years, but its income statement is cyclical and cannot be valued like a bond.

I. What exactly does it sell

TSMC does not sell wafers; it sells yield. Give the same design to TSMC and 2nm can run at 90% yield; give it to anyone else and it may run at 60%, or not at all. A 30-percentage-point yield gap doubles per-chip cost, and that is precisely why customers pay a premium.

The price structure is clear: a 3nm wafer costs about USD 20,000, while 2nm is reportedly about USD 30,000 (media figures; the company does not confirm), 30%–50% more than the prior generation. Customers do not haggle, because what they save is not money but time to market — shipping three months late in the AI accelerator market means losing a generation.

Pricing power in this business therefore rests not on cost but on scarcity. The 67.7% gross margin in Q2 2026 is a direct reading of that scarcity.

2. Customer mix: from a handset company to a computing-power company

2026Q2 revenue mix: HPC 66%, smartphones 22%, IoT 5%, automotive 4%, digital consumer 1%. Three years ago smartphones were still the largest platform; they now sit at 22% and are still shrinking 4% quarter over quarter.

The shift improves business quality. Smartphone customers run on a one-year cycle, cut orders fast and squeeze pricing hard, whereas compute customers plan capex three years out, sign capacity reservation agreements and are willing to prepay. Much of the business TSMC is booking now is for 2027 capacity.

The risk sits here too: rising customer concentration. The top two customers (Apple, Nvidia) together account for more than 40% of revenue (estimated), so any shift of orders by either would show up immediately in monthly revenue.

III. Capex is both a moat and a shackle

2026 capex is USD 60–64 billion, 70%–80% of it directed to advanced nodes — about TWD 1.9–2.0 trillion, equivalent to 70% of full-year net profit. In other words, shareholders do not keep the entire profit; the money has to go back into buying machines.

The Duan Yongping-style test applies: a good business is one where spending money makes more money. Every USD 1 of capex TSMC spends generates roughly USD 1.5 or more of incremental annual revenue (a rough calculation on a cumulative 2022–2026 basis) — an excellent conversion rate among asset-heavy industries. Panels, memory and airlines are equally asset-heavy, yet convert at only 0.5–0.8.

The constraint cuts both ways: stop investing and you fall behind within two generations; over-invest and depreciation eats gross margin. This is not a business that earns while lying down — you have to keep running. That caps its fair valuation; it should not command the 30x of a consumer monopoly.

IV. Will it still be here in ten years?

Most likely yes, and most likely still number one. The reason is not technology but customer relationships: leading-edge processes are developed jointly, and TSMC is in the room during the chip-definition phase for Apple and Nvidia. Switching such a relationship costs two years, and no customer is willing to pay that.

What is uncertain is how high a margin that "number one" position can sustain. Overseas fabs (Arizona, Kumamoto, Dresden) will permanently dilute gross margin by 3–4 percentage points; competitors (Samsung, Intel) will keep pressing on price; and customers (Nvidia, Apple, cloud providers) all have an incentive to cultivate a second supplier, if only as a bargaining chip.

So TSMC a decade from now may be a company with 60% share and 55% gross margin that is still the most profitable in its field, rather than one with 70% share and 67.7% gross margin. Buy it paying for the former, not the latter.

Conclusion

Business judgement: a perfect business, but margins sit at historical extremes. Understandable and holdable, though not a free ride. Fair value at 20–25x P/E; above 25x, you are paying for a cyclical peak.
