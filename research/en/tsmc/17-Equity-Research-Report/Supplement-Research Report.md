## Chapter 1 Company Overview

Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Company (2330.TW), founded in 1987, is the world's largest wafer foundry. It has two businesses: wafer manufacturing (foundry by process node) and advanced packaging (CoWoS, SoIC, InFO). 2026Q2 revenue was TWD 1,270.38 billion, net profit after tax TWD 706.56 billion, market cap about TWD 62.5 trillion (about USD 1.95 trillion).

Capacity footprint: Taiwan (Hsinchu, Taichung, Tainan, including the main 2nm site), Arizona in the US, Kumamoto in Japan, Dresden in Germany (production starts 2027), and Nanjing in China. About 80,000 employees. Chairman and CEO C.C. Wei.

Revenue mix (2026Q2): HPC 66%, smartphones 22%, IoT 5%, automotive 4%, digital consumer 1%. Process mix: 7nm and below accounted for 77% (5nm 33%, 3nm 30%, 7nm 11%, 2nm 3%).

Ownership and governance: no controlling shareholder; the largest shareholder is Taiwan's Executive Yuan National Development Fund (about 6%), foreign investors hold about 70%, and the rest is held by Taiwanese domestic institutions and value funds. The nine-seat board includes independent directors. The stability of this structure comes from institutions rather than individuals; the 2024 chairman transition did not raise governance concerns. About 80,000 employees, of whom about 10,000 are in R&D, with R&D spending at 6%–7% of revenue.

## Chapter 2 Industry Analysis

The global semiconductor market is roughly USD 700–800 billion, with foundry accounting for about 25%. Foundry is a highly concentrated industry: TSMC about 70%, Samsung Foundry about 8%, UMC about 6%, SMIC about 5%, GlobalFoundries about 5%, and Intel Foundry under 3%.

The reason for this concentration is economies of scale and the learning curve: larger output lowers cost, lower cost makes pricing more competitive, and this positive feedback loop over a decade creates a chasm. Advanced nodes (below 7nm) are almost entirely in TSMC's hands, while mature nodes are a price war.

Cycle position: this upcycle began in the second half of 2023 and enters its third year in 2026. Both capex and gross margin are at all-time highs, a typical late-upcycle characteristic. Based on historical rhythm, the second half of 2026 to the first half of 2027 is the risk window.

Demand-side breakdown: AI servers are the only strong incremental driver in 2026, accounting for more than 40% of global semiconductor demand growth (institutional estimate). Phones and PCs are only recovering modestly, while automotive and industrial control are restocking after destocking. This means the cyclical nature of semiconductors is being driven by a single AI pole—the upside is high growth, the downside is high concentration: once AI capex turns, no other demand can pick up the slack.

The supply-side constraints must be stated clearly: capacity expansion at advanced nodes is limited by three things—EUV lithography tool lead times (18 months), cleanroom construction cycles (24 months), and the training of process engineers (over 5 years). This means that even if demand suddenly surges, supply cannot catch up within two years. This is the structural reason gross margin can reach 67.7% in 2026, and also the source of risk from concentrated capacity release after 2028.

## Chapter 3 Business Model

Revenue model: build capacity → ramp yield → charge per wafer, with prices rising at each process node. A 2nm wafer is reportedly quoted at about USD 30,000, 30%–50% higher than 3nm. Customers do not haggle because there is no substitute.

Cost structure: equipment depreciation accounts for 40%–50% (a 2nm fab costs USD 20 billion and up, depreciated over five years), materials 10%–15% (wafers, photoresist, specialty gases), and labor plus electricity about 10% combined. The business loses money below 80% utilization; at full load gross margin can reach 67%.

Four legs of the moat: technology leadership (one to two generations ahead), switching costs (12–18 months to re-tape-out), scale (USD 60–64 billion in annual capex), and customer trust (it does not compete with its customers). The crack is geopolitics: overseas expansion dilutes gross margin by 3–4 percentage points.

Customer relationships are part of the model: TSMC engages at the chip definition stage (co-development), charges development fees and tunes process parameters to customer requirements. This gives it the upper hand in pricing negotiations for the next node — customers have already committed design resources to the process, so switching costs arise before tape-out.

## Chapter 4 Financial Analysis

Profitability: 2026Q2 gross margin 67.7% (+9.1pct YoY), operating margin about 57%, net margin after tax 55.6%, ROE about 33%. The three sources of gross margin — price increases (5–6pct), full utilization (2–3pct), product mix (1–2pct) — all peak simultaneously in 2026.

Cash flow: Q2 operating cash flow about RMB 950 billion (rough calculation), capex USD 15.7 billion (about RMB 495 billion), free cash flow about RMB 455 billion, still positive. Full-year capex USD 60–64 billion, about 45% of revenue, the heaviest investment cycle in history.

Balance sheet: cash about RMB 3.5 trillion, interest-bearing debt about RMB 1 trillion, net cash RMB 2.5 trillion. Interest coverage above 100x. Contract liabilities (customer prepayments) growing, a leading indicator of demand intensity. Financial risk negligible.

Cost side supplement: R&D expense estimated at about TWD 250 billion in 2026 (6%–7% of revenue), G&A about RMB 120 billion, selling expense extremely low (concentrated customers, no promotion needed). The decline in expense ratios comes mainly from revenue dilution, not absolute cost savings — this reverses immediately when revenue growth slows, one of the concerns for 2027.

One more easily overlooked metric: return on invested capital (ROIC). With 2026 NOPAT of about TWD 2.3 trillion and invested capital (net debt + equity) of about TWD 7 trillion, ROIC is about 33%, far above the weighted average cost of capital (estimated 8%–9%). This means every yuan of new capital invested creates value, and expanding capacity is the right call — this is the core criterion for judging whether an asset-heavy company should keep investing.

## Chapter 5 Management

C.C. Wei (born 1952), Chairman and President, joined in 1998, became President in 2018 and took over as Chairman in 2024. His communication style is "never promise what you cannot deliver"; over the past eight years, earnings-call guidance has been met more than 90% of the time.

Dual COOs: Y.J. Mii (R&D, led 7/5/3/2nm) and Y.P. Chyn (manufacturing and global capacity), both promoted in 2024. CFO Wendell Huang. This is a transitional arrangement in the succession plan.

Capital allocation: 5nm in 2019–2021, 3nm and CoWoS in 2022–2024, raised to USD 60–64 billion in 2026 — all three times half a step ahead of demand. No large acquisitions; dividends raised year after year (RMB 24 in 2026, +33%); share count grew within 0.5% a year on average over five years.

Incentives and constraints: director and executive compensation is less than 0.1% of net profit; incentive intensity rests on industry reputation and long-term shareholding. This is a double-edged sword — no foolish moves for short-term bonuses, but also hard to lock in core process executives with equity. In 2021–2022 some executives were indeed poached by competitors, and the company subsequently increased the size of restricted stock grants.

## Chapter 6 Growth Drivers

Driver 1: AI compute capex. HPC accounts for 66% of revenue, up 20% QoQ, with customer order visibility extending to 2027. The four major North American cloud providers sharply raised 2026 capex, a direct source of TSMC's revenue growth.

Driver 2: ASP uplift from process upgrades. 2nm accounted for 3% of revenue in Q2, ramping rapidly in the second half and on track for a double-digit percentage in 2027; A16 (1.6nm) enters risk production in the second half of 2026. Each generation lifts ASP by more than 30%.

Driver 3: Advanced packaging. CoWoS is the second bottleneck for AI chips; capacity keeps doubling yet remains in short supply, with the market estimating gross margins above 50%. This business is not disclosed separately but is a key source of gross-margin mix improvement.

Quantifying the contribution of the three drivers: of the roughly TWD 4 trillion in 2026 revenue increment, AI-related (HPC) contributes about 70%, ASP uplift from process upgrades about 20%, and advanced packaging about 10% (estimate). The 2027 incremental structure will be similar, but the ASP uplift contribution will expand on the 2nm ramp, and the packaging contribution will rise on CoWoS capacity expansion.

## Chapter 7 Competitive Landscape

Samsung Foundry: the only rival not lagging on node (2nm in volume production), but yield is estimated 20 percentage points behind, the foundry business is loss-making, and the structural obstacle of "competing with customers" remains unresolved.

Intel Foundry: 18A ramps in 2026, backed by the US government, but lacks customer trust and scale, and the foundry business is still loss-making. The threat comes after 2028.

SMIC: locked at the 7nm-class by equipment controls, serving China's domestic demand, gross margin around 22%, not in the same market as TSMC. UMC and GlobalFoundries have exited advanced nodes and compete on price in mature nodes.

One more underappreciated competitive front: customer in-house design. Apple, Nvidia, Google and Amazon are all evaluating shifting some mature-node orders to Samsung or Intel — not to save money, but for negotiating leverage. This does not affect advanced-node share, but it does affect the utilization buffer — once demand turns down, customers flee faster than anyone, and utilization will fall more steeply than in past cycles.

## Chapter 8 Risk factors

Geopolitical risk: over 90% of advanced capacity is concentrated in Taiwan, with an annualized probability of 2%–5% and extreme impact. This is the only risk that could wipe out the investment entirely, and it cannot be eliminated through research.

Cyclical risk: if AI capex stalls in 2027, revenue growth falls to single digits, gross margin reverts to 55%–58% and EPS declines 15%, implying a 29% share-price decline (bear case: RMB 1,710).

Cost risk: overseas fabs dilute gross margin by 3–4 percentage points (company guidance), with roughly USD 13 billion per year of incremental depreciation in 2027–2028. Customer concentration risk: the top two customers account for more than 40% of revenue.

The transmission paths must be spelled out: geopolitical event → capacity disruption → customer reallocation (irreversible) → long-term share loss. Cyclical downturn → lower utilization → gross margin compression → lower valuation center. The difference between the two paths: the former is a one-time wipeout, the latter a de-rating. For holders, the former can only be defended against through position sizing; the latter can be pre-empted by trimming on observable indicators.

## Chapter 9 Earnings Forecast

2026E: revenue of about TWD 14.5 trillion (+38% YoY, rough estimate), gross margin 65%–66%, EPS of about TWD 106 (TWD 49.33 in 1H plus about TWD 57 in 2H).

2027E, three scenarios: bear case EPS TWD 95 (AI capex growth slows to 10%, gross margin 55%), base case TWD 125 (revenue +18%, gross margin 60%), bull case TWD 140 (demand extends into 2028, 2nm fully loaded).

Key assumptions: 2027 capex stays above USD 60 billion, 2nm reaches a double-digit percentage of revenue, overseas fabs dilute margin by no more than 4 percentage points. If any one falls short, EPS is cut 10%–15%.

Sensitivity: based on the 2027 base case (EPS TWD 125), every 5-percentage-point change in revenue growth moves EPS by about TWD 8; every 1-percentage-point change in gross margin moves EPS by about TWD 4.5. In other words, gross margin matters more than revenue growth — **which is why this report keeps stressing gross margin rather than revenue.**

Quarterly path: 2026Q3 revenue, extrapolated from the July–August run rate, is about TWD 1.5 trillion (+18% QoQ, rough estimate), with gross margin of 66%; in Q4, deeper 2nm ramp may pull gross margin to 64%–65%, but revenue should keep hitting new highs. Full-year EPS of TWD 106 breaks down as: Q1 22.08, Q2 27.25, Q3 about 29, Q4 about 28 (the last two quarters are estimates).

## Chapter 10 Valuation

Method: use 2027E EPS as the denominator and apply an 18–24x PE. Rationale for this range: TSMC's PE centered around 18x from 2015–2025, then shifted up to 22–27x after 2024 on the AI narrative; the asset-heavy nature caps the upper bound, while technology leadership supports the lower bound.

Target price: base case TWD 2,625 (TWD 125 × 21x), bear case TWD 1,710 (TWD 95 × 18x), bull case TWD 3,360 (TWD 140 × 24x). At the current price of TWD 2,410, this implies +9% in the base case, -29% in the bear case and +39% in the bull case.

Expected value: weighted at 30%/45%/25% probabilities, the expected price is TWD 2,548, only 5.7% above the current price. **The risk/reward does not support opening a new position.**

Cross-check: on EV/EBITDA, 2026E EBITDA is about TWD 10 trillion and enterprise value about TWD 60 trillion (market cap TWD 62.5 trillion less net cash TWD 2.5 trillion), implying 6x — within the reasonable 5–8x range for manufacturers and at the midpoint. On dividend discount, a 1.0% yield cannot support the valuation, implying the market is buying growth, not dividends. **Both lenses point to the same conclusion: not cheap, but not a bubble.**

One more point on the historical valuation range: 12–18x PE in 2016–2019 (single-digit growth), 15–25x in 2020–2023 (5G and the chip shortage), 22–30x in 2024–2026 (AI). Each upward shift came with a narrative upgrade, and each retreated when the narrative faded. **We are now in the upper band of the third cycle, and mean reversion to 18–20x is the reasonable assumption.**

## Chapter 11 Investment Recommendations

Rating: Hold. Existing positions stay; those with no position should wait for a pullback. Rationale: business quality scores 9–10 (top tier), but a 22.7x forward PE already prices in 2027 growth, leaving only 9% upside in the base case.

Entry: build the first tranche at 2,000 (19x 2026 EPS), the second at 1,800, and fill to the cap at 1,600. Exit: thesis invalidated — quarterly gross margin falls below 60%, or monthly revenue turns negative month-on-month for two consecutive months while capex is raised again.

Position: core holding 8%–12%; calculate AI exposure combined with NVIDIA and ASML to avoid looking diversified while actually being concentrated.

Advice by investor type: long-term allocators (five years or more) can hold within an 8% position and ignore short-term volatility; swing traders should wait for below 2,000, because the risk-reward at the current price is unattractive; conservative investors (who cannot tolerate a 30% drawdown) should not allocate, because annualized volatility exceeds 30%.**The same company gets different answers from different money.**

Finally, a watch list in chronological order: mid-October Q3 earnings call (actual gross margin, Q4 guidance, preliminary 2027 capex framework) → around November 10, October revenue → January 2027, final full-year capex → April 2027 Q1 earnings call (2nm share of revenue). If any two of these four checkpoints turn unfavorable, cut the position from 10% to 6%.
