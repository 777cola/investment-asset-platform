Core call: business 10 out of 10, price 5 out of 10

Five and a half of six gates passed. TSMC's business quality is top-tier in this report collection: the only large-scale mass producer of 2nm globally, 67.7% gross margin, 33% ROE, customers lining up to prepay for capacity. The first three gates are essentially uncontested. The only hesitation is the fifth gate—NTD 2,410 corresponds to 27.9x P/E (TTM) and 22.7x 2026E EPS, with the stock just 4% below its 52-week high. A good company at a reasonably expensive price, so it is a hold, not an add. Overall 50/60.

Gate one: circle of competence — 9/10

TSMC makes money from one thing: manufacturing chips designed by others, charging by wafer count and process node. Its customers are Apple, Nvidia, AMD, Qualcomm, Broadcom and MediaTek, plus cloud providers designing their own silicon — Google, Amazon and Microsoft. The revenue model has not changed in thirty years: build capacity, ramp yield, quote a price. It is understandable.

Where the money flows is equally legible: a 2nm fab starts at USD 20 billion, equipment accounts for more than 70%, depreciation runs five years, and the fab turns a profit only above 85% capacity utilization. All of this reconciles line by line against monthly revenue and earnings-call guidance. I deduct one point for the technology boundary — beyond gate-all-around (GAA) and backside power delivery, I cannot say how many more generations the transistor structure can sustain, or in which year the physical limit lands. That is uncertainty about the technology path, not the business model, so the deduction is only one point.

Stage two: good business — 10/10

Foundry is a capital-intensive, deeply cyclical grind, and by conventional logic it should not earn a 10. But TSMC's heavy assets are not the same as everyone else's: its capacity is booked by customers first, then built with capital. 2026 capex was raised from USD 52–56 billion to USD 60–64 billion, not because "we want to expand" but because customers are pushing.

By the numbers: 2026Q2 gross margin 67.7%, operating margin about 57%, net margin 55.6%, ROE about 33%. Install a set of equipment, recover the cost within the five-year depreciation period and still keep gross margin above 60% — a return profile with almost no second in manufacturing. Durability matters more: in the 2019 industry downturn its gross margin was 46%, still the highest among global foundries. The business does not lose money at its worst, and at its best it earns RMB 706.56 billion in a single quarter, so it gets full marks.

Gate three: moat — 10/10

Process leadership is an outcome, not a cause. Three things cause it: R&D and capex of about USD 40 billion a year, data accumulated along the yield learning curve, and the trust that lets customers hand over their most expensive chips. All three compound, and a challenger must close all three gaps at once.

The evidence sits in the customer list: Apple's A series and M series, Nvidia's Rubin generation, AMD's MI series — every launch is at TSMC. Samsung's 2nm is already in volume production, but it cannot win Nvidia's main training-card orders; Intel's 18A has US government backing yet still lacks scale and outside customer trust. The hardest part of the moat is switching cost: an advanced-process chip takes 12–18 months from design to volume production, and changing foundries means redoing the entire validation chain — no customer takes that risk during ramp.

Gate 4: Management — 9/10

C.C. Wei became chairman in 2024 after six years as president, making him one of the executives in TSMC's history with the deepest grasp of both process technology and customers. His style is "never promise what you cannot deliver": during the 2023 industry downturn he refused to cut 2nm R&D; in 2024 he called AI demand "real, but it depends on whether customers can get paid"; and when raising capex in 2026 he stressed at the same time that "depreciation will pressure gross margin."

Capital allocation has been restrained: the 2026 dividend is TWD 24 per share, up 33% YoY, for a payout ratio of about 23%, with the rest invested entirely in capacity; there have been no large acquisitions and no buying of bad assets for the sake of scale. The one point deducted is for governance structure — TSMC is a widely held company with "no actual controlling shareholder," and the chairman concurrently serves as president, concentrating power to a high degree that rests on institutions rather than equity constraints.

Gate 5: Margin of safety — 5/10

NTD 2,410 implies a market cap of TWD 62.5 trillion (NTD 2,410 × 25.93 billion shares, rough calculation; about USD 1.95 trillion) and 27.9x P/E (TTM). On full-year 2026 EPS of about NTD 106 (H1 49.33 plus an estimated H2 of about 57), the forward P/E is about 22.7x. That price embeds two assumptions: revenue grows another 18%+ in 2027, and gross margin holds at 60%.

Both are reasonable, but neither leaves room for error. More important is the direction of gross margin: Q3 guidance of 65%–67%, a midpoint 1.7 percentage points below Q2, with the 2nm ramp diluting 3–4 percentage points and overseas fabs a further 2–3 percentage points. Revenue is still hitting new highs while gross margin has already turned—a combination that only appears late in a cycle. The fifth gate can therefore only score 5: the company is not the problem; the price has already used up the good news.

Level six: Discipline — 7/10

TSMC is one of the easiest companies to verify: it reports consolidated revenue around the 10th of each month, guides to three items—revenue, gross margin and capex—on every quarterly earnings call, and CoWoS capacity can be corroborated from equipment suppliers' order books. The thesis rests on numbers, not narrative.

That suits holders but penalises anyone trying to profit from an information edge—the market gets the data at the same time you do, so excess returns can only come from judging where we are in the cycle. The same point is where it loses marks: verification metrics this clear mean everyone is watching the entry point, and a price below 2,000 may need a genuine piece of bad news to materialise. The discipline test here is not the company—it is whether you can wait.

Comprehensive: what the 50/60 score distribution shows

The structure is typical: business 29/30, price 5/10, discipline 7/10. That is not a score for "the company has several hard flaws" but for "the company is fine, the price is the problem."

The approach follows: those already holding stay put, since there is no reason to sell an asset that can still grow 18% in 2027 with gross margin holding at 60%; those with no position should not chase RMB 2,410 and should wait for below RMB 2,000 (about 19x 2026E P/E) or for a signal that gross margin has bottomed.The difference between these two waits: the former waits for price, the latter waits for bad news.The latter is more reliable, because when bad news arrives, the market usually hits the price first and revises expectations afterward.

Conclusion

Conclusion: Pass, 50/60, Hold. Margin of safety is the only weak link among TSMC's six tests. The triggers for adding to the position are clear: a share price below TWD 2,000, or quarterly gross margin falling to around 58% after the stock has already sold off (denominator reset). Trimming has a single trigger: capex raised again for 2027 while monthly revenue turns negative MoM — the standard signal of overcapacity.
