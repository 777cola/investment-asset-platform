## Initial screen: eliminate names with too large a technology gap

The first filter is access to advanced processes. Nikon and Canon are both out—not for lack of scale, but because the lithography segment is already settled: Nikon exited the EUV alliance and continues to cede DUV share, while Canon has talked up nanoimprint for a decade without reaching volume production. SMEE sits at the 90nm node, more than two generations behind, and is held back by export controls, so it is not near-term competition.

The second filter removes the non-pure-play equipment names: lithography is only a small slice of Canon's revenue, so owning it means owning copiers and medical equipment. NAURA is the mainstay of China's domestic substitution story, with good growth (+30%), but its technology gap and customer mix cap the upside—it earns "substitution" money, not "monopoly" money.

## Final pick: ASML vs KLA

That leaves KLA and ASML. Both hold segment monopolies, both post the industry's top-tier gross margins, and both serve the same customers. They differ on three points.

- Monopoly strength.ASML holds 100% exclusivity in EUV, with no second player; KLA holds about 50% share in metrology, contested by ASML (YieldStar), Hitachi and Applied Materials. The former is "no substitute"; the latter is "best but not the only one."

- Growth slope.ASML revenue +36% in 2026, KLA about +20%. ASML's growth is driven by capacity expansion, KLA's by rising inspection intensity.

- Valuation.ASML at 41x TTM / 31x 2027E; KLA about 30x. A 30% quality premium — reasonable, but not cheap.

## Why it: three conditions that cannot be replicated

First, physical exclusivity. A 13.5nm light source, multilayer reflective mirrors and a dual-stage wafer stage: only one engineering path worldwide can make this work, and it has taken twenty years to get there. Second, customer lock-in. Advanced-node yield parameters are tuned jointly with the equipment, so switching suppliers means rebuilding the line—a two-year minimum. Third, supply rigidity. Even with 30% capacity growth in 2027–2028, supply remains insufficient; capacity, not demand, sets the revenue ceiling—an extremely rare state for a cyclical stock.

Of these three, the first determines whether anyone can challenge it, the second ensures customers cannot leave, and the third determines how much it earns over the next two years. It is the only equipment company with all three at once.

## Why not buy now: the valuation already discounts this outcome

Adding an "odds" layer to the same funnel changes the conclusion. ASML at 31x 2027E implies 4% upside in the neutral scenario, while KLA at 30x implies roughly 10%–15% upside (rough calculation, assuming 15% EPS growth in 2027). On quality, ASML wins; on odds, KLA is slightly better.

The funnel is therefore not a "pick the best company and buy it" tool but a "pick the best company and wait for its price to enter a reasonable range" tool. ASML's reasonable range is 25x 2027E (about USD 1,300); KLA's is 25x (about 83% of the current price). Neither has reached it yet.

## Why KLA was ultimately not selected

KLA is a good company: 60% gross margin (above ASML), 60% ROE, 30x valuation. The issue is not quality but that its "monopoly hardness" is a notch weaker — it holds 50% of the metrology segment, contested by ASML's YieldStar, Hitachi and Applied Materials. A 50% share means customers have choices, and choices mean bargaining power.

Growth source is the other reason. KLA's growth comes from "rising inspection intensity" (more inspection steps per node), which is linear; ASML's comes from "capacity expansion + value-based pricing," which is step-function. In an AI capex upcycle, the latter offers greater elasticity.

The funnel's conclusion: ASML for quality, KLA for odds. For a single equipment name held for ten years, choose ASML; to profit from this cycle alone, KLA offers better value for money.

## Beyond the funnel: why not AMEC or ACM Research

Several domestic equipment names merit attention: AMEC's etching (5nm validation passed), Shengmei's cleaning and Piotech's thin-film deposition. The problem is that each is a single-segment player with customers concentrated in China and is an inverse beneficiary of export controls — a growth logic that is the exact opposite of ASML's. ASML earns from a global monopoly; they earn from substitution once blocked.

The two cannot be compared in the same funnel because their valuation anchors differ: domestic equipment stocks are priced on "dream multiples" (above 35x, on substitution upside), while ASML is priced on discounted cash flow (31x, on global demand). If allocating, allocate separately — do not treat them as substitutes for each other.
