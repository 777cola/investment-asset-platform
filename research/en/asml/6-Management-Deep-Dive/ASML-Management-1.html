Core call: three years of converting monopoly advantage into cash

Fouquet took over in April 2024 and has got three things right in two and a half years: locking in capacity, pushing value-based pricing, and large buybacks. The capital allocation record is clean — no big M&A, cash goes to buybacks and dividends, and the only "non-core" investment was the EUR 1.3 billion stake in Mistral AI in 2025. Governance follows the Dutch two-tier system, with an independent supervisory board. The real concern is not integrity but the handover of technical leadership: once Van den Brink's generation of engineers retires, the company shifts from engineer-driven to manager-driven.

Power structure: a smooth but telling handover

Peter Wennink retired in April 2024 and Christophe Fouquet succeeded him as CEO, with Martin van den Brink stepping down as President/CTO. Their joint departure ended ASML's decade-long dual-leadership system — one leader running the business, the other running the technology. Fouquet, who is French, joined from Applied Materials in 2008 and previously headed the EUV business; he is the type who understands technology but operates more like a manager.

The significance of this handover is that the company shifts from engineer-led to manager-led. For a business that makes its living at the limits of physics, that is a change worth watching — not a bad thing, but part of the moat (technical intuition) is no longer guarded by founder-grade engineers.

One further detail on the power structure deserves attention: under Dutch-style governance, ASML's CEO holds considerable power — major technology roadmaps are proposed by management and approved by the supervisory board, with almost no day-to-day interference. This structure worked well in the Wennink era because it was paired with a strong CTO. Now the CTO seat is empty and decision-making power is concentrated in the CEO alone — more efficient, but slower to correct course.

Capital allocation: no major M&A; all cash returned to shareholders

The record is clean. All four historical acquisitions (Cymer, HMI, Berliner Glas, Zeiss stake) were vertical integration; not one was "buying revenue." M&A essentially stopped after 2020, with cash redirected to buybacks and dividends.

In Q3 2026, single-quarter buybacks totaled EUR 4.1 billion alongside an interim dividend of EUR 1.88 per share, yet cash still rose by EUR 5.15 billion — a case of "more money than it can spend." Buffett would approve of this allocation: absent high-return targets, buybacks are the only honest move.

The one question mark is the September 2025 Mistral AI deal: EUR 1.3 billion for 11%, with no direct link to the lithography core business. Management frames it as "understanding AI customer needs," but it looks more like a financial investment. The stake already generated EUR 500 million in mark-to-market gains in Q3 — good-looking, but unsustainable, and it should not count toward operating capability.

A defensible read exists from another angle: ASML's customers are treating AI as the core of the next demand cycle, and management wants to "understand what customers are buying." EUR 1.3 billion is not large for a company with EUR 12.7 billion in cash, and is acceptable as an information fee. But if the investment keeps expanding, the question becomes: is this a lithography company, or an investment company.

Integrity and governance: no blemishes, but one historical controversy

ASML's governance ranks in the top tier of European equities: a Dutch two-tier board, an independent supervisory board, a 4% shareholding disclosure threshold, and fully staffed audit and remuneration committees. The past decade brought no financial restatements, no related-party scandals and no insider-trading penalties against management.

The one blemish is the 2024 "Dutch business climate" episode. The company publicly warned that Eindhoven's power grid, housing and talent visas could not keep pace with its expansion, and it even weighed expanding abroad. The Dutch government responded with the roughly EUR 2.5 billion "Project Beethoven" to upgrade infrastructure and keep the headquarters in the country. The lesson: a company's capacity plans can be held hostage by a country's housing policy.

Succession: the biggest uncertainty of the next five years

Christophe Fouquet took over in 2024 and, by Dutch company convention, his term runs to around 2028. The real succession question is not the CEO but the technical layer: once Martin van den Brink retires, it is unclear from the outside who signs off on the EUV and High-NA technology roadmap. ASML's technical decision-making has always rested on "one person decides + long-termism" — an efficient but fragile structure.

The metrics to watch are specific. If High-NA customer adoption goes smoothly in 2027, the technical handover has succeeded; if it slips repeatedly, question whether the technical judgment has gone wrong. This matters more than any financial metric because it determines the moat after 2030.

Also watch management's stance on "value-based pricing." It is the most important commercial decision this management team will make and the only judgment verifiable from the outside — the 2027 gross margin is the acceptance test.

An easily overlooked governance detail: no customer representative sits on the supervisory board

Under the Dutch two-tier system the supervisory board is accountable to shareholders alone; neither customers nor suppliers sit on it. That matters greatly for ASML: are its customers also its shareholders? No. TSMC, Samsung and Intel are pure buyers and none holds a stake, so company decisions cannot be held hostage to customer interests and value-based pricing can be pushed through.

Conclusion

Management score 8/10. Execution A: capacity locked in, buybacks executed, guidance delivered as promised. Transparency B: value-based pricing without numbers, order value not disclosed separately. Succession risk B-: no clear path visible for the transfer of technical leadership. No hard flaws in governance and no reckless capital allocation. The 8 reflects three years of genuinely converting monopoly advantage into cash; the 2-point deduction reflects that the next five years of technology judgment remain unproven.
