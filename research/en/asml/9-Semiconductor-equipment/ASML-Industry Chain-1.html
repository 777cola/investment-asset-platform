Core call: exposure to both the most expensive and the most cyclical segment

Value in the semiconductor supply chain accrues at both ends: the furthest upstream EDA/IP (gross margin above 80%) and the furthest downstream design (Nvidia at 70%). In between sit the asset-heavy manufacturing segments — foundry at 40%–60%, memory at 30%–55%, packaging and testing at 15%–20%. Equipment is the exception — it sits in the middle yet earns 45%–60% gross margins, because it is the only segment in the entire chain with a "segmented monopoly." ASML occupies the most expensive segment within equipment: lithography, with a 100% EUV monopoly. Cycle position: third year of the equipment upcycle, revenue still has room, gross margin has peaked.

Value distribution: why equipment captures excess profit

On the smile curve, both ends (design, EDA) earn high margins while the middle (manufacturing, packaging and testing) earns thin ones. Equipment sits in the "middle" yet captures upstream margins because monopoly structure within it is segmented: EUV lithography has one player, metrology is dominated by KLA, and etching is split among three. The more exclusive the position, the higher the gross margin — KLA 60%, ASML 57%, Applied Materials 47%, Tokyo Electron 45% — a line that aligns almost perfectly.

Contrast packaging and testing: ASE gross margin 20%, JCET 15%, because too many players compete and technical differentiation is small. Within the same supply chain, position determines margin, and the degree of monopoly determines how much that position is worth.

ASML's position in the chain: the choke point that determines whether advanced nodes are feasible

Lithography accounts for roughly 20%–25% of fab equipment investment on an industry basis, yet it is the only step where "if you don't do it, you can't make advanced nodes." Etch can be compensated for if slightly off; deposition can be tuned if slightly off; but if lithography resolution is insufficient, the entire process node cannot scale down. That is why lithography can take 20% of WFE investment yet capture the highest gross margin in the equipment industry.

Upstream, ASML controls the supply chain: Zeiss (24.9% stake, exclusive optics), Cymer (wholly owned, light source) and Trumpf (laser source). These are not procurement relationships but co-development relationships; switching suppliers would mean redoing a decade of work. Downstream, it has pricing power over customers: one EUV tool costs over EUR 200 million, High-NA over EUR 350 million, and customers queue for two years.

The only soft spot is politics: to whom it sells is not entirely its decision. Mainland China accounted for 23% of Q3 net system sales, but EUV has long been banned from sale.

Cycle position: third year of the equipment upcycle

The semiconductor equipment cycle runs roughly every four years: down in 2019, up in 2020–2021, down in 2022, bottoming in 2023, up in 2024–2026. On that rhythm, 2026 is the third year of the upcycle.

The drivers this time differ from the last cycle. The last was 5G, handsets and restocking amid the chip shortage; this one is AI compute and HBM. The former was a consumer electronics cycle — high beta but short. The latter is a capex cycle — longer, but more dependent on decisions by a handful of customers. Capex at the four major North American cloud providers peaks historically in 2026, which is both why this cycle has run into a third year and the biggest risk to it.

China variable: the one link in the chain politics can sever

China has the lowest localisation rate in equipment: lithography <5%, etch/deposition 20%–30%, metrology <10%. SMEE's DUV sits at the 90nm node, more than two generations behind ASML. The implication: China demand is real, but supply is constrained by controls.

For ASML, China is a sell-what-you-can market—full-year 2026 guidance is about 20%, mainly DUV and metrology. For domestic equipment makers (Naura, AMEC), China is a market they must crack. Both logics hold at once, which is why domestic equipment valuations (over 35x) have long run above ASML's peers.

Upstream: the positioning of Zeiss and Cymer

ASML's upstream is no ordinary component supply chain: it is built on joint R&D and equity ties. Zeiss SMT supplies the optical systems, in which ASML holds a 24.9% stake and has funded the expansion of its High-NA optical production line; Cymer is the light-source business ASML acquired outright in 2013 for about USD 2.5 billion; Trumpf has long supplied the CO2 laser drive on an exclusive basis. How fast these three expand capacity directly determines how many machines ASML can deliver.

That is also why ASML's capacity expansion is so slow: the binding constraint is not how fast ASML can build fabs but how fast Zeiss can add mirror-polishing capacity. Producing mirrors at atomic-level flatness in volume rests on master craftsmen's touch and decades of process accumulation; it is not a matter of throwing money at the problem to double output.

Downstream: five or six customers, none of whom you can afford to offend

TSMC, Samsung, Intel, SK hynix, Micron and SMIC. The top three together account for more than half of revenue (industry basis; the company does not disclose customer by customer). As a result, a capex decision by any one of them can swing ASML's quarterly revenue by 10% either way.

The reverse also holds: customers cannot do without it. Advanced-node capacity expansion plans are scheduled around "how many EUV tools we can get"; if they cannot get them, the plans slip. This mutual lock-in is more durable than simple "seller power" — customers do not want it to fail, they only want it to build a few more tools.

Quantitative reference for cycle positioning

Three indicators frame the picture. First, WFE growth: global WFE will reach roughly USD 130 billion in 2026, up around 25% YoY (rough estimate). Historically, only 2021 and 2017 exceeded +25%, and each was followed by a pullback. Second, ASML's own EUV shipments: about 65 units in 2026, up 50% YoY, a growth rate in the historical peak range. Third, capex intensity at memory makers: elevated in 2026 on HBM demand.

All three point to the late stage of an upcycle. This does not contradict "capacity shortage through 2028": capacity locks in volume, while the cycle determines price and gross margin. Volume can still rise; price (gross margin) has already peaked.

Conclusion

Industry chain view: equipment is the highest-margin intermediate link in the semiconductor chain, and lithography is the most exclusive segment within equipment. ASML's position is impeccable, but it is tied to customer capex, and capex is at a historic peak. On the cycle, revenue has one to two years left; gross margin has already peaked.
