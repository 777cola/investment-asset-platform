Risk Assessment (Li Lu's perspective) — ASML

Four risks, one that can truly kill the valuation
Research date 2026/10/16  ·  Price $1,641.52 (-0.96%)  ·  Market cap About USD 624 billion (about EUR 538 billion)  ·  PE(TTM) Approximately 41x
← Back to the hub

Core view

Ranked by damage: an AI capex inflection (can knock 40%+ off), escalation of export controls (can cut 20% of revenue), High-NA economics proven false (compresses the valuation multiple), customer concentration (structural, but not fatal). The first two are exogenous variables the company cannot control; the latter two are endogenous and observable. The risk is not "whether it happens" but "whether you can withstand it when it does."

Top risk: an inflection point in AI capex

Capex at the four major North American cloud providers peaks at a historic high in 2026. If two of them cut 20%, the chain transmits as follows: cloud providers cut orders → Nvidia/Broadcom cut wafer demand → TSMC delays 2nm capacity expansion → ASML orders shift from "backlog" to "pushout." The whole process takes two quarters, and the stocks move first.

This is the only variable capable of causing a drawdown of more than 40%, and it is the same cause behind both the 2019 and 2022 halvings. What makes it frightening: when it happens, every fundamental indicator still looks good—orders in hand, guidance unchanged, management saying "demand is healthy."

Second risk: export controls

China accounts for 23% of Q3 net system sales, with full-year guidance around 20%. That figure contains almost no EUV (long banned for sale), mainly DUV and metrology. If controls expand from an "equipment model list" to an "end-user licensing regime," the impact is a direct cut to revenue, not a delay.

The company's position is clear: it opposes further DUV restrictions, arguing they will accelerate China's own development. But it has no say in the decision. In Li Lu's framework, this type of risk is called "force majeure policy risk," and the way to handle it is not forecasting but position limits—companies with single-policy risk exposure above 20% get no more than a 5% position.

Third risk: the economics of High-NA

High-NA costs more than EUR 350 million per unit, 1.5x standard EUV. Customers are not buying it because it is cheap but because it "can make sub-2nm." If throughput (wafers per hour) falls short of expectations, their unit costs rise instead and adoption gets pushed back.

The answer comes in 2027. High-NA adoption at TSMC, Samsung and Intel is the key indicator for the post-2028 growth curve — more important than any order figure.

Fourth risk: customer concentration

Five or six customers, the top three of which together account for more than half of revenue (industry estimate; the company does not disclose customer by customer). Capex swings at any one of them are amplified into single-quarter revenue volatility. This is structural and cannot be diversified away.

But it is not fatal—these customers will not disappear at the same time, and their technology roadmaps all depend on ASML. The real risk is not "customers leaving" but "customers all stopping capacity expansion," which brings us back to the first risk.

The other side of the risk: what would prove this wrong

To be clear: if AI demand keeps beating expectations through 2027, the four major cloud providers raise capex again and value-based pricing lands smoothly, then today's 31x is cheap and all the risk discussion is noise. The odds are not low — 2026 played out along a path on which every risk was falsified.

The point of the risk list, then, is not to "predict a crash" but to "size the position." At this price the reasonable approach is: give it 3%–5% of the portfolio, not 10%. If it falls, you have dry powder to add; if it rises, you are not left on the sidelines.

Conclusion

Four risks matter. AI capex can genuinely kill the valuation; export controls can cut revenue; High-NA compresses the multiple; customer concentration is structural. Conclusion: this is a ten-year hold asset, but it should not be a heavy position at 41x P/E. Position discipline matters more than price judgment.
