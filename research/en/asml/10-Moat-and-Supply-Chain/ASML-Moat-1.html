Core call: technology is the moat; licensing is the Achilles' heel

ASML's moat is not one layer but three stacked together: a physical moat (13.5nm light source + picometer-grade mirrors), a supply-chain moat (exclusive Zeiss partnership + 24.9% stake) and a customer moat (joint yield-parameter development; switching equipment means rebuilding the line). Stacked together, no one has breached it in twenty years. One score is low, however — political licence 4/10: who the equipment can be sold to is decided by the Dutch and US governments, not by the company.

Position in the value chain: the "switch" for the entire chain

Upstream sit Zeiss, Trumpf and Cymer — "co-development" suppliers rather than interchangeable parts vendors. Zeiss's mirrors must be flat to atomic scale, with roughness at the picometre level, and no second company worldwide can make them; ASML holds a 24.9% stake and funds their R&D, in effect turning suppliers into joint-venture partners. Midstream is whole-machine integration, where the real capability lies in tuning tens of thousands of parts to nanometre precision while sustaining mass-production yield. Downstream are TSMC, Samsung, Intel, SK hynix and Micron.

What makes this position special: it does not compete on chip prices, it decides who gets to compete. TSMC's 2nm, Samsung's 1.4nm and Intel's 18A all require the machine first. Its pricing power therefore is not "a bit more expensive than others" but "no alternative".

Why the moat has not been breached in twenty years

Three reasons. First, the physical barrier: EUV uses a 13.5nm wavelength and requires a vacuum, multilayer reflective mirrors and a tin-droplet light source, and the engineering difficulty of any single link is orders of magnitude. Second, the time barrier: building a prototype in 2006 and reaching mass production in 2018 took twelve years and tens of billions of euros, a path no company is willing to walk again. Third, the ecosystem barrier: customers, suppliers and standards are all built around ASML, and a new entrant would have to win over all three at once.

On scoring, technology and supply chain are close to full marks (10/9), as both moats are deep and widening: High-NA raises the physical barrier another level, while Zeiss's capacity expansion serves ASML alone.

The biggest danger is not the competitor, but the license

No technological rival: Canon's nanoimprint trails by a generation on throughput and yield, Nikon has exited the EUV alliance, and Shanghai Micro Electronics remains at the 90nm level. What can genuinely cost it 20% of revenue is export control, not competition.

Mainland China accounts for 23% of Q3 net system sales, mainly DUV and metrology. The Dutch government has required licences for advanced DUV since 2023 and tightened the rules repeatedly in 2024–2026. ASML has publicly opposed further restrictions, arguing they will accelerate China's own development—but it has no say in the decision. This item scores 4/10, the only failing grade among the six dimensions.

That is also why its moat score is 47/60 rather than 55/60: technology sets the width of the moat, politics sets its usability.

The real threat isn't Canon — it's customers refusing to add capacity

If forced to name a single opponent, it is not Canon, not Nikon, and not SMIC — it is the customer capex curve. Both halvings, in 2019 and 2022, came not from competition but from customers hitting the brakes at the same time.

The second-most-dangerous opponent is the "technology roadmap fork": if chip performance gains one day stop depending on shrinking linewidth — if 3D stacking, advanced packaging and optical interconnect, for example, become mainstream — lithography's importance will decline. That will not happen within a decade, but it is the long-term ceiling on the moat.

Is the moat widening or narrowing?

Widening. Three pieces of evidence. First, High-NA raises the physical barrier another notch—from 13.5nm to 0.55 NA, the difficulty of the optical system is exponential, and only Zeiss and ASML can do it. Second, the installed base is snowballing, service revenue is growing thicker, and customer switching costs are rising. Third, value-based pricing starts to show from 2027, indicating that customers' bargaining power is falling, not rising.

Only one part is narrowing: politics. The control list keeps getting longer, and the addressable market is shrinking. This is the only variable that deteriorates over time.

The supply chain's fragile point is not a supply cutoff but capacity

ASML's supply-chain risk is not "can't buy it" but "upstream can't expand." Zeiss's mirrors, Trumpf's laser sources and Cymer's light-source chambers all depend on single-point capacity and long-cycle expansion. If another 30%-plus expansion does materialise in 2027–2028, the bottleneck will most likely sit not in ASML's fab buildings but in Zeiss's polishing workshops.

That is also why the company says it is "studying further expansion" rather than "will expand" — it is waiting on an answer from upstream. Investors should treat Zeiss's expansion progress as a leading indicator of ASML's capacity, even though Zeiss is unlisted and does not disclose.

How to read the scorecard

Four of the six score above 8, confirming that the "core" of the moat is extremely solid. Two score below 8: scale cost at 7 (small-batch, high-price, so cost was never an advantage) and political licence at 4 (the licence is not in its own hands).

That totals 47/60, or 78%. For context, most manufacturing companies score 40%–55% on moat and consumer brands 60%–75%; ASML at 78% is "near full marks but with a hard crack." That crack is political rather than technological, so its valuation will always carry a "policy discount" and will never command the 40x multiple of a pure software monopoly.

Conclusion

Moat 47/60: technology 10, switching costs 9, supply chain 9, service 8, cost 7, politics 4. A very wide moat with one political crack. Competitive risk is negligible; policy risk and demand-cycle risk must be priced in.
