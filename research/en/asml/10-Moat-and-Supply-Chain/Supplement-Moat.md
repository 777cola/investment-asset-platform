## Position in the value chain: it is the "switch" for the entire chain

Upstream sits Zeiss, Trumpf and Cymer — "co-development" suppliers, not interchangeable parts vendors. Zeiss's mirrors must be flat to atomic scale (roughness on the picometre level), and no second company worldwide can make them; ASML holds a 24.9% stake and funds their R&D, in essence turning suppliers into joint-venture partners. Midstream is whole-machine integration, where the real capability lies in tuning tens of thousands of parts to nanometre precision while sustaining mass-production yield. Downstream are TSMC, Samsung, Intel, SK hynix and Micron.

What makes this position special: it does not compete on chip prices, but it decides who gets to compete. TSMC's 2nm, Samsung's 1.4nm and Intel's 18A all require the machine first. So its pricing power is not "a bit more expensive than others" but "no alternative".

## Why the moat has not been breached in twenty years

Three reasons. First, the physical barrier: EUV uses a 13.5nm wavelength and requires a vacuum, multilayer reflective mirrors and a tin-droplet light source; the engineering difficulty of any single link is orders of magnitude. Second, the time barrier: from building a prototype in 2006 to mass production in 2018 took twelve years and tens of billions of euros in investment—no company is willing to walk that path again. Third, the ecosystem barrier: customers, suppliers and standards are all built around ASML, and a new entrant would have to win over all three at once.

On scoring, technology and supply chain are close to full marks (10/9), because both are deep and widening: High-NA raises the physical barrier another level; Zeiss's capacity expansion serves ASML alone.

## The biggest danger is not the competitor, but the license

No technological rival: Canon's nanoimprint lags by a generation on throughput and yield, Nikon has exited the EUV alliance, and Shanghai Micro Electronics sits at the 90nm level. What can genuinely cost it 20% of revenue is export control, not competition.

Mainland China accounts for 23% of Q3 net system sales, mainly DUV and metrology. The Dutch government has required licences for advanced DUV since 2023 and tightened rules repeatedly in 2024–2026. The company has publicly opposed further restrictions, arguing they will accelerate China's own development—but it has no say in the decision. This item scores 4/10, the only failing grade among the six dimensions.

That is also why its moat score is 47/60 rather than 55/60: the width of the moat is set by technology, the usability of the moat is set by politics.

## The most dangerous rival: not Canon, but customers' refusal to expand capacity

If forced to name a single opponent, it is not Canon, not Nikon, and not SMIC — it is the customer capex curve. The two halvings in 2019 and 2022 came not from competition but from customers hitting the brakes at the same time.

The second-most-dangerous opponent is the "technology roadmap fork": if one day chip performance gains no longer depend on shrinking linewidth (for example, 3D stacking, advanced packaging and optical interconnect become mainstream), lithography's importance will decline. That will not happen within a decade, but it is the long-term ceiling on the moat.

## Is the moat widening or narrowing?

Widening. Three pieces of evidence. First, High-NA raises the physical barrier another notch—from 13.5nm to 0.55 NA, the difficulty of the optical system is exponential, and only Zeiss and ASML can do it. Second, the installed base is snowballing, service revenue is growing thicker, and customer switching costs are rising. Third, value-based pricing starts to show from 2027, indicating that customers' bargaining power is falling, not rising.

Only one part is narrowing: politics. The control list keeps getting longer, and the addressable market is shrinking. This is the only variable that deteriorates over time.

## The supply chain's fragile point: not a supply cutoff, but capacity

ASML's supply-chain risk is not "can't buy it" but "upstream can't expand." Zeiss's mirrors, Trumpf's laser sources and Cymer's light-source chambers all rely on single-point capacity and long-cycle expansion. If another 30%-plus expansion really happens in 2027–2028, the bottleneck will most likely sit not in ASML's fab buildings but in Zeiss's polishing workshops.

That is also why the company says it is "studying further expansion" rather than "will expand" — it is waiting for an answer from upstream. Investors should treat Zeiss's expansion progress as a leading indicator of ASML's capacity, even though the company is unlisted and does not disclose.

## How to read the scorecard

Four of the six score above 8, showing that the "core" of the moat is extremely solid. Two score below 8: scale cost 7 (because it is small-batch, high-price, so cost was never an advantage), and political license 4 (because the license is not in its own hands).

Together that is 47/60, or 78% in percentage terms. For comparison: most manufacturing companies score 40%–55% on moat, consumer brands 60%–75%, and ASML at 78% is "near full marks but with a hard crack." That crack is not technological but political—so its valuation will always carry a "policy discount" and will never be awarded the 40x multiple of a pure software monopoly.
