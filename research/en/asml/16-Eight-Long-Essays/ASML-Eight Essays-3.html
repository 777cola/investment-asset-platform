Moat anatomy: four layers of barriers, one crack — ASML

Core view

ASML's moat rests on four layers, not one: the physical (light source and optics), the engineering (integrating tens of thousands of parts), the ecosystem (joint R&D with customers and suppliers) and time (twelve years from prototype to mass production). Stacked together, these four layers have gone twenty years unbreached. Only one crack exists: politics.

Physical layer: 13.5 nm light

EUV uses extreme ultraviolet light at a wavelength of 13.5nm, which is absorbed by almost all materials, including air and lenses. The entire system must therefore operate in a vacuum, with the light path guided by multilayer reflective mirrors (alternating molybdenum/silicon, each layer a few nanometres thick). Mirror surface roughness must be controlled at the picometre level—scale a mirror up to the size of a country and its tallest bump would not exceed one centimetre.

The light source is more extreme still: tin droplets are ejected 50,000 times per second, struck twice by a carbon dioxide laser and turned into a plasma that emits 13.5nm light. The engineering challenge is turning a laboratory apparatus into mass-production equipment that runs 24 hours a day.

Engineering layer: yield across tens of thousands of components

An EUV machine comprises tens of thousands of parts and hundreds of thousands of part numbers, sourced from thousands of suppliers. The real capability lies not in designing it but in getting every unit to run stably for ten years, which requires pushing tolerances, vibration, thermal drift and contamination control to the extreme.

This layer of barriers is the hardest to replicate: what matters is not patents but process parameters accumulated through trial and error. Nikon's technology in the 2000s was not bad; it lost on engineering and mass-production yield—it could build in the lab but not in the fab.

Ecosystem layer: both customers and suppliers are locked in

In 2012, Intel, TSMC and Samsung jointly invested about EUR 3.85 billion in ASML, subscribing for shares and funding EUV research. This is extremely rare in business history: customers paying to help a supplier build its moat, because they knew there would be no next-generation process without ASML.

The same holds upstream: ASML funded the expansion of Zeiss SMT's High-NA optics line, and the two hold stakes in each other (ASML holds 24.9% of Zeiss). This is not a procurement contract but a "co-investment, exclusive supply" bond.

Time horizon: twelve years

The journey from the 2006 EUV prototype to the first volume-production tool, the NXE:3400B, shipped in 2018, took twelve years and tens of billions of euros. Any new entrant would need the same time and money — and would have to catch up while ASML keeps moving. Commercially unviable.

Four layers of barriers stack up to a 47/60 moat score (10 technology + 9 switching costs + 9 supply chain + 8 service + 7 cost + 4 politics). The one crack is politics: the Dutch and US governments decide who the equipment can be sold to. That crack does not narrow the moat, but it does limit how usable it is.

Why others won't copy it: the math doesn't work

Any company setting out to challenge EUV today would need 10–15 years, more than EUR 20 billion in R&D and an entirely new supply chain spanning optics, light source, vacuum and control software — and would still have to persuade TSMC and Samsung to validate it on volume production lines. Even then it would face a rival that has already amortised its costs and holds an installed base of thousands of units plus a global service network.

That math does not pass at any board. This is the economic essence of a moat: not "I do it well," but "it doesn't pay for anyone else to do it." The technology barrier is only the surface; the cost barrier and first-mover scale are the substance.

Monetizing the moat: from defense to rent collection

A moat only has value if it can be monetised; otherwise it is merely good-looking. ASML has three monetisation paths: raising equipment prices (High-NA unit price above 350 million), service fees (installed base management of 3.289 billion per quarter, +50% YoY), and value-based pricing (revenue sharing with customers from 2027).

The three differ in certainty: services are the most certain (installed base), equipment price increases are fairly certain (scarcity), and value-based pricing is the least certain (customer negotiation). The market's current 31x multiple roughly implies that "services + equipment price increases" are already priced in, while "value-based pricing" is not. If value-based pricing materialises in 2027, the multiple has room to re-rate—this underpins the optimistic-case USD 2,380.

Conclusion

Four layers of barriers: physical, engineering, ecosystem and time. The first three cannot be resolved within a decade; the fourth (politics) is the only crack. Conclusion: competitive risk is negligible, policy risk must be priced in.
