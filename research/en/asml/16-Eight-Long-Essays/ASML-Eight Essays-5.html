Industry and competition: one chain, one switch — ASML

Core view

Semiconductor equipment is an industry of segmented monopolies: one player in EUV lithography, KLA dominant in metrology, three players splitting etch. ASML sits at the "switch" — it does not make chips, but decides who can. Competitive history suggests this position was not won on technological leadership alone, but by betting on the right roadmap and co-investing with customers.

2004–2008: the immersion lithography battle and how Nikon lost

In the early 2000s, lithography faced two routes: 157nm dry and 193nm immersion. Nikon bet on 157nm; ASML and TSMC bet on immersion together, adding a layer of water between the lens and the wafer to cut the effective wavelength to 134nm.

Immersion won, and won decisively, extending the life of 193nm by fifteen years. Nikon's 157nm route was scrapped, and it never recovered in high-end lithography.This was not a technology gap but a route call plus customer lock-in. Whatever TSMC wanted, ASML delivered—that is how it won.

2012: Customers put up the money to help it build EUV

By 2012 EUV development had burned through too much cash, leaving ASML in need of outside funding. Intel, TSMC and Samsung jointly invested about EUR 3.85 billion, subscribing for shares and funding R&D — an arrangement almost without precedent in business history: customers paying to help a supplier build a moat.

Why were they willing? Without EUV, Moore's Law would run out of road, and none of the three could survive. In essence, the investment was a ticket to the next generation.

Current competitive landscape: no rival in technology

Canon's nanoimprint lithography (NIL) trails by a generation on throughput and defect rate and addresses only select memory layers; Nikon has exited the EUV alliance; Shanghai Micro Electronics remains stuck at 90nm, constrained by export controls. Metrology is dominated by KLA, but that is complementary to lithography.

Competition, in other words, barely applies to ASML. Its problem has never been share loss but customers not expanding capacity.

Cycle history: lessons from two halvings

Semiconductor equipment entered a downturn in 2019: ASML's revenue stalled and the stock fell 30%. In 2022, memory makers slashed capex and orders went from "fully booked" to "postponed" within two quarters, taking the stock from USD 900 to USD 360 — a 60% drop.

The cause was the same both times: customers hit the brakes simultaneously. And both episodes shared one feature — every fundamental indicator looked good before the fall. That is the cruelty of cyclical stocks: they give you no warning, only the outcome.

Three reshuffles in the equipment industry

The first came in the 1990s, when US players GCA and SVG declined and Nikon and Canon rose on the strength of their DUV engineering capability. The second ran from 2004 to 2010: the immersion lithography battle, in which ASML bet on the right route alongside TSMC and moved from follower to leader. The third spanned 2015–2020: EUV volume production, which left ASML the sole supplier and drove Nikon out.

The pattern across all three reshuffles is the same: the winner is not the one with the strongest technology but the one that bets on the right route and locks in the largest customer. That also explains why ASML responds so quickly to TSMC's needs—it knows how its throne was built.

China variable: demand is real, supply is stuck

China is the world's largest market for mature-node capacity expansion and accounted for roughly 20% of ASML's net system sales in 2026. EUV, however, has long been banned from sale, and advanced DUV requires a licence. The implication: Chinese customers are buying equipment that "works," not equipment that is "best."

For domestic equipment makers (Naura, AMEC, SMEE), this is a historic window—not a technology breakthrough, but "replacement demand created by the blockade." For ASML, China is an installed-base market where it sells as much as it can, not a growth engine. The two cannot be lumped together.

Customer concentration: three customers account for half of revenue

ASML does not disclose revenue by customer, but its industry breakdown shows the top three customers together account for more than half of revenue, with TSMC the largest. Such concentration is normal in the equipment industry—only five or six companies worldwide can afford EUV.

High concentration cuts both ways. The upside is that customers are "must-buy" and will not walk away; the downside is that a single customer cutting orders sends quarterly revenue swinging sharply. The 2022 slump came as memory customers (Samsung, SK hynix, Micron) cut orders simultaneously.

Conclusion

Industry view: equipment is the highest-margin segment of the semiconductor chain, and lithography the most exclusive part of equipment. Competitive risk is negligible; cyclical risk is not. 2026 marks the third year of the upcycle: revenue still has room to run, but gross margin has peaked.
