Management: an engineer hands over to a manager — ASML

Core view

Under Peter Wennink (2013–2024), ASML grew revenue from EUR 5.2 billion to 27.6 billion, built on getting EUV right, binding customers close and not overspending. Christophe Fouquet took over in 2024 and set about turning the monopoly into cash: locking up capacity, pushing value-based pricing and buying back stock in size. The two CEOs had different mandates, and both executed well.

Peter Wennink: fivefold in ten years

When he took over in 2013, ASML's annual revenue was about EUR 5.2 billion and EUV was still a loss-making investment. By the time he left in 2023, revenue had reached EUR 27.6 billion, gross margin was 51% and market cap had risen tenfold. Over those ten years he got three things right: he held to the EUV roadmap without wavering, turned customers into co-investors, and refused to chase low-margin broad semiconductor equipment.

The third was the hardest. In the equipment industry the easiest way to expand is to "add another step" (etching, inspection, for example), but he did not. ASML kept in-house production limited to core subsystems and outsourced the rest. That restraint is strategic capability.

Fu Ke Li: The Executor

French national who joined from Applied Materials in 2008 and previously ran the EUV and applications businesses. His style is "say it clearly, give the numbers": 2026Q3 guidance, capacity plans and the value-based pricing timeline are all more specific than his predecessor's.

He has done three things: lock in EUV capacity under long-term customer agreements, reducing order volatility; push value-based pricing, due to land in 2027; and buy back stock in size, EUR 4.1 billion in Q3. All three point the same way—converting monopoly advantage into shareholder returns.

Capital allocation: unusually clean

All four historical acquisitions were vertical integration: Cymer (light source, about USD 2.5 billion in 2013), HMI (e-beam inspection, about USD 3 billion in 2016), a 24.9% stake in Zeiss (2016) and Berliner Glas (about EUR 1.1 billion in 2020). Not one was about "buying revenue."

M&A essentially stopped after 2020, with cash redirected to buybacks and dividends—the right approach for a mature monopolist: when returns in the core business exceed all external opportunities, return the money to shareholders.

Succession concerns: the CTO seat sits empty

Van den Brink retired in 2024. He joined in 1984, was a company veteran and the main driver of the EUV roadmap. After his departure, ASML did not appoint an equivalent CTO, leaving technical decisions concentrated in the CEO and a handful of business heads.

For a company that earns its living at the limits of physics, this is a change worth watching. A misjudgment on technology does not show up immediately in the financials; it lags by two to three years. The pace of High-NA adoption in 2027 will be the first test of this technical leadership team.

Compensation and incentives: aligned with shareholders?

ASML's management pay is structured as base salary plus annual bonus plus long-term equity incentive, with the long-term component accounting for the bulk. Performance metrics include relative TSR (benchmarked against the SOX index), ROIC and ESG targets. The structure is standard for European equities and not particularly aggressive.

One point stands out: including relative TSR means management can earn full payout simply by outperforming the semiconductor index. In a bull market that easily hands out free money; in a bear market it offers protection, since falling less than peers still counts as a win. For shareholders the metric is not perfect, but it beats absolute share price—at least it does not encourage management to chase buybacks at elevated prices.

Governance structure: Dutch-style steadiness and slowness

The two-tier system's strength is an independent supervisory board and slower but steadier decision-making; its weakness is a slower response to market shifts than US companies. The 2024 "Dutch business climate" episode is a case in point: companies complained that infrastructure was falling behind, and it took six months to negotiate the "Beethoven Plan" with the government.

That governance is a plus for "ten-year assets" (no reckless moves) and a minus for "rapid iteration" (slow response). ASML's business happens not to require speed, so the structure fits.

Conclusion

Management 8/10: Peter Wennink built the monopoly and Christophe Fouquet is monetising it, with clean capital allocation. Marks off for succession — the handover of technology leadership is unproven, and that is what decides the moat beyond 2030.
