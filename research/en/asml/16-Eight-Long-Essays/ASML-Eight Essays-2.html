The business model does not sell machines; it sells a "time window" — ASML

Core view

An EUV tool costs more than EUR 200 million. Why will customers pay? Not because the machine is expensive, but because when they get it determines whether they can catch the window for this generation of process nodes. A year late, and a competitor takes the market. What ASML really sells, then, is time, not equipment.

What exactly are customers buying

When TSMC buys an EUV tool, the question is not "how much does this machine cost" but "how much more can we earn by volume-producing 3nm a year earlier." By industry convention, an advanced-node fab running 100,000 wafers a month at a per-wafer value in the thousands of dollars generates revenue on the order of USD 10 billion from a year of earlier production. Set against that, the EUR 200 million machine price is negligible.

This arithmetic determines pricing power: when a product's price accounts for only 2%–3% of the customer's return, the customer is insensitive to price and acutely sensitive to delivery timing. Hence ASML can build a backlog, take prepayments and raise prices—it sells "earlier," not "cheaper."

Two legs: lump sum and annuity

Revenue splits into two parts. System sales are one-off: 2026Q3 system revenue was EUR 8.373 billion (EUV about EUR 4.1 billion + non-EUV about EUR 4.27 billion). Installed-base management is annuity-like at EUR 3.289 billion, up about +50% YoY. The former tracks orders; the latter tracks the installed base.

The annuity business is higher quality. Customers will not stop maintenance simply because they are not expanding capacity in a given year, since the loss from one day of downtime far exceeds the service fee. This revenue therefore has low cyclical elasticity, high gross margin and strong predictability. It now accounts for 28% of revenue, versus just over 20% at one point historically — a rising share that is structural evidence the business is improving.

Pricing strategy shift: from cost-plus to value-based pricing

ASML's pricing logic has shifted from "cost plus a reasonable gross margin" to "how much extra the customer earns with this machine, and we take a share of that". This has already been negotiated with major customers, will begin to show in 2027 and be fully reflected in 2028.

That is a substantive change in the business model: from manufacturing to rent collection. Manufacturing gross margins are squeezed by cost and competition; rent-collection gross margins are set by scarcity. A 57.5% gross margin already marks ASML as an outlier among manufacturers; if value-based pricing takes hold, above 60% is not fantasy.

The trade-off: customers will start asking whether what they pay matches the value delivered. Once the customer's ROI no longer works — for example, if High-NA output efficiency falls short of expectations — value-based pricing turns into a value dispute.

Where is the ceiling for this business?

The ceiling is not competition; it is the economics of Moore's Law. As long as "more advanced process = lower cost per unit of compute" holds, customers will keep buying. Once that equation breaks down—costs surge and returns diminish beyond 1nm, for example—capacity expansion stops.

That ceiling will not be reached within a decade, but it caps the valuation multiple: the market will not award a 40x P/E to a business with a visible endgame. At 31x, the pricing roughly implies another ten years of growth.

Why customers don't build it themselves

TSMC, Samsung and Intel all have the money and the engineers, so why not build their own lithography tools? They have tried: in the 1980s and 1990s, SVG and GCA in the US and Nikon and Canon in Japan all manufactured them, yet only three survived, and in EUV only ASML was left. The reason is diseconomies of scale — a tool must sell several hundred units globally to spread R&D costs, and building solely for one's own fabs would triple the cost.

Neutrality is the other reason. If TSMC built its own lithography tools, would Samsung and Intel dare to buy them? No. Equipment makers must stay neutral; this is part of the industry structure and the precondition for ASML to serve all customers at once.

The limits of pricing power: you cannot squeeze customers dry

ASML has pricing power, but it cannot be pushed to the limit. Price an EUV tool so high that customers cannot make the numbers work and they will do two things: extend the depreciable life of existing equipment and accelerate alternative solutions, even if less efficient. Both reactions hurt ASML's long-term interests.

The essence of "value-based pricing" is therefore not "raising prices" but "taking a share of the customer's incremental gain" — the customer earns 100, we take 20, and the customer is still willing to keep buying. At 50, the business becomes one-off. Management says the effect will start to show in 2027 and be fully reflected in 2028; that pace itself shows they know where the boundary lies.

Conclusion

Business essence: selling time windows and collecting two kinds of revenue, one-off plus annuity, as the model shifts from manufacturing to toll-collecting. The quality of this business is flawless; the ceiling is set by Moore's Law, not by competitors.
