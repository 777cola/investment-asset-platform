## Coarse screen: three elimination lines

First filter: will it still be at the table in three years. Cambricon fails — not because the company is bad, but because its 218x P/E already prices in "domestic substitution will inevitably succeed." H1 2026 revenue of RMB 5.996 billion and net profit of RMB 2.311 billion are real, but Q2 grew only 7.8% QoQ and inventory of RMB 8.248 billion equals 60.8% of net assets. A company with growth shifting down a gear, bloated inventory and a 218x valuation does not deserve portfolio allocation.

Second filter: is it earning its own money. Broadcom is held back here — AI semiconductor revenue growing triple digits YoY and Non-GAAP gross margin of 77% mean extremely high business quality, but the scale of the financing it arranges for customers cannot be calculated from the outside. The same disease as Nvidia, except Nvidia's cause is documented more clearly (USD 108.5 billion in guarantees and USD 279 billion in commitments all have figures).

Third filter: cycle position. Micron is marked by this filter as "not a long-term hold" — FY26Q3 gross margin 84.9%, net margin 68%, P/E 6.5x, unreasonably cheap, but memory prices are set by the cycle, not by a moat. Buying it requires judging "how much cycle is left," not "whether the company is good."

## Final pick: Nvidia and TSMC — one the most expensive, one the most stable

The remaining two companies run on entirely different logic.

TSMC is the "pick-and-shovel" play. 2026Q2 revenue was TWD 1.27 trillion, up 36% YoY, with gross margin of 67.7% and net profit after tax of TWD 706.56 billion, up 77.4% YoY; HPC accounted for 66% of revenue. It does not need to judge which AI company will win — as long as AI capex flows, it earns. 26x P/E, 35% ROE, USD 60–64 billion capex: a fair price for a "certainty premium."

Nvidia is the "toll booth." Gross margin 75%, net margin 62%, growth 106% — a better position than TSMC (stronger pricing power), but a more complex valuation (36% cash conversion, USD 108.5 billion in guarantees, USD 93.9 billion in customer equity). 15x FY2028 sounds cheaper than TSMC, but on free cash flow it is 19x — more expensive than TSMC.

So the "final pick" is not either-or; it is a division of roles: if I could only buy one, I would buy TSMC — higher certainty, more reasonable valuation, no off-balance-sheet risk. Nvidia is not unworthy of buying; its price has already fully priced in "better," plus an extra premium for "believing the guidance."

## Why it: three reasons to hold Nvidia

If TSMC is the steadier asset, why keep Nvidia in the portfolio at all? Three reasons.

First, it is the only company that captures the "doubling of value per GW." Hopper USD 18 billion → Grace Blackwell USD 25 billion → Vera Rubin USD 40 billion per GW. That curve shows Nvidia is not selling hardware; it is selling compute output per kilowatt-hour. TSMC cannot occupy this position — it charges by wafer.

Second, its customer base is broadening. ACIE up 138% YoY, sovereign AI more than 2x YoY, NeoCloud installed capacity 3GW→8GW. Demand is spreading from four cloud providers to dozens of countries and enterprises, which lowers single-customer risk — though it raises credit risk.

Third, its software ecosystem compounds. Two decades of CUDA accumulation, reinforced with every new hardware generation. This is the only moat that deepens with use, and the only moat competitors cannot buy with money.

So the answer is: hold Nvidia, but do not add; make TSMC the core AI position and Nvidia the flex position. On sizing, TSMC 5%, Nvidia 5%, the two combined no more than 10%.

One more portfolio constraint: Nvidia's correlation with TSMC, Micron and Broadcom is 0.8–0.9, so a combined 10% position is effectively 13% exposure to the AI compute chain. This is a ceiling, not a starting point.
