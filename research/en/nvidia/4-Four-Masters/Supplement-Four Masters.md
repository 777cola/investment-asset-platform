## Duan Yongping: the best business I can understand, but not a good price

Duan Yongping applies three criteria: the business must be simple, it must have pricing power, and you must be able to understand it ten years out. Nvidia passes all three. The business is simple enough to state in one sentence — it sells compute, and customers queue up. Pricing power is evident in the 75% gross margin. And will AI compute demand still exist ten years from now? Most likely yes, because "intelligence" has become something that can be priced per token.

But he has a habit when buying: he only acts when the price does not require precise forecasting. He bought Apple at 13x P/E, Moutai at 20x, NetEase at 1x. Nvidia now trades at 28x TTM and 15x FY2028 — if FY2028 delivers, he would buy at 15x; if it does not, 28x means he loses half. He would not make that judgment; he would wait for a price at which "even 30% growth is enough to avoid a loss," roughly below USD 180.

There is one more layer he would flag: parts he cannot understand do not count toward value. Guaranteeing customer obligations, investing money in customers, and committing to purchase cloud services — he would strike all three first, then apply a discount to what remains. After striking them out, this is still a good business, just no longer one you can buy with your eyes closed.

## Buffett: the moat is deep, but it took on credit risk today

Buffett would concede the moat first: CUDA is a twenty-year ecosystem, rack-scale systems raise the barrier from "building chips" to "building supercomputers," and USD 279 billion locking up capacity is a capital barrier. He likes businesses where "customers can't leave and pay upfront"—he understands the float-style business model.

Then he would turn to what lies beyond the liabilities page. Once a company starts providing land, power, data-center space and credit support for customers' projects, its risk profile shifts from "selling equipment" to "half a bank + half a developer." He buys banks because he understands credit, and buys equipment companies because he doesn't want to understand credit. Nvidia is now doing both at once.

He would also ask a very plain question: if this business is really so good, why does the company need to put up its own money to help customers buy? There are usually two answers—customers have no money, or competition is so fierce it has to front the capital. Neither sounds good.

His conclusion would most likely be "a great company, but I don't understand its off-balance-sheet." That is not a rejection; it is walking away.

## Munger: what would it take for a 40% drop? Three paths, and one has already opened

Munger's habit was to invert the question: for USD 220 to fall to USD 130 (-41%), what has to happen?

- Path one: capex stalls. The five hyperscalers will spend about USD 800 billion on capex in 2026 and an estimated USD 1.3 trillion in 2027; no growth rate can persist at that pace for three years. If 2027 growth merely slows from +60% to single digits, Nvidia's order visibility shrinks from four quarters to one. This is the most likely path.

- Path two: an AI cloud customer defaults. Of the USD 108.5 billion guarantee ceiling, how much sits with the same group of NeoClouds? The company does not say. NeoCloud installed capacity rises from 3GW at end-2025 to 8GW at end-2026, more than double, funded by debt.

- Path three: inference gets taken by ASICs. OpenAI's Jalapeño, Google's TPU and Broadcom's XPU hold an advantage in inference unit cost. Nvidia's response is to bring Groq's LPU in-house (Groq 3 LPX is already in mass production), but that shows it knows general-purpose GPUs are not the optimal solution for interactive inference.

Of the three paths, the first is cyclical, the second is credit, the third is structural. Munger would say: the first two will come but will pass; the third, once it comes, will not pass.

## Li Lu: A toll booth, but the rent depends on traffic

Li Lu would frame Nvidia within a civilisation-scale trend: the essence of this AI cycle is turning "intelligence" into tradable compute, and producing compute requires a very small set of scarce resources—advanced process nodes, HBM, power and rack-level system integration. Nvidia sits astride three of them at once—a classic infrastructure toll booth. This year it upgraded the toll booth: from "selling hardware" to "selling hardware + collecting rent."

The new business model deserves serious attention. Nvidia has partnered with Apollo, BlackRock, Blackstone, Brookfield, Goldman Sachs and KKR to mobilise USD 500 billion of third-party capital; for some compute it provides guaranteed minimum payment commitments in exchange for a share of revenue above the guarantee. Sell the hardware once, collect rent for twenty years—an attempt to swap one-time revenue for perpetual revenue.

But he would press on the endgame: how long the toll booth can keep collecting rent depends on the number of cars passing through, not on the toll booth's mood. If the real revenue generated by AI applications cannot outrun the compute bill, traffic will thin. In the first half of 2026, global AI venture funding exceeded USD 400 billion, about 70% of it for compute procurement—that is demand, and also a thermometer for bubble temperature. When 70% of venture funding is buying shovels, the gold diggers have not yet started making money.

Li Lu's conclusion is usually "right direction, good position, wait for the price"—rarely in agreement with Duan Yongping.

## Where the four converge and diverge: three questions, two answers

Compress the four investors' views into three questions and the disagreement becomes clear.

- Question one: Is this a good business? All four say yes, without exception—the only unanimous vote on this panel.

- Question two: Does financing for customers count as a problem? Duan Yongping and Li Lu say "yes, but it can be discounted"; Buffett says "yes, and I don't understand it, so I won't touch it"; Munger says "yes, and it is the dirtiest of the three downside paths." All four treat it as a problem; they differ only in how to handle it.

- Question three: Is 15x expensive? Duan Yongping says it is not cheap (he would wait for 12x); Buffett says it is not cheap (he does not know what multiple to assign); Munger says "I never guess multiples"; Li Lu says "right direction, good position, wait on price." All four oppose adding now.

Three people saying the same thing in different languages is rare in the panels I have read. Usually the four masters diverge on business quality; this time the divergence is entirely on price and structure—which means the company's business has been thoroughly researched, and what remains is purely a question of valuation and risk appetite.

## Odds assessment: 1 : 0.5 — the kind of price that is most painful for a good company

Neutral USD 248 implies just +12%, the bear case USD 168 implies -24% and the bull case USD 386 implies +75%. Probability-weighted (30% bear, 45% neutral, 25% bull) this gives USD 245, 11% above the current price. That expected value is not unattractive, but it rests on the 25%-probability bull case, and that 25% in turn depends on the company delivering its own 70% guidance.

A more intuitive framing: at the current price I assign a 30% probability to a 24% decline, a 45% probability to a 12% gain and a 25% probability to a 75% gain. Odds = 0.45×12% + 0.25×75% − 0.30×24% = 5.4 + 18.8 − 7.2 = +17%. Positive expectancy on paper, but the variance is enormous: the worst case is an order gap in some quarter of 2027, with the stock below USD 130.

The four of us therefore agree: the question is not whether to own it, but whether to add at this price. The answer is no. Hold existing positions, because selling an asset with 75% gross margin and still-accelerating demand takes more courage than buying. That is not cowardice; it is discipline.
