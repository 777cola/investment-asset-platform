Body

Ready to publish. The point: in Nvidia's earnings, the good news sits on the income statement; the bad news sits on the cash flow statement.

The good news first: the company generated RMB 59.7 billion in a single quarter

Nvidia reported FY2027 second-quarter results after the close on August 26.

Revenue came in at USD 96.221 billion, up 106% YoY and 18% QoQ. Data center revenue was USD 89.023 billion, up 117% YoY. Gross margin was 75.0%. Net income reached USD 59.688 billion, up 126% YoY.

Net income of USD 59.7 billion in a single quarter — how large is that? It exceeds the annual fiscal revenue of many countries.

Guidance for the next quarter is even more striking: USD 108 billion. Note the premise — assuming zero China data center compute revenue. Even after stripping out revenue from the world's second-largest market, the company still guides to 12% QoQ growth.

More unusually, the company for the first time gave guidance more than a year out: FY2028 revenue growth of about 70%. The CFO described this guidance as "supply constrained." Jensen Huang put it more bluntly: "If we were not constrained, our growth would be much higher."

This is good news — and the good news is solid.

Now the bad news: the money has not all been collected

Operating cash flow in the same filing's cash flow statement was USD 24.077 billion.

Against net income of USD 59.688 billion, that implies a conversion rate of 40.3%, down from 58.1% a year earlier. Free cash flow was USD 21.341 billion, a conversion rate of 35.8% versus 50.9% a year earlier.

Where did the money go? Three places.

Accounts receivable rose USD 22.346 billion to a balance of USD 63.059 billion, with days sales outstanding moving from 45 days to 60 days. Inventory rose USD 5.784 billion to USD 31.575 billion, building stock for new products. Prepayments rose USD 5.497 billion. A further USD 7.771 billion of net income came from revaluation of equity investments, which brings in no cash at all.

On payment terms, the company explains that large orders from investment-grade customers are delivered across multiple quarters, extending the payment cycle. The explanation is reasonable, but it also says one thing — customers are getting bigger, and they are paying more slowly.

Three things it had never done before

Nvidia did three things this quarter it had never done before.

First, it issued USD 24.9 billion of debt. Long-term borrowings jumped from USD 7.469 billion to USD 32.366 billion. This is a company with USD 23.2 billion of net cash — it does not lack money; the bond sale was to keep cash available for elsewhere.

Second, it bought USD 15.8 billion of equity (USD 42.4 billion cumulative in the first half). It now holds USD 42.783 billion of marketable equity and USD 51.157 billion of non-marketable equity — USD 93.940 billion combined, or 29.3% of total assets, up from 16.3% six months ago. The company says it has invested nearly USD 50 billion in frontier AI labs.

Third, it is guaranteeing its customers. It provides credit guarantees for data center projects, with maximum total exposure of USD 108.5 billion. Add USD 279 billion of supply commitments and USD 36 billion of cloud service purchase commitments, and off-balance-sheet commitments total roughly USD 530.5 billion, per the Australian Financial Review's tally of the 10-Q notes.

Taken together, Nvidia is doing something new: it has started financing its own customers.

How does management explain this, and does the explanation hold up?

Jensen Huang addressed it head-on: "We know the outside world will read arrangements like this as circular financing."

His defence has three layers.

First, the technology at these AI labs is proven and demand is real. This layer is verifiable — revenue at these labs is indeed rising.

Second, the capital deployed is only a small fraction of free cash flow over the same period. This layer warrants the most caution. By the company's own account, AI lab business requiring support from Nvidia's balance sheet will account for roughly a quarter of total business next year.

A quarter. Not a number after the decimal point.

Third, compute is fungible and can be reallocated to other customers, so the risk is manageable. This layer holds only partly — compute can indeed be resold, but if the entire industry is oversupplied at the same time, who do you sell it to?

He also flagged a more important shift: Nvidia no longer just sells hardware. It is partnering with Apollo, BlackRock, Blackstone, Brookfield, Goldman Sachs and KKR to mobilise USD 500 billion of third-party capital to build AI infrastructure, providing guaranteed minimum payments on part of the compute in exchange for a share of the upside.

Sell hardware once, collect rent for twenty years. This is an attempt to swap one-time revenue for perpetual revenue. If it works, the valuation framework has to be rewritten; if it doesn't, that USD 500 billion is enormous leverage.

So can we buy now?

USD 220.78, USD 5.33 trillion market cap, 28x TTM. On the company's own FY2028 guidance, 15x.

Is 15x expensive? On net income, it looks unreasonably cheap. On cash flow it does not: FY2027 free cash flow of about USD 140 billion puts the stock at 38x; FY2028 doubles that to USD 280 billion, or 19x. The two measures differ fourfold, and the gap is the 40% cash conversion rate.

Three scenarios: bear case USD 168 (FY2028 growth of only 30%), base case USD 248, bull case USD 386. Upside 12%, downside 24%, odds 1 : 0.5.

So the answer is clear: hold, do not chase.

Keep existing positions, because demand, supply and ecosystem are all intact. New money waits for one of two signals: the stock back below USD 180, or the November 17 FY2027Q3 report showing the 70% slope for the first time while receivables days hold at 65.

One last aside.

In this AI cycle, the most misread phrase is "compute is revenue." Compute is indeed revenue, but between revenue and cash sits a customer. Everything Nvidia is doing now shortens that distance — with equity, with guarantees, with financing platforms.

That is not necessarily bad. But it means you are not buying a chip company; you are buying a chip company plus an infrastructure investment company.

The former deserves 25x, the latter 12x. Combined, 15x. That is what the market is pricing today, and why I think it is neither cheap nor expensive.

What is really worth waiting for is not a lower stock price, but the day cash conversion returns to 60%. On that day, 15x will genuinely be cheap.

Conclusion

In one sentence: the income statement shows how strong demand is; the cash flow statement shows who is paying for it. Both are true. What decides whether this investment works is not whether AI continues, but whether those customers can turn compute into cash. Hold, do not chase; add below USD 180 or once the 70% slope is validated. Not investment advice.
