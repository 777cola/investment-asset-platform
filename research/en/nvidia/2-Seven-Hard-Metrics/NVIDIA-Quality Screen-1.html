Quality Screen — NVIDIA

Core thesis

Five of seven hard metrics pass. Revenue growth, gross margin, net margin, ROE and debt-to-asset ratio do more than pass — they clear the bar by a wide margin: growth +106%, gross margin 75.0%, net margin 62.0%, annualised ROE 124%, debt-to-asset ratio 28.5% and net cash of USD 23.2 billion.

The two failures are two sides of the same coin: operating cash flow is only 40.3% of net profit, and free cash flow only 35.8%. A year ago those figures stood at 58.1% and 50.9%. This is not accounting fraud: accounts receivable rose USD 22.346 billion in a single quarter, inventory rose USD 5.784 billion and prepayments rose USD 5.497 billion, while USD 7.771 billion of net profit came from revaluation of equity investments and brings in no cash.

Five of them have it easy — so easy it doesn't look like manufacturing

Gross margin 75.0%, net margin 62.0%, expense ratio 8.7% — three numbers that admit only one explanation: pricing power sits with Nvidia, not with its customers. Over the same period TSMC posted a gross margin of 67.7% (Q2 2026), Zhongji Innolight 46.59% on optical modules (H1) and Hon Hai 6.12% (Q2); Nvidia captures the thickest layer of the chain.

The balance sheet is clean too: total assets USD 320.272 billion, total liabilities USD 91.288 billion, debt-to-assets ratio 28.5%. Long-term borrowings rose from USD 7.469 billion to USD 32.366 billion — USD 24.896 billion of new debt issued this quarter, the first large-scale leverage-up in a decade, but against a USD 5.33 trillion market cap and USD 23.2 billion of net cash, not a problem.

ROE of 124% needs a discount: buybacks compress the denominator, with equity rising from USD 157.293 billion to USD 228.984 billion while USD 19.732 billion was repurchased and USD 6.047 billion paid in dividends in a single quarter. ROIC is the truer read on earning power: annualized NOPAT of about USD 212.8 billion against invested capital (equity + debt − cash and bonds) of about USD 205.9 billion, an ROIC of roughly 103%. Also top-tier.

Where did the cash go: three "pluses", one "illusion"

Operating cash flow of USD 24.077 billion against net income of USD 59.688 billion leaves a USD 35.6 billion gap. Item by item:

  • Accounts receivable +USD 22.346 billion. The balance rose from USD 40.713 billion to USD 63.059 billion, up 54.9% QoQ, while revenue grew only 18%. Receivable days stretched from 45 to 60; the 60-day figure is the company's own metric, acknowledged on the earnings call, with the explanation that large orders from investment-grade customers are delivered across multiple quarters, lengthening payment cycles.
  • Inventory +USD 5.784 billion, balance USD 31.575 billion, which management attributes to stockpiling ahead of the Vera Rubin launch. Inventory equals 119 days of cost of revenue.
  • Prepaid and other assets +USD 5.497 billion, corresponding to locked-up upstream capacity.
  • USD 7.771 billion in gains from revaluation of equity investments booked in net income but excluded from operating cash flow, equal to 10.9% of pre-tax profit.

Together the four items exactly explain the gap. This is not financial engineering; three things are happening at once: orders are getting bigger, payment terms are getting longer, and the company itself is funding its customers.

But is it fatal: not fatal, but it caps the valuation

Bottom line: not fatal, but enough to explain why this company deserves only 15x FY2028, not 30x.

Three reasons it is not fatal. First, the customers are investment grade: the four cloud players behind the hyperscaler segment's USD 48.7 billion in revenue all have expanding cash and operating cash flow, and the five major cloud players will spend roughly USD 800 billion in capex combined in 2026 and an estimated USD 1.3 trillion in 2027 (company call figures) — these are not marginal customers borrowing to buy chips. Second, the absolute receivables figure is manageable: USD 63 billion equals 0.6 quarters of revenue, and even a full bad-debt provision would only wipe out half a year's profit without damaging the balance sheet. Third, cash is not scarce: RMB 22.443 billion in cash plus RMB 34.143 billion in marketable securities, together with a USD 99 billion buyback authorization, give the company room to slow expansion at any time.

But it caps the valuation. A company with a 36% cash conversion rate will not be priced on DCF by the market, only on P/E, and a P/E the market dares not push too high. The real risk is not this period but the next: if quarterly revenue growth falls below 20%, receivables will crack first, then inventory, then that USD 108.5 billion in guarantees.

Beyond the seven, I also watch two numbers

First, interest coverage. The company issued USD 24.896 billion of new debt this quarter, with long-term borrowings jumping from USD 7.469 billion to USD 32.366 billion — apparently the first major leverage-up in a decade. But with cash plus bonds of USD 56.586 billion on the balance sheet, net cash of USD 23.220 billion and other net income of USD 7.773 billion (mainly interest and investment gains), interest expense is no pressure at all. This is not a risk but a reminder: when a net-cash company suddenly issues USD 25 billion of debt, the motive is not a shortage of money — it is to preserve cash for equity investments and capacity prepayments.

Second, customer concentration. The 10-Q discloses that in the first half of FY2027, three direct customers contributed 16%, 15% and 13% of revenue respectively, 44% combined, while receivables from the top five customers account for 70%. These figures say more about the nature of the business than gross margin does — it does not "sell to the whole world," it "sells to five people." If any one of those five moves to in-house development, that is a double-digit revenue gap.

Put these two numbers into the seven-point framework and the conclusion is unchanged: it passes five, and fails on cash conversion. But you will know what to watch — not gross margin, but payment terms and the customer list.

Conclusion

Five of seven pass. One failure is an accounting inevitability (investment gains never enter cash flow); the other is the cost of the business model (fronting capital to customers). The negative screen concludes "can keep, but at a discount": not the cleanest of first-tier companies, but a first-tier company whose revenue quality is deteriorating. One metric to watch: receivables turnover days. Holding at 60 days means it is a payment-terms issue; rising above 75 days means it is a demand issue.
