Core view

The three most common beliefs about Nvidia are all wrong: it is not an AI concept stock (it has USD 96.2 billion in single-quarter revenue and a 75% gross margin); it is not a chip company (it sells rack-level systems plus software plus capacity allocations); and it does not "earn more the more it sells" (gross margin has already begun to decline). Once the perception is reset, a plainer question remains: customers are buying its goods with borrowed money — how long can this business last.

The first insight: it is not a concept stock

From 2023 to 2024, the market traded Nvidia as an "AI concept," with valuations built on imagination. In August 2026, that phase ended.

Look at the numbers: quarterly revenue of USD 96.221 billion, +106% YoY; net income of USD 59.688 billion, +126% YoY; gross margin 75.0%, operating margin 66.2%. These are not the financials of a concept stock; these are the financials of a money printer. Put Nvidia next to any manufacturing company, and its 8.7% expense ratio would leave the other party with no place to hide.

More convincing is the customer mix. Of the USD 89.023 billion in data center revenue, hyperscale cloud accounted for USD 48.710 billion, and AI cloud, industrial and enterprise for USD 40.313 billion. The former is four of the world's best-cash-flow companies; the latter is dozens of sovereign nations, regional clouds and enterprises. If this were a concept, there would not be this much real money being spent.

So the first perception must change: it has gone from a "storytelling company" to a "financials-delivering company." That also means the market's pricing method for it is shifting from PS to PE, from "how far can growth go" to "how real is the profit." This shift is negative for the stock price—the premium enjoyed by growth stocks will be replaced by the discount applied to value stocks.

Second insight: it does not sell chips

Many people still understand Nvidia through "GPU shipments." That is the 2018 framework. The 2026 framework has three layers.

First layer: selling systems. Vera Rubin is not a card. It is an integrated delivery of Vera CPU + Rubin GPU + NVLink vertical interconnect + Spectrum-6 Ethernet + full rack + power and cooling. What customers buy is an "AI factory" that produces tokens the moment it is powered on. The company gave a quantified metric: commercial value per gigawatt of compute is USD 18 billion for Hopper, USD 25 billion for Grace Blackwell, and USD 40 billion for Vera Rubin.

Second layer: selling standards. CUDA is the default language for AI developers. Models, libraries, tutorials, talent — all are benchmarked against CUDA. The value of this layer is not in revenue but in switching costs: a customer changing platforms must rewrite code, retrain teams, and redo performance tuning, a cycle measured in years.

Third layer: selling allocation. The USD 279 billion in supply and capacity commitments is essentially using cash to lock up the next two years of HBM and advanced packaging capacity. In a 2026 of across-the-board shortages, "being able to get the goods" is itself a product, and only Nvidia can offer it.

These three layers combined are the source of its 75% gross margin. A company that only sells chips has a gross margin of around 50% (AMD 56.0%); only a company that sells systems + standards + allocation has 75%.

Third insight: selling more does not mean earning more

This is the most counterintuitive point. A company with revenue up 106% year over year is seeing gross margin decline.

The path is clear: FY2026Q2 72.4% → FY2027Q1 74.9% → FY2027Q2 75.0% → Q3 guidance 74.0% → Q4 guidance 71%–72% → FY2028 expected to recover to 72%–73%.

Why? The company is blunt about it: memory chip price increases are "far exceeding prior expectations," and prices will keep rising next year. To lock in capacity, Nvidia is buying up memory and manufacturing resources for the next two years in advance, writing the costs into its financials ahead of time.

This is an explicit trade: gross margin for certainty. In a shortage cycle, the trade pays off—selling one more dollar of product matters more than keeping one more point of gross margin. But the cost shows up in FY2028: if demand weakens by then, the locked-in costs become a burden.

So the third takeaway needs revising: Nvidia does not "make more money as it gets bigger"; the bigger it gets, the more it must trade gross margin for share. This is the same script as TSMC in 2019 and SK hynix in 2021—the winners of a shortage cycle are often the ones holding the inventory in the next downturn.

After the cognitive reset, what questions remain?

With those three perceptions corrected, Nvidia's image comes into focus: a company that has upgraded from a chip company to a systems company, from selling products to selling standards, from earning gross margin to earning share.

But one question remains unanswered, and it is the root of all questions: where does the customers' money come from?

Three numbers in the financials answer half of it: USD 93.94 billion in equity securities (USD 35.137 billion six months ago), USD 108.5 billion in credit guarantee ceilings, and roughly a quarter of next year's revenue requiring support from its own balance sheet.

In other words, Nvidia is not just selling compute power; it is supplying capital to the buyers of compute power. This is not financial fraud, nor an accounting trick — it is an extension of the business model: translating "the customer cannot afford it" into "I will help you buy it."

Whether this extension holds depends on one thing: whether these customers can ultimately make money from compute power. If they can, Nvidia is the toll booth of this generation of infrastructure and deserves a 25x valuation; if they cannot, it is this generation's Cisco and deserves 12x. The share price of USD 220.78 sits exactly between these two answers.

Conclusion

Conclusion of the cognitive reset: treat it as "a systems company that delivers financials plus an infrastructure company that collects rent," not a concept stock and not a pure chip company. On that view, the RMB 96.2 billion revenue is real, the 75% gross margin is real, and the downward slope of gross margin is real too. The core question left is where the customers' money comes from — the financials give no answer; only the 2027 quarterly reports will.
