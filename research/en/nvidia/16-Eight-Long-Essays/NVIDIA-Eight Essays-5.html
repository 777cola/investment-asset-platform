Core thesis

Competition is not about "whose chip is faster" but about "which demand standardizes first." Training is customized demand, where customers must use the best general-purpose platform; inference is standardized demand, where customers choose the cheapest solution. Nvidia is irreplaceable in training but is being squeezed at both ends in inference by ASICs and LPUs. Once inference accounts for more than half, the nature of the moat changes.

Training and inference: division of labor, not substitution

The most common misjudgment in the industry is treating "training" and "inference" as one market. They are two markets with opposite commercial logic.

Training demands "the most powerful general-purpose compute": model architectures are still evolving, with attention mechanisms, parallelism methods and precision formats changing every six months, so customers must buy the most flexible platform. NVIDIA holds a near-monopoly here, because CUDA lets any new architecture run fastest.

Inference demands "the cheapest cost per token": the model is fixed, so chips can be customized for that single model. General-purpose GPU flexibility then becomes waste, and ASIC advantages emerge. Broadcom's XPU, Google's TPU and Amazon's Trainium all follow this logic.

The company itself acknowledged this division on its earnings call: Groq 3 LPX is an "interactive AI inference accelerator" suited to high-ASP, low-latency workloads, but "the vast majority of the world's data centers will still be anchored by Vera Rubin NVL72." In translation: general platforms hold the core, specialized chips attack the edge.

To judge when this line shifts: watch inference revenue as a share of data center revenue. The company does not disclose it separately today, but management says "the spread of agentic AI is driving CPU and inference demand." If inference exceeds half by 2027 and NVIDIA's share starts to slip, division becomes substitution.

The first layer of competition: AMD chased for three years without gaining share

AMD is the most easily overestimated competitor, and also the most easily underestimated one.

Easily overestimated: Q2 2026 data center revenue was USD 6.718 billion, up 107% year over year — the same growth rate as Nvidia, but at one-thirteenth the scale (Nvidia: USD 89.023 billion). Three years of chasing, the same growth rate, and no share shift. This shows that in the training market, parameters are not the deciding factor — the ecosystem is.

Easily underestimated: AMD's strategy is not to "beat Nvidia" but to "offer customers an option when they want to push prices down." As long as it can deliver usable large-scale clusters, it can help customers bargain in procurement negotiations. This "second supplier" role will, over time, compress Nvidia's gross margin by 3–5 percentage points.

So AMD's threat lies not in share but in price. Nvidia's Q4 gross margin fell to 71%–72%; the company attributes this to memory price increases, but customers using AMD to push prices down is also part of the backdrop.

The second layer of competition: ASIC is the most structural

The logic behind custom ASICs is not that they are more powerful than GPUs, but that they are cheaper. Broadcom's XPU has built seven generations of TPUs for Google and custom chips for customers including Meta and ByteDance; Amazon's Trainium is used at scale for its own inference; OpenAI's Jalapeño is claimed to outperform Blackwell.

Why cheaper? Three reasons. First, stripping out generality: supporting only one model architecture saves 30%–50% of die area. Second, stripping out the software stack: customers use their own compilers and do not need CUDA compatibility layers. Third, stripping out the middleman: tape out directly at TSMC and keep the margin.

Nvidia's response runs on two tracks. One is to bring Groq in-house (about USD 20 billion) and use LPUs to attack low-latency inference — Groq 3 LPX is already in mass production, and the company claims token output per second is nearly 4x that of competitors, with Yandex as its first customer. The other is to make rack-scale systems more complex, so customers cannot simply buy chips and build their own.

But both tracks are defensive. The real offensive is to keep CUDA's advantage on the inference side too — and the difficulty is that inference customers do not care about the ecosystem, only about cost.

Cycle position: third year of shortage, financing conditions tightening

Timeline of this upcycle: Hopper in 2024, Blackwell in 2025, Vera Rubin in 2026. Company guidance is that shortages persist through the end of FY2028, with all four constraints tight: wafers, HBM, power and data centers.

Three demand-side figures: capex at the five major cloud providers of about RMB 800 billion in 2026 and RMB 1.3 trillion in 2027; cloud industry backlog above RMB 2 trillion; NeoCloud installed capacity from 3GW to 8GW.

But financing conditions are tightening. In mid-August, Gundlach publicly warned that AI chip financing resembles the eve of the 2007 subprime crisis; the market has begun debating "whether depreciation schedules are reasonable" and "the payback period on compute investment." These debates will not change 2026 orders, but they will affect 2027 financing costs.

My cycle call is "three years of buildout, two years of digestion": 2027 is the peak, 2028 enters digestion, and growth falling from 70% to below 20% is normal. The real risk to guard against is order declines and credit contraction happening at the same time—that is the 2001 Cisco script.

Conclusion

Competitive conclusion: in the near term no one can replace Nvidia in the training market (CUDA + systems + capacity); the inference market is being sliced away layer by layer by ASICs; AMD's role is to compress pricing, not to replace. Cyclically, this is the third year of shortage and the year financing conditions begin to tighten. Key things to watch: the inference revenue share and Nvidia's share of inference — these two numbers determine whether it trades at 20x or 12x after 2027.
