Industry Research (Munger's Lens) — NVIDIA

Core thesis

Nvidia's position in the AI compute chain has few historical parallels: upstream, it can lock in capacity through prepayments (USD 279 billion in commitments); downstream, it can allocate product (whoever gets chips first brings models online first); and in between, it controls the software standard (CUDA). The last company to hold all three at once was Intel in the 1990s — and Intel's ending was to lose that position.

Industry-chain position: the only company holding all three cards at once

Upstream: TSMC's advanced nodes and CoWoS, SK hynix and Samsung HBM, optical modules, power and cooling. Midstream: GPU design, NVLink and Spectrum-6 networking, rack-scale systems (GB300 / Vera Rubin), CUDA and the inference software stack. Downstream: the four hyperscalers, NeoClouds, model companies, sovereign AI, industrial and enterprise.

Three cards: architecture definition (setting how the next generation computes), capacity allocation (USD 279 billion locks up HBM and advanced packaging that others cannot get), software standard (CUDA is the default language of global AI developers). TSMC has only capacity, SK hynix has only capacity, the cloud vendors have only demand. Only Nvidia has all three.

One detail, though: this year it picked up a fourth card — capital. A USD 500 billion third-party financing platform, USD 93.9 billion of equity investment, USD 108.5 billion of guarantees. This card lets it create demand, and also forces it to bear the cost if demand fails.

Competitive landscape: a three-tier structure, with the outermost tier the most dangerous

The first layer is AMD, chasing the same product. Its MI series matches Nvidia on hardware specs, but the software ecosystem remains the weak spot. Data centre revenue in 2026Q2 was USD 6.718 billion, up 107% YoY — the same growth rate on one-thirteenth the scale. This layer is not dangerous: customers switch to it to drive down prices, not to replace Nvidia.

The second layer is custom ASICs: Broadcom, Google TPU, Amazon Trainium and OpenAI's Jalapeño. The logic is not stronger than GPUs, but cheaper — for inference on a single model, unit cost can be a fraction of the alternative. Nvidia's counter is to bring Groq's LPU in-house (Groq 3 LPX is already in mass production, and the company claims token output per second is nearly 4x that of competitors), fighting dedicated chips with dedicated chips.

The third layer is regional substitution: Huawei Ascend is the only option in the China market. This layer does not affect global revenue, but it has pushed China data centre revenue to zero — this quarter's guidance explicitly excludes China data centre compute revenue, and H200 units delivered compliantly in Q2 accounted for less than 1% of data centre revenue. China has gone from "largest increment" to "zero." That has already happened; it is no longer a risk but an established fact.

Cycle position: third year of shortage, most expensive year

The upcycle timeline is clear: Hopper ramps in 2024, Blackwell in 2025, Vera Rubin in 2026. Management's line is that shortages persist at least through the end of FY2028, with wafers, HBM, power and data centers all tight.

Three demand-side numbers: capex at the five major cloud players is about USD 800 billion in 2026 and projected at USD 1.3 trillion in 2027; cloud industry backlog exceeds USD 2 trillion; NeoCloud installed capacity goes from 3GW at end-2025 to 8GW at end-2026.

The Munger-style question: is this capex cycle a three-year story or a ten-year story? My call is "three years of buildout, two years of digestion." 2027 is the construction peak; 2028 enters digestion, when growth falling from 70% to below 20% is normal, not a collapse. The real risk to guard against: if the USD 1.3 trillion of 2027 is funded by debt and equity issuance, then the 2028 digestion period brings order declines and credit contraction at the same time, and the two amplify each other.

Conclusion

Best positioned, most expensive cycle. It is the only company in the chain holding all four cards — a structure seen once in twenty years — yet it sits in year three of the capex cycle, when valuations are richest. The metric to track is not orders but the financing structure of cloud capex: when the share covered by operating cash flow falls below 60%, that is the harbinger of a cycle turn.
