Core thesis

The essence of this business is selling "the right to use compute," not selling chips. What customers pay for is time—launch a quarter late and your model falls a version behind. So price is not the sensitive variable; lead time is. But something changed in 2026: the payers spread from four cash-rich cloud providers to a batch of AI labs and compute-leasing firms that are not yet profitable. It is still a good business, but the payers have changed.

Who pays: five people paid 44%

Start with the customer structure. The 10-Q discloses that in the first half of FY2027, three direct customers contributed 16%, 15% and 13% of revenue respectively, 44% combined; receivables from the top five customers account for 70%. This is not a business of "selling to the whole world," it is a business of "selling to five people."

The market broadly knows who these five are: Microsoft, Amazon, Google, Meta, Oracle. What they share is abundant cash and capex funded by operating cash flow. Amazon announced this quarter that it will deploy an additional 2 million GPUs from this quarter through fiscal Q2 2029, paired with Vera CPUs—only one kind of customer can place such an order: one that can pay without relying on financing.

But they are not the fastest-growing. Hyperscale cloud rose 102% year over year, ACIE rose 138% year over year. ACIE houses NeoCloud (CoreWeave, Nebius, Nscale, Firmus, YTL), sovereign AI, enterprise and AI-native companies. This segment accounts for 45% of data center revenue and is the fastest-growing, weakest-paying part.

Why pay: revenue per kWh sold is doubling

Why must customers buy? The company offered a very useful framing: the commercial value per gigawatt of compute — USD 18 billion in the Hopper era, USD 25 billion for Grace Blackwell, USD 40 billion for Vera Rubin. Same rack, same power, more than double the revenue it can generate.

That number explains three things. First, why customers are willing to queue: they are not buying chips, they are buying "30x higher throughput per megawatt and 35x lower cost per token" (the company's comparison against Grace Blackwell Ultra). Second, why price increases stick: prices rise starting FY2028Q1, passing through memory cost inflation, and customers accept it because the revenue growth from compute outpaces cost growth. Third, why in-house chips are not easily substitutable: customers' own XPUs target inference for a single cloud and a single workload, whereas Nvidia sells a general-purpose asset spanning the full chain of data processing, pre-training, post-training and agentic inference. The value of generality is that the asset can be reused, and reuse means it keeps earning throughout its depreciation life.

The simplicity of the business lies here too: it does not matter what customers do with the compute, only that they keep buying the next generation.

How long can it pay: that is the only question that needs answering

Duan Yongping-style questions boil down to one: will anyone still be paying in ten years? My answer has three layers.

Layer one: demand has not peaked. Global AI venture funding exceeded USD 400 billion in the first half of 2026, roughly 70% of it for compute procurement; the five major cloud providers hold over USD 2 trillion in backlog. These are company figures, but the direction can be verified by third parties — the cloud providers' own capex guidance is being revised up, not down.

Layer two: supply is the bottleneck. The company puts it plainly: real demand is about 100%, guidance covers only 70%, and the entire gap is capacity — wafers, HBM, power, data centers, all four are tight. The shortage lasts at least through the end of FY2028. When supply is short, the seller has the easiest business in town — that is common sense.

Layer three, the most uncertain: do the people paying have the money? Of the USD 400 billion in AI venture funding in the first half of 2026, how much ultimately becomes Nvidia revenue, and how much becomes Nvidia receivables and guarantees? By the company's own account, AI lab business requiring support from Nvidia's balance sheet will be about a quarter of total business next year. A quarter — that ratio is what turns this from "selling shovels" into "selling shovels plus financing the buyers."

So my judgment: the business is good, good enough that it needs no argument; the payers are changing, shifting to a group that needs Nvidia's help. That does not change the business. It changes how the business should be valued.

Conclusion

A good business, but the payment structure is deteriorating. The hyperscale cloud half still generates rock-solid cash flow; the ACIE half is propped up by financing. The former supports a 20x valuation; the latter cannot support 30x. One metric to watch: ACIE as a share of data center revenue. It is 45% now; if it climbs above 55% by 2027 while payment terms keep lengthening, the quality of this business is heading downhill.
