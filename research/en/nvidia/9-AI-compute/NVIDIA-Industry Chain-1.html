Core thesis

Profit pools along the AI compute chain are highly uneven. Upstream memory (Micron FY26Q3 gross margin 84.9%) and foundry (TSMC 2026Q2 67.7%) capture the bulk of the economics, Nvidia (75.0%) takes the fattest cut in the middle, downstream assembly (Hon Hai 6.1%) scrapes by on thin margins, and cloud providers and model companies absorb the final depreciation and losses.

Nvidia's position is unique: it can lock in upstream capacity through prepayments (USD 279 billion in commitments), allocate supply downstream, and control the software standards in between. The only precedent for such dual bargaining power in manufacturing history is Intel in the 1990s. The difference: Intel did not have to backstop its customers' obligations; Nvidia now does.

Upstream: two companies hold the physical bottleneck

The biggest money in this AI cycle is being made not by chipmakers but by memory suppliers and foundries.

Memory. Micron's FY26Q3 gross margin was 84.9% and net margin 68%, on quarterly revenue of USD 41.46 billion, up 346% YoY; SK hynix's 2026Q1 operating margin was 71.5%. These are outlier margins for manufacturing, driven by HBM supply falling short of demand: in early 2026 DRAM contract prices rose 60%–70% and NAND more than 100%, and the sell-side expects the shortage to persist into 2027. Nvidia's Q4 gross margin fell to 71%–72%, eroded by this layer.

Foundry. TSMC's 2026Q2 gross margin was 67.7%, after-tax net profit TWD 706.56 billion, up 77.4% YoY, with HPC at 66% of revenue. CoWoS advanced packaging is the hard bottleneck, and 2026 capex was raised to USD 60–64 billion. TSMC's pricing power rests on there being no second supplier, yet it dares not raise prices to memory-like levels—because its customers' alternative is not to build at all.

What the two upstream players share: they do not need to know whether AI applications make money; as long as Nvidia's orders keep coming, they keep earning. That is the lowest-risk position in the entire chain.

Midstream: Nvidia's position, and why it is moving downstream

NVIDIA's role in the chain is a threefold overlay: architecture definition (how the next generation computes), system integration (GB300 / Vera Rubin racks) and software standards (CUDA). The result: it captures the fattest gross-margin layer of the entire chain at 75.0%, and it collects customer cash upfront — 60-day payment terms are the exception, not the norm.

This year, however, it did something that violates its midstream identity: it moved downstream. A USD 500 billion financing platform (Apollo, BlackRock, Blackstone, Brookfield, Goldman Sachs, KKR), USD 93.9 billion in customer equity, USD 108.5 billion in credit guarantees, USD 36 billion in cloud-service purchase commitments. It went from "selling equipment to cloud providers" to "helping customers build clouds, then selling equipment to those clouds."

Why? The company's stated reason: demand is real, and what customers lack is balance sheet, not orders. That reasoning holds in a shortage cycle: when supply is limited, whoever can make the customer able to pay sells more goods. The cost is that it has moved downstream credit risk onto itself.

In one sentence: NVIDIA is the toll booth on this chain, and it has now started making loans to the cars driving through.

Power is another underappreciated battleground. The company itself says building an AI factory requires locking in land, power and shell space 2–3 years ahead; with SB Energy it has secured land, power and shell at the Portsmouth, Ohio campus, with a first phase planned at 4.25GW for OpenAI's use, and each generation of AI factory able to deploy roughly 1.5 million GPUs. Power has gone from "infrastructure" to "scarce resource," and NVIDIA has already started buying it directly — this is its second move upstream.

Downstream: cloud providers profit; model companies burn cash; NeoClouds take on debt

The downstream splits into three layers, each with a completely different financial profile.

Hyperscalers (USD 48.7 billion revenue, 55% of the data center market). Microsoft, Amazon, Google, Meta and Oracle will spend a combined ~USD 800 billion in capex in 2026 and an estimated USD 1.3 trillion in 2027. Their compute can be sold to others (cloud revenue) and used in their own businesses (advertising, search, e-commerce), so returns on investment are the easiest to calculate. This layer is the ballast for the entire chain.

NeoCloud (part of ACIE, and the main increment within the USD 40.3 billion). CoreWeave, Nebius, Nscale, Firmus and YTL will go from 3GW installed at end-2025 to 8GW at end-2026. Their business model is "buy chips, rent them out," with thin balance sheets, expanding on private credit and project finance. This layer is the most fragile link in the entire chain, and the main counterparty Nvidia guarantees.

Model companies (OpenAI, Anthropic, xAI). Revenue is rising, but they are broadly loss-making and pay their compute bills with equity financing. Global AI venture funding exceeded USD 400 billion in the first half of 2026, with about 70% going to compute purchases — a ratio that shows most venture money turns into Nvidia revenue.

Cycle position: third year of shortage, the most expensive year

The roadmap is clear: Hopper ramps in 2024, Blackwell in 2025, Vera Rubin in 2026. Guidance points to the shortage lasting at least through the end of FY2028, with all four constraints tight: wafers, HBM, power and data-center space.

Three demand-side data points: capex at the five major cloud providers is about USD 800 billion in 2026 and USD 1.3 trillion in 2027; cloud industry backlog exceeds USD 2 trillion; NeoCloud installed capacity rises from 3GW to 8GW.

Our view: "three years of buildout, two years of digestion." 2027 is the construction peak, 2028 enters digestion, and growth slowing from 70% to below 20% is normal, not a collapse.The real risk is the financing structure — if more than half of the USD 1.3 trillion in 2027 comes from debt and equity financing, the 2028 digestion period will see order declines and credit contraction simultaneously, with the two amplifying each other.

The best indicator of where we are in the cycle is not Nvidia's orders but the cloud providers' capex/operating cash flow ratio. That ratio is now near 60%; historically, periods when it exceeded 80% (telecom in 2000, cloud computing in 2021) all peaked within two years.

Conclusion

Profit along the chain is concentrating upstream and midstream, risk is sinking downstream and ultimately flowing back to the midstream through guarantees. Nvidia holds the strongest position, but it has itself dismantled part of that protection. On the cycle, this is the third year of shortage and the most expensive year for valuations.Track three numbers: the magnitude of memory price increases, the ratio of cloud capex to operating cash flow, and NeoCloud financing costs. The first two determine revenue; the third determines whether guarantees turn into bad debt.
