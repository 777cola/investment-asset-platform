Nvidia's earnings: the good news is in the income statement, the bad news is in the cash flow statement — NVIDIA

Body

The print is strong, but the most valuable disclosure in this report sits well below the income statement.

Let us finish with the good news first

Nvidia reported FY2027 second-quarter results after the close on August 26. Revenue came in at USD 96.221 billion, up 106% YoY and 18% QoQ, with data center revenue of USD 89.023 billion, up 117% YoY. Gross margin was 75.0% and net income reached USD 59.688 billion, up 126% YoY.

For the next quarter, management guides to USD 108 billion. The premise matters: the guidance assumes zero China data center compute revenue. In other words, a company that has cut off the relevant revenue from the world's second-largest market can still guide to 12% QoQ growth.

Rarer still, the company gave more than a year of forward guidance for the first time: FY2028 revenue growth of about 70%. The CFO framed it as "this guidance is supply-constrained," implying real demand is higher than 70%. Jensen Huang was blunter on the call: "If we were not constrained, our growth would be much higher."

Now the bad news: not all the money has been collected

Operating cash flow in the same filing's cash flow statement was USD 24.077 billion.

Against net income of USD 59.688 billion, that implies a conversion rate of 40.3%, down from 58.1% a year earlier. Free cash flow was USD 21.341 billion, a conversion rate of 35.8% versus 50.9% a year earlier.

Where did the money go? Three items: accounts receivable up USD 22.346 billion (balance USD 63.059 billion, days sales outstanding from 45 to 60 days); inventory up USD 5.784 billion (balance USD 31.575 billion, stocking for new products); prepayments up USD 5.497 billion. Separately, USD 7.771 billion of net income came from revaluation of equity investments and brought in no cash at all.

The company attributes the payment terms to large orders from investment-grade customers being delivered across multiple quarters, which extends the payment cycle. The explanation is reasonable, but it also says one thing — customers are getting bigger, and they are paying more slowly.

Look again at the balance sheet: 30% of assets are client equity stakes

Nvidia did three things this quarter it had never done before.

First, it issued USD 24.9 billion of debt, lifting long-term borrowings from USD 7.469 billion to USD 32.366 billion. With USD 23.2 billion of net cash, the company does not lack money; the bond sale was about keeping cash free for use elsewhere.

Second, it bought USD 15.8 billion of equity (USD 42.4 billion cumulative in the first half). It now holds USD 42.783 billion of marketable equity and USD 51.157 billion of non-marketable equity — USD 93.940 billion combined, or 29.3% of total assets, up from 16.3% six months ago. Management says it has invested nearly USD 50 billion in frontier AI labs.

Third, it extended credit guarantees to data center projects, with maximum total exposure of USD 108.5 billion. Add USD 279 billion of supply commitments and USD 36 billion of cloud service purchase commitments, and off-balance-sheet commitments total roughly USD 530.5 billion, per the Australian Financial Review's tally of the 10-Q footnotes.

Together, these mark something new: Nvidia has begun financing its own customers.

How does management explain this, and does the explanation hold up?

Jensen Huang addressed it head-on: "We know the outside world will read this kind of arrangement as circular financing." His defence rests on three arguments. First, the technology at these AI labs is proven and demand is real. Second, the capital invested amounts to only a small fraction of free cash flow over the same period. Third, compute is fungible and can be reallocated to other customers, so the risk is manageable.

Of the three, the first is verifiable — revenue at these labs is rising. The third holds only partly: compute can indeed be resold, but not when the entire industry is oversupplied at once. The second warrants the most caution — by the company's own account, the AI lab business requiring support from Nvidia's balance sheet will represent roughly a quarter of total business next year.

A quarter is not a rounding error.

So what should be done now?

USD 220.78, USD 5.33 trillion market cap, 28x TTM. On the company's own FY2028 guidance, 15x.

Is 15x expensive? On net income, it looks unreasonably cheap. On cash flow, it is not: FY2027 free cash flow of about USD 140 billion puts the stock on 38x, and FY2028 free cash flow doubles to USD 280 billion, or 19x. The two measures differ fourfold, and that gap is the 40% cash conversion rate.

Three scenarios: bear case USD 168 (FY2028 growth of just 30%), base case USD 248, bull case USD 386. That implies 12% upside against 24% downside, a reward-to-risk ratio of 1 : 0.5.

The answer is clear: hold, do not chase. Existing positions can be held, because demand, supply and ecosystem are all intact; new money should wait for one of two signals — the stock falling below USD 180, or the November 17 FY2027Q3 results showing the 70% slope for the first time, with receivables days holding at 65.

Conclusion

One-line summary: the income statement shows how strong demand is; the cash flow statement shows who is paying for it. Both are true. What decides whether this investment works is not whether AI continues, but whether those customers can turn compute into cash.Hold, do not chase; add below USD 180 or once the 70% slope is validated.
