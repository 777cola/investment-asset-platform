Body

This is a publishable piece. The conclusion in one sentence: the results are strong, but the most valuable information in this report is not on the income statement.

Let us finish with the good news first

After the close on August 26, Nvidia reported FY2027 second-quarter results. Revenue was USD 96.221 billion, up 106% year on year and 18% quarter on quarter. Data center revenue was USD 89.023 billion, up 117% year on year. Gross margin was 75.0%, and net income was USD 59.688 billion, up 126% year on year.

For the next quarter, the company guides to USD 108 billion. Note the premise behind this guidance: it assumes zero China data center compute revenue. In other words, a company that has cut off the relevant revenue from the world's second-largest market can still guide to 12% quarter-on-quarter growth.

Rarer still, the company for the first time gave more than a year of forward guidance: FY2028 revenue growth of about 70%. The CFO's framing was that "this guidance is supply-constrained," implying that real demand is higher than 70%. Jensen Huang put it more bluntly on the call: "If we were not constrained, our growth would be much higher."

Now the bad news: not all the money has been collected

In the cash flow statement of the same filing, operating cash flow was USD 24.077 billion.

Net income USD 59.688 billion, operating cash flow USD 24.077 billion. Conversion rate 40.3%. A year earlier that figure was 58.1%. Free cash flow USD 21.341 billion, conversion rate 35.8%, versus 50.9% a year earlier.

Where did the money go? Three items: accounts receivable up USD 22.346 billion (balance USD 63.059 billion, days sales outstanding from 45 to 60 days); inventory up USD 5.784 billion (balance USD 31.575 billion, stocking for new products); prepayments up USD 5.497 billion. Separately, USD 7.771 billion of net income came from revaluation of equity investments and brought in no cash at all.

The company's explanation for the payment terms: large orders from investment-grade customers are delivered across multiple quarters, extending the payment cycle. The explanation is reasonable, but it also says one thing — customers are getting bigger, and they are paying more slowly.

Take another look at the balance sheet: 30% of assets are client equity stakes

This quarter Nvidia did three things it had never done before.

First, it issued USD 24.9 billion in debt. Long-term borrowings jumped from USD 7.469 billion to USD 32.366 billion. This is a company with USD 23.2 billion in net cash — it does not lack money; the bond sale was to leave cash available elsewhere.

Second, it bought USD 15.8 billion of equity (USD 42.4 billion cumulative in the first half). It now holds USD 42.783 billion of marketable equity and USD 51.157 billion of non-marketable equity, USD 93.940 billion combined, or 29.3% of total assets. Six months ago that ratio was 16.3%. The company says it has invested nearly USD 50 billion in frontier AI labs.

Third, it provided credit guarantees for data center projects, with a maximum total exposure of USD 108.5 billion. Add USD 279 billion in supply commitments and USD 36 billion in cloud service purchase commitments, and off-balance-sheet commitments total roughly USD 530.5 billion, per the Australian Financial Review's tally of the 10-Q footnotes.

Taken together, Nvidia is doing something new: it has begun financing its own customers.

How does the company explain it, and does the explanation hold up

Jensen Huang addressed it directly: "We know the outside world will read this kind of arrangement as circular financing." His explanation has three layers: the technology at these AI labs is proven and the demand is real; the money invested is only a small fraction of free cash flow over the same period; compute is fungible and can be reallocated to other customers, so the risk is manageable.

Of the three, the first can be verified (revenue at these labs is rising), the third holds partly (compute can indeed be resold, but not when the whole industry is oversupplied at the same time), and the second warrants the most caution — by the company's own account, next year the AI lab business that needs support from Nvidia's balance sheet will account for about a quarter of total business.

A quarter is not a rounding error.

So what should be done now?

USD 220.78, USD 5.33 trillion market cap, 28x TTM. On the company's own FY2028 guidance, 15x.

Is 15x expensive? On net income, it is unreasonably cheap. On cash flow: FY2027 free cash flow of about USD 140 billion, 38x; FY2028 doubles to USD 280 billion, 19x. The two measures differ fourfold, and that gap is the 40% cash conversion rate.

Three scenarios: bear case USD 168 (FY2028 growth of just 30%), base case USD 248, bull case USD 386. 12% upside, 24% downside, a reward-to-risk ratio of 1 : 0.5.

So the answer is clear: hold, do not chase. Existing positions can be held, because demand, supply and ecosystem are all intact; new money should wait for one of two signals — the stock falling below USD 180, or the November 17 FY2027Q3 results showing the 70% slope for the first time, with receivables days holding at 65.

Conclusion

One-line summary: the income statement shows how strong demand is; the cash flow statement shows who is paying for it. Both statements are true. What decides whether this investment succeeds is not whether AI continues, but whether those customers can turn compute into cash.Hold, do not chase; add below USD 180 or once the 70% slope is validated.
