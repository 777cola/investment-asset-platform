Core thesis

For a Duan Yongping-style investor, this earnings report carries only one piece of information: the quality of the business has not changed; what has changed is the capital structure of this business. A 75% gross margin, an 8.7% expense ratio, customer prepayments—not one of the metrics he values has deteriorated. What needs reassessment is this: customers have shifted from four cloud providers to a group of financing-dependent AI companies.

He can sum up this business in three sentences.

What does it sell? Compute. Who buys? The world's richest companies, lining up to buy. Why must they buy? Because launching a quarter late puts your model a version behind.

Three sentences, and he knows it is a good business. A 75% gross margin, an 8.7% expense ratio and a 66.2% operating margin — put those three numbers together and pricing power sits with the seller. Among the companies he has bought, only Apple's gross-margin structure comes close, but Apple has to manage supply chains, channels and inventory; Nvidia only has to manage capacity.

He would also notice one detail: of the RMB 59.688 billion in net profit, RMB 7.771 billion is revaluation of equity investments. He would strike that out outright. "Money not earned by the core business does not count as profit." After striking it out, net profit is RMB 53.2 billion and net margin 55.3% — still impossibly good.

What he takes issue with is this line item: receivables of RMB 63 billion

The line Duan Yongping watches closest is "customers pay first." Apple collects receivables within a very short cycle; Moutai's distributors pay upfront. Nvidia's payment terms are 60 days, versus 45 days a quarter ago.

For him, longer payment terms are a signal, not an accounting detail. They usually mean one of three things: customers have weakened, competition has intensified, or the company is trading terms for orders.Management attributes the shift to "large orders from investment-grade customers delivered across multiple quarters." The explanation holds, but it also points to a customer mix tilting toward large projects and slower payment.

He also looks at inventory: RMB 31.575 billion, equal to 119 days of cost of revenue. Stocking up ahead of new products is normal, but if demand fails to follow through next quarter, that RMB 31.6 billion becomes an impairment.

Put the two together, and his verdict would be: "Still a good business, but watch how it collects its money."

His conclusion: a good business, waiting for the price

Duan Yongping does not change his view of a company on the back of a single earnings report; only price changes what he does.

USD 220.78, 28x TTM, 15x FY2028. His rule: where a company can sustain above 20% growth over a visible five years, he will pay 20x; where it can sustain 50%, he will pay 25x. Nvidia's growth comfortably clears that bar, so 15x looks cheap.

But the denominator of that 15x is the company's own guidance. He does not use other people's forecasts to set his own price. On his method: FY2027 free cash flow of USD 140 billion against a market cap of USD 5.33 trillion gives 38x; even if FY2028 free cash flow doubles, it is 19x. Paying 19x for a company whose growth may fall from 70% to 20% is not something he would do.

His answer is therefore clear: "I understand this company, but I don't like the price. I'll wait until it's below 180."

Conclusion

Duan Yongping-style verdict: business 9 points, financials 7 points (marked down for payment terms and cash conversion), price 4 points.The financials did not change his view of the company's quality; they merely added one item to watch — accounts receivable. As he put it: "The time to buy a good company is when it makes a mistake, but right now it hasn't made a mistake, it's just expensive."
