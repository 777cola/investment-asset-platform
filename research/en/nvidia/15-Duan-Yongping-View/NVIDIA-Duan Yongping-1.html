Q: Is this a good business?

Answer: Good — good enough that no argument is needed.

Gross margin 75.0%, operating margin 66.2%, expense ratio 8.7%, net margin 62.0%. Put these four numbers together and they say one thing: pricing power sits with the seller, and it is very strong pricing power.

What is the standard for a good business? Mine has three: a product that can sell for many years without changes, customers who cannot switch away, and no need to borrow to grow. Nvidia scores full marks on the first two. On the third, it stopped qualifying this year — it issued USD 24.9 billion of debt, bought USD 42.4 billion of customer equity in H1, and extended USD 108.5 billion of guarantees.

So the accurate answer is: this is a good business, but this year it began growing with other people's money and its own balance sheet, and that deserves a markdown in the price.

Q: Will it still be around in ten years?

Answer: most likely yes, but the shape will change.

Will AI compute demand still exist in ten years? Yes. Because "intelligence" has become something priced per token, and tokens require compute to produce. That demand will not disappear; it will only get cheaper.

But Nvidia in ten years may not be the Nvidia of today. Three changes have already begun. First, from selling chips to selling racks (Vera Rubin ships as complete racks; customers buy an "AI factory," not a GPU). Second, from selling hardware to collecting rent (the USD 50 billion financing platform, with guaranteed payments in exchange for a share of revenue). Third, from a GPU company to a CPU + networking + LPU company (Vera CPU, Spectrum-X, Groq LPX).

All three changes are right, but each one makes the company more complex. The companies I like are those still doing the same thing ten years later — Apple is like that, Moutai is like that. What Nvidia does ten years from now will be very different from today.

Q: Can you understand it?

Answer: I understand the core business; I do not understand the off-balance-sheet items.

The core business in one sentence: selling compute, customers lining up, 75% gross margin. This I understand, and with high confidence.

Three off-balance-sheet lines I do not understand: supply and capacity commitments of USD 279 billion (I know the amount, not how it is handled if demand weakens), data center credit guarantees of USD 108.5 billion (I know the ceiling, not who the counterparties are or what the trigger conditions are), and cloud service purchase commitments of USD 36 billion (I know the amount, not the purpose).

My principle: what I do not understand does not count toward value, and then I apply a discount to what remains. So I split Nvidia into two parts—one is "a good business selling compute," worth 20x; the other is "an investment company financing its customers," worth 8x. Adding the two, USD 220 is roughly a fair price, not cheap.

Q: At what price to buy?

Answer: below USD 180.

Where does that number come from? FY2028 EPS of USD 14.61 (on revenue of RMB 641.3 billion and a 55% net margin), and USD 180 corresponds to 12.3x. Buying a company whose growth may fall from 70% to 20% at 12x means you don't lose even if guidance is cut by 30%.

Another approach: FY2027 free cash flow of about USD 140 billion; USD 180 implies a market cap of USD 4.34 trillion, or 31x; if FY2028 free cash flow doubles to USD 280 billion, that is 15.5x. Both measures sit in the "not expensive" range.

Why not wait for cheaper? Because a company like this won't give you an 8x opportunity. The last time it broke below 180 was late July (a quarterly low of 190.01), and before that, 2025. Wait too long and what you get may be "earnings above consensus plus a stock that doesn't look back." So my approach: take 5% at the current price, add to 8% on a drop to 180, and revisit the thesis on a drop to 150.

Q: What is the biggest fear?

Answer: The biggest fear is that its customers run out of money.

Not AMD, not Broadcom, not Huawei. None of these three can take its position — AMD has chased for three years without moving share, Broadcom can only take custom silicon, Huawei can only take China.

The fear is this: the company says next year its AI lab business requiring NVIDIA balance-sheet support will account for about a quarter of total business. A quarter — USD 160 billion of revenue — backed by a group of companies that are not yet profitable.

If these customers cannot raise money in 2027, how does it transmit? First payment delays (payment terms stretching from 60 days to 90 days), then guarantee compensation (the first part of the USD 108.5 billion), then inventory impairment (the USD 279 billion of committed purchases that cannot be sold). All three happen within two quarters, and the stock falls 50%.

That is why I give 5% and not 10%. It is not distrust of the company, it is distrust of its customers.

Q: So what now?

Answer: hold, don't add, don't sell.

Hold, because demand, supply and ecosystem are all intact; selling an asset with a 75% gross margin and 106% growth requires a stronger reason than buying it.

Don't add, because USD 220 corresponds to 15x FY2028 and 19x FY2028 free cash flow; that price has already priced in the good news, plus an extra premium for "believing the guidance."

Don't sell, because what would you swap it for? Something cheaper but a worse business? Cash to wait? My experience is that most people who sell a good company and wait for a pullback end up buying back at a higher level.

So here it is: hold 5%, add to 8% below USD 180, cut to 3% if receivables days exceed 75. Don't forecast, just prepare.

Conclusion

Six questions and six answers, landing on one sentence: this is the best business I can understand, but USD 220 is not a good price. Business 9 out of 10, price 4 out of 10, off-balance-sheet 3 out of 10 (cannot be figured out).Action: hold 5%, cap at 8%, add on dips at USD 180, trim on receivables days. A good company does not need a good story on top of a good price; what it lacks now is not a story but a clear explanation of that USD 108.5 billion.
