## Capital allocation: three data points for A and one new data point

The first piece of evidence is buybacks. In FY2027Q2, buybacks totalled USD 19.732 billion and dividends USD 6.047 billion, returning USD 26 billion combined; for H1, buybacks were USD 39.044 billion and dividends USD 6.290 billion. Management's stated target is to return more than 50% of free cash flow; year-to-date actuals stand at 60%. Remaining buyback authorization is USD 99 billion. Buying back stock on this scale around a share price of USD 220 implies management does not view its own shares as expensive—a claim that can be tested.

The second piece of evidence is M&A restraint. The USD 6.9 billion acquisition of Mellanox in 2019 secured InfiniBand and networking capabilities; today Spectrum-X Ethernet is up 260% YoY and the company calls itself the world's largest networking vendor—money extremely well spent. The USD 40 billion attempt to acquire ARM in 2020–2022 was blocked by regulators and simply abandoned rather than dragged into a quagmire.

The third piece of evidence is the dividend cadence. A quarterly dividend of USD 0.25 per share (USD 1.00 annualized) and a payout ratio below 10% leave the vast majority of cash for reinvestment and buybacks. For a company still expanding at high speed, this is the right choice.

The new evidence is three moves in 2026: USD 24.896 billion in debt issuance (long-term borrowings jumping from USD 7.469 billion to USD 32.366 billion), USD 42.404 billion in equity securities purchases in H1 (cumulative investment of nearly USD 50 billion in frontier AI labs), and credit guarantees of up to USD 108.5 billion for data center projects. Add the Groq asset transaction (about USD 20 billion, with USD 2.944 billion paid in cash this quarter). Taken together, these four moves mark the largest capital allocation shift in the company's history: from buying assets to buying customers.

## Integrity and governance: no scandals, but structural flaws

Start with what is absent: no financial restatements, no SEC accounting investigation, no allegations of founder cash-out-style share sales. Jensen Huang holds 3.58%, accumulated since 1993, not from option exercises. By founder-CEO standards, this is clean.

Now what is present: three structural flaws.

First, no chairman. The company has had no chairman since 2024, with independent directors taking turns presiding over the board. The official rationale is "avoiding concentration of power"; the practical effect is that Jensen Huang, as the sole executive director, has no peer counterweight on the board.

Second, no public succession plan. The company has no COO and no clear number two. CFO Colette Kress is the only near-term successor, but her background is finance, not product. For a company that depends on an architecture roadmap, this is not an ideal backup.

Third, family presence. Huang's children work at the company (in Omniverse and robotics-related teams respectively), rare among large Silicon Valley firms. This is not a violation, but it adds a layer of explanation cost to a "merit-first" hiring culture.

One more external pressure: on June 11, 2026, Huang is due to testify at a Senate hearing on chip export compliance at Senator Warren's invitation; on June 5, the day the news broke, the stock fell more than 6%. The China business is no longer just a commercial issue — it is a political one.

## Incentive structure: is he on the same side as shareholders?

Jensen Huang's total FY2026 compensation was USD 36.34 million, down 27% from USD 49.90 million the prior year, comprising USD 24.80 million in stock awards and USD 6.00 million in cash bonus. Against a USD 5.33 trillion market cap, that is negligible.

The real incentive is ownership: 870.6 million shares × USD 220.78 ≈ USD 192.2 billion, the overwhelming majority of his personal wealth. Nvidia has a single class of stock and no dual-class AB structure, so his voting power aligns with his economic interest—the best feature of Nvidia's governance structure, and stronger than at many tech companies.

It also means his wealth is highly concentrated in one stock, and that stock's valuation depends on guidance he himself provides. When 97% of a person's net worth rides on the company, his risk appetite on decisions such as "guaranteeing on behalf of customers" will differ from that of diversified shareholders. This is not a moral issue; it is an incentive-structure issue.

## Succession: the question that most needs an answer within five years

Jensen Huang was born in 1963 and turns 63 in 2026. On his current energy, another five years is not in doubt. But investors need to ask: if he were gone tomorrow, what happens to the company?

Today, nobody knows. There is no public succession plan, no designated number two, and no board transition mechanism beyond a lead independent director. For a USD 5.33 trillion company, this is the single biggest key-man risk, and it appears in no financial report.

Three observable clues. CFO Colette Kress carries an increasingly heavy load on earnings calls — this quarter she covered guidance, the gross-margin path and off-balance-sheet metrics, touching nearly every key disclosure. Ian Buck, as head of the CUDA stack, is where technical continuity resides. And if the board creates a COO position in the next 12 months, that is the signal that succession has begun.

My approach: price this risk into position sizing, not valuation. The 8% position cap exists precisely because an irreplaceable person sits in that seat.
