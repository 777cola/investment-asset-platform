Core thesis

Jensen Huang is one of the most successful founder-CEOs of the past three decades: he founded the company in 1993 and for thirty-three years has done nothing but accelerated computing, turning a graphics-chip company into an AI infrastructure company with a market cap of USD 5.33 trillion. He holds 870.6 million shares, or 3.58%, and received total compensation of USD 36.34 million in FY2026 — low for a company of this scale.

Capital allocation earns an A: no dividend at first, then a small one; large buybacks (USD 20 billion in a single quarter, USD 99 billion remaining under authorization); and restrained M&A (Mellanox at USD 6.9 billion is the model case; ARM at USD 40 billion was abandoned decisively). But 2026 brought a new move: USD 24.9 billion of debt issuance, USD 42.4 billion of equity purchases in customers, and USD 108.5 billion of guarantees for customers. That is capital allocation, and also risk allocation. Governance earns only a B-: no chairman, no public succession plan, and family members in key roles.

Capital allocation: three pieces of evidence for A and one new piece of evidence

The first piece of evidence is buybacks. In FY2027Q2, buybacks totaled USD 19.732 billion and dividends USD 6.047 billion, returning USD 26 billion combined; for H1, buybacks were USD 39.044 billion and dividends USD 6.290 billion. The company's stated target is to return more than 50% of free cash flow, with year-to-date actuals at 60%. Remaining buyback authorization stands at USD 99 billion. Large-scale buybacks around a share price of USD 220 indicate management believes its own stock is not expensive—a claim that can be tested.

The second piece of evidence is M&A restraint. The USD 6.9 billion acquisition of Mellanox in 2019 secured InfiniBand and networking capabilities; today Spectrum-X Ethernet is up 260% year over year and the company calls itself the world's largest networking vendor—that money was extremely well spent. The USD 40 billion attempt to acquire ARM in 2020–2022 was blocked by regulators and simply abandoned, rather than dragging into a quagmire.

The third piece of evidence is the dividend cadence. A quarterly dividend of USD 0.25 per share (USD 1.00 annualized), a payout ratio below 10%, leaves the vast majority of cash for reinvestment and buybacks. For a company still expanding at high speed, this is the right choice.

The new evidence is three moves in 2026: USD 24.896 billion in debt issuance (long-term borrowings jumping from USD 7.469 billion to USD 32.366 billion), USD 42.404 billion in equity securities purchases in H1 (cumulative investment of nearly USD 50 billion in frontier AI labs), and credit guarantees of up to USD 108.5 billion for data center projects. Add the Groq asset transaction (about USD 20 billion, with USD 2.944 billion paid in cash this quarter). Taken together, these four moves mark the largest capital allocation shift in the company's history: from buying assets to buying customers.

Integrity and governance: no scandals, but structural flaws

First, what is absent: no financial restatements, no SEC accounting investigation, no allegations of founder cash-out-style share sales. Jensen Huang holds 3.58%, accumulated since 1993, not from option exercises. By founder-CEO standards, this is clean.

Now what is present: three structural flaws.

First, no chairman. The company has had no chairman since 2024, with independent directors taking turns presiding over the board. The official rationale is "avoiding concentration of power"; the practical effect is that Jensen Huang, as the sole executive director, has no peer counterweight on the board.

Second, no public succession plan. The company has no COO and no clear number two. CFO Colette Kress is the only person who could take over in the near term, but her background is finance, not product. For a company that depends on an architecture roadmap, this is not an ideal backup.

Third, family presence. Huang's children work at the company (in Omniverse and robotics-related teams respectively), rare among large Silicon Valley firms. This is not a violation, but it adds a layer of explanation cost to a "merit-first" hiring culture.

One more external pressure: on June 11, 2026, Huang is due to testify at a Senate hearing on chip export compliance at Senator Warren's invitation; on June 5, the day the news broke, the stock fell more than 6%. This shows the company's China business is no longer just a commercial issue — it is a political one.

Incentive structure: is he on the same side as shareholders?

Jensen Huang's total FY2026 compensation was USD 36.34 million, down 27% from USD 49.90 million the prior year, comprising USD 24.80 million in stock awards and USD 6.00 million in cash bonus. Against a USD 5.33 trillion market cap, this number is negligible.

The real incentive comes from ownership: 870.6 million shares × USD 220.78 ≈ USD 192.2 billion, the overwhelming majority of his personal wealth. The company has a single class of stock and no dual-class AB structure, so his voting power aligns with his economic interest—this is the best feature of Nvidia's governance structure, stronger than at many tech companies.

But it also means something else: his wealth is highly concentrated in one stock, and that stock's valuation depends on guidance he himself provides. A person with 97% of his net worth riding on the company will have a different risk appetite from diversified shareholders when making decisions like "guaranteeing on behalf of customers." This is not a moral issue; it is an incentive-structure issue.

Succession: the question that most needs an answer within five years

Jensen Huang was born in 1963 and turns 63 in 2026. Given his energy, another five years is not in question. But investors need to know: if he were gone tomorrow, what happens to the company?

The current answer: nobody knows. The company has no public succession plan, no designated number two, and no board transition mechanism beyond a lead independent director. For a USD 5.33 trillion company, this is the single biggest point risk, and it does not appear in any financial report.

Three observable clues: CFO Colette Kress carries an increasingly heavy role on earnings calls (this quarter she covered guidance, the gross-margin path and off-balance-sheet metrics, touching nearly every key disclosure); Ian Buck, as head of the CUDA stack, is where technical continuity resides; and if the board creates a COO position in the next 12 months, that is the signal that succession has begun.

My approach: price this risk into position sizing, not valuation. The 8% position cap exists precisely because an irreplaceable person sits in that seat.

Conclusion

Capital allocation A: generous buybacks, restrained M&A, moderate dividends; but three new moves in 2026 (RMB 24.9 billion in bond issuance, RMB 42.4 billion to buy customer equity, RMB 108.5 billion in guarantees) turn capital allocation into risk allocation. Governance B-: single-class share structure is a strength; no chairman, no succession plan, family appointments are weaknesses. Conclusion: management does not lose enough points to affect holding, but enough to affect the position cap—8%, no higher.
