## Three profit pools in the chain, Meituan captures two

There are only three pools of money in the local services chain. The first is "exposure": merchants pay commissions and advertising to be seen, with gross margins above 80% — the fattest segment. The second is "fulfillment": delivering meals from restaurant to home, charging a 6–9 yuan delivery fee and paying riders 7–8 yuan, for a gross profit of 1–2 yuan per order, making money on scale. The third is "goods": self-operated pharmaceuticals, liquor and fresh produce, with gross margins of 15%–25%.

Meituan holds the first two at once; the third has only just started.That is its structural advantage: high-margin in-store subsidizes low-margin fulfillment, forming a closed loop. Douyin has only the first, so its in-store business must make big money on its own; JD has only the second, so its food delivery must buy scale with subsidies; Meituan has both and can reprice between the two ends.

But "having both ends" also means "defending both ends." In the 2025 battle, Douyin attacked in-store and Alibaba attacked fulfillment, squeezing Meituan's profit from both sides at once. This is the structural reason it was dragged into a RMB 23.36 billion loss — not a management failure.

## The company's position in the chain: thickest in the middle, and most easily squeezed

Upstream are 8 million merchants and several million farmers/brands, over whom Meituan has pricing power—commission rates, ad slots and campaign participation are all set by the platform. But that pricing power is weakening: Q3 merchant services revenue was about RMB 44 billion, up only 8% year on year, indicating merchants are either pushing down commissions or cutting spend. Merchants are not unprofitable; they were scarred by the 2025 subsidy war and are recovering more slowly than users.

Downstream are about 800 million annual transacting users, over whom Meituan has almost zero pricing power—for the same braised chicken dish, a RMB 3 price gap across three platforms is enough to make users leave. That is why the market's first reaction when subsidies stop is "will order volume fall"; Q3's answer is that it did not, and revenue even accelerated—the most important evidence in this earnings report.

In between are 6 million riders. Costs here are rigid: from July 1, 2026, "new occupational injury" insurance covers the whole country with premiums fully borne by the platform, raising per-order cost by RMB 0.1–0.2, or RMB 3–6 billion a year. That money cannot be passed to merchants (regulators would notice) or to users (orders would fall), so the company must absorb it. This is the structural cost of its position in the chain.

## Cycle position: early stage, not the peak

Three signals show this is still early-cycle. First, the marketing expense ratio is 22.9%, 7 points above 2024's 16% — subsidies have not fully exited, so a large part of the elasticity remains. Second, core local commerce operating margin is 5.6% versus 20.9% in 2024, a 15-point gap. Third, new initiatives are still losing RMB 2.16 billion and have yet to break even.

Conversely, none of the peak signals have appeared: no price increases, no cuts to rider subsidies, no halt to overseas expansion, no R&D cuts below 5%. The company is now in "recovery," not "harvest." That is good for investors — if it were already harvesting, the elasticity would be exhausted; the elasticity is still there now, it is just unclear when it will be released.

Note that the cycle in this chain is not set by Meituan but by the capex cadence of the three players. Alibaba's HKD 80 billion allocation in 2026 is going into AI, not food delivery; JD's share has fallen back to single digits; Douyin's in-store growth has slowed from 60% to around 30%. All three rivals decelerating at once is what creates this "early stage." If any one of them re-accelerates, the cycle position would immediately reverse.
