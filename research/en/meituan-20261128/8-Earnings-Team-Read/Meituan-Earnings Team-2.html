Quarterly Report Close Read (Buffett) — Meituan

What is the profit, where is the money, and who manages it

Core view

When I read a quarterly report, I look at only three statements: the income statement to see how much was earned, the cash flow statement to see whether the money is real, and the balance sheet to see whether there is risk. In Meituan's quarterly report, all three statements pass, but none excels.

Income statement: gross profit improving, expenses contracting

Revenue RMB 115.56 billion, gross profit RMB 40.06 billion (34.7%, derived), with gross margin up 1.7 points YoY and 1.2 points QoQ. Gross margin still rose in a quarter of subsidy rollback, showing the product mix is improving — higher-margin in-store and self-operated instant retail are taking a larger share.

Cost side: sales and marketing RMB 26.5 billion (22.9%), R&D RMB 8.65 billion (7.5%), administrative RMB 3.2 billion (2.8%), the three together 33.2%. Gross margin 34.7% minus the three expense lines at 33.2% leaves 1.5% — that is the operating margin. The structure says one thing: this company now spends essentially all of its gross profit on expenses, and the slightest disturbance puts it back into loss.

That is what I dislike. A company's margin of safety should not be 1.5 percentage points. In 2024 the same business had 33% gross margin, a 22% three-expense ratio and a 20.9% operating margin — the missing 11 points all sit in marketing expense.

Cash flow: this is the best part of the quarterly report

Operating cash flow of about RMB 8.5 billion, capex of about RMB 2.5 billion and free cash flow of about RMB 6 billion (derived). Net profit of RMB 2.071 billion against free cash flow of RMB 6 billion — cash flow runs at three times profit because food delivery is a prepaid business. Users pay upfront, riders are settled later and merchants settle monthly, so working capital is negative.

This is what I often call "the hallmark of a good business": growth does not depend on financing, and expansion does not depend on borrowing. Meituan lost RMB 23.355 billion in 2025 and still held RMB 168.3 billion in cash, precisely because of this structure.

One caveat: Q4 is the off-season, and full-year free cash flow will not reach RMB 24 billion — RMB 12–15 billion would be a good outcome. Annualising a single quarter to calculate the FCF yield (6.2%) overstates it; the actual figure is about 3%–4%.

Balance sheet: cleaner than a company that just lost RMB 23.3 billion has any right to be

Cash of RMB 104.7 billion, short-term wealth management products of RMB 63.6 billion, interest-bearing debt at nearly zero and net cash of about RMB 120 billion, or 31% of market cap. Buying in now therefore gets you one-third cash and two-thirds business.

The business portion is valued at RMB 265.5 billion and can earn RMB 23 billion in 2027 on my neutral assumption, or 11.5x. For comparison with companies I have bought: Coca-Cola traded at 15x in 1988, Wells Fargo at 5x and Apple at 13x. At 11.5x, that is reasonably cheap for a company with a moat but no pricing power.

The liability side is clean too: no large interest-bearing debt and no complex off-balance-sheet structures. That is rare among internet companies — many dress up their statements with convertible bonds and buybacks; Meituan does not.

Management: good with numbers, but not generous enough

Wang Xing chose to defend profit and cede share at the worst possible moment, and he was right. A company that can lose money without limit in pursuit of share will never deliver a return to its shareholders. He spent a year proving the call correct: Q3 profit of RMB 2.071 billion with share held at 50%.

On capital allocation, though, I grade him only C+: net cash of RMB 120 billion, a 0.4% buyback, no dividend, and USD 1 billion going to Brazil. In his position, I would lift buybacks to 5% before talking about overseas expansion.

Management integrity is not in question — the 2021 antitrust fine of RMB 3.442 billion was accepted, the structure was changed, no dragging of feet; on 2026 rider insurance, the company took on the cost proactively. These are pluses. But "generous to shareholders" is one claim it has yet to prove.

Conclusion

All three statements pass: a thin income statement, strong cash flow and a clean balance sheet. Management counts every penny but is not generous enough — capital allocation is the only weak spot. At 11.5x ex-net-cash the valuation is reasonably low; buying is acceptable, but hold most of the money until below HKD 58, where the FCF yield exceeds 5%.
