Earnings Deep Dive (Li Lu) — Meituan

Look at the decade, not the quarter

Core view

I do not care much about a single quarter's profit. What I care about is: has this business become more important over a ten-year horizon? The answer lies in three clues in this quarterly report.

Clue one: the category is expanding

New business revenue was RMB 35.54 billion, up 30.8% year on year, the fastest of the three segments. This shows the "use delivery capacity to sell other things" path works — flash shopping, pharmaceuticals, liquor and fresh groceries all add categories onto the same fulfillment network.

The long-term significance of this far outweighs short-term profit. If Meituan can go from "delivering meals" to "delivering everything," its ceiling is no longer the RMB 5 trillion food and beverage market, but the instantly deliverable portion of the entire RMB 30 trillion retail sales pool. This is a ten-year shift.

The caveat: new business still lost RMB 2.16 billion, a 6.1% loss ratio, widening quarter on quarter. Expansion and losses are two sides of the same coin — the question now is how much of the loss is "investment" and how much is "subsidy." The former is investment; the latter is burn.

Clue two: overseas operations prove replicability

Keeta reached unit-economics profitability in Hong Kong in October 2025 and in Saudi Arabia in July 2026, taking only 22 months. This is a rare achievement for a Chinese internet company going overseas — it won not by buying market share with subsidies, but through dispatch efficiency.

The significance: this system is "technology plus operations," not "China-specific policy arbitrage." If it were the latter, it would be useless abroad; if it is the former, it can be replicated in any high-density city.

But Brazil is a different matter. Saudi Arabia has high average order value and low competition; Brazil has low average order value and strong rivals (local platform iFood holds dominance). The USD 1 billion over 5 years is only a committed ceiling. I treat Brazil as an option: success switches the valuation framework, failure is tuition.

Clue 3: Institutional risk has already materialized

The 2021 antitrust fine was RMB 3.442 billion; in July 2026, rider "new occupational injury" insurance achieved nationwide coverage, fully funded by the platform. Both issues are now settled, the costs are quantifiable, and no "could happen again at any time" overhang remains.

This is rare among Chinese internet companies. Many companies trade at a valuation discount not because the business is poor, but because nobody knows what regulators will do next. Meituan is now relatively clear on this front: the antitrust case is closed, rider costs run RMB 3–6 billion a year, and algorithm transparency has limited impact.

What remains is local regulation (such as restrictions on e-bikes in certain cities) and whether the social insurance scope will widen further. The former has localized impact; the latter is industry-wide.

What worries me most is the people, not the business.

Wang Xing is 47, and the company has no clear second-in-command. Mu Rongjun runs technology, Wang Puzhong runs core local commerce, and Chen Shaohui runs capital. Each controls one area, and none is publicly positioned as the "next CEO." On a ten-year horizon, this is the biggest uncertainty.

Compared with Alibaba (partnership system plus job rotation), JD.com (the succession tier after Xu Lei), and Pinduoduo (Chen Lei), Meituan's organization looks more like "founder plus business units" — high efficiency, concentrated succession risk. If no succession tier emerges in 2027–2028, the valuation should take a further 5%–10% discount.

Conclusion: The business is improving (category expansion, overseas replication, institutional risk now settled), while the people are maturing (no answer yet). These two facts mean it can be held long term, but not with a heavy position.

Conclusion

Over a ten-year horizon, this business has become more important: the category is expanding, the model has proven replicable, and regulatory risk has landed. The only uncertainty is people — there is no clear succession plan. Conclusion: hold through cycles at no more than 5% of the portfolio; overseas expansion is an option, and we will not pay full price for it.
