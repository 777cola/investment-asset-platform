## Industry-chain position: controls the demand gateway, the fulfillment network and merchant digitalization at the same time

The previous section noted that this chain has three money bags, and Meituan took two. More precisely: it simultaneously holds the trio of "demand gateway (App DAU), fulfillment network (6 million riders), and merchant digitalization (POS, supply chain, Kuailv)." The value of this trio lies not in any single piece but in the combination — merchants using Meituan's POS system are more willing to run promotions on Meituan; higher rider density means faster delivery; faster delivery makes users willing to buy more categories.

The benefit of this position is pricing power: charging commissions to merchants, delivery fees to users, and advertising fees to brands. The downside is that it must bear the full fulfillment cost itself — any rise in labor costs hits profit directly. Compare Douyin: Douyin does "traffic distribution + in-store redemption," with no fulfillment, no riders, no social insurance — far lighter and far faster. This is why Douyin captured half the in-store market in 3 years, while Alibaba and JD spent two years to reach only 43% share in food delivery.

## Moat by moat: which is the hardest, which is the softest

The strongest is the fulfillment network, 9 points. This is not technology; it is assets: sites in 2,800 counties and cities, 6 million riders with orders, and a scheduling algorithm refined over a decade. JD.com invested tens of billions of yuan in food delivery in 2025, yet its share in 2026 is still only about 7%. The reason is not insufficient money but insufficient density—a site handling 200 orders a day versus 2,000 orders a day differs by more than RMB 2 in per-order cost. The essence of this moat is "time"; money cannot buy time.

The weakest is in-store, 6 points. In-store is a pure traffic business: merchants want exposure, users want discounts, and whoever has more engaging content wins. Douyin has 700 million DAU and algorithmic recommendation, a playbook inherently suited to it. In 2025, Douyin's in-store GTV exceeded RMB 800 billion, with growth of nearly 60%, and Meituan's in-store share was pressed from above 80% to around 60%. In-store is Meituan's fattest profit pool (operating margin around 30%), and also the one it can least defend.

The two in the middle are merchant ecosystem and user mindshare, 7 points each. Eight million merchants are an asset, but merchant services grew only 8% in Q3, showing that bargaining power is eroding; user mindshare persists at high ticket sizes but has been spoiled by subsidies in the low-price band—these users belong to no platform; they belong to price.

## The most dangerous rival: not Alibaba, but Douyin

Most people would say the most dangerous competitor is Taobao Shangou, because it has taken 43% of food delivery order volume. I disagree. In the food delivery war, Alibaba is "trading money for share" — once the money runs out, it has to stop. The HKD 80 billion it allocated in 2026 went to AI infrastructure, not food delivery, and that is the evidence. Food delivery is also a tough business: RMB 1–2 gross profit per order, 6 million riders to manage, social insurance to pay. Alibaba's return on capital in this business is far below its e-commerce business.

Douyin is different. What it is taking is in-store — 80% gross margin, no riders needed, no social insurance, no fulfillment. For the same RMB 10 billion of investment, the profit Douyin can earn in in-store is ten times that of food delivery. Its traffic is also its own, so marginal cost is close to zero. In 2025, in-store GTV exceeded RMB 800 billion, growing nearly 60%. If that pace holds for three years, Meituan's in-store profit will be cut in half.

So the most dangerous competitor is Douyin, because its business model fits the "in-store" segment better than Meituan's does, and in-store is the foundation of Meituan's profit. Losing share in food delivery only means earning less; losing share in in-store damages the foundation.
