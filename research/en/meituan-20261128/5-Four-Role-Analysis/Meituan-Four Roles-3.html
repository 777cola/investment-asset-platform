Industry Research (Munger's Lens) — Meituan

The structure of this business means all three players are destined to lose money together.

Core view

Munger would start with structure, not quarters. Local services is a low-barrier, high-exit-cost industry: anyone with riders can enter, but exiting means dismantling an organisation tens of thousands strong. The product is homogeneous (the same braised chicken) and pricing is transparent. The result is an industry anyone can enter and no one dares leave.

Industry structure: four characteristics determine margins

First, low barriers to entry. JD.com went from zero to 7% share in a single year in 2025 on the back of "Dada plus subsidies", without building a single new network. Low barriers mean excess profit will inevitably be eroded by new entrants.

Second, high exit costs. 6 million riders, 2,800 stations and an organisation of tens of thousands: no player dares say "I'm out", because exiting means writing down all of these assets.

Third, homogeneous product. The same meal from the same restaurant, delivered by three platforms, is the same thing; users choose on price and speed alone. In homogeneous competition, no one can raise prices long term.

Fourth, price transparency. Users need only 30 seconds to compare prices across three apps. This is the most lethal factor — it makes "differentiated pricing" impossible.

Conclusion: this is an industry where "scale determines cost, cost determines survival, but no one can eliminate anyone else." Steady-state margins will not be high; 20.9% in 2024 is an outlier, not the norm.

Inverse view: what would make this business irreparably worse

First, regulation turns rider costs into employee costs. Riders are currently classified as "crowdsourced/flexible labour." Reclassifying them as employees under labour law, with full social insurance and housing fund contributions, would lift per-order cost from RMB 0.1–0.2 of insurance to over RMB 2. If that happens, unit economics across the entire industry turn negative, Meituan included. The July 2026 "new occupational injury" insurance is a compromise, and it shows regulators are running the same math.

Second, technology erases the density advantage. If drones and autonomous vehicles can push 3-kilometer delivery costs below RMB 1, the 6 million riders shift from asset to liability. Meituan is investing in autonomous delivery itself, but how much faster than rivals is anyone's guess.

Third, traffic entry points are migrating. If users stop opening the app and instead order through WeChat, Douyin or AI assistants, Meituan becomes a back-end fulfilment provider and its valuation framework shifts from platform to utility. 30% of its traffic now comes from external entry points, and it cannot defend that share.

Competitive landscape: a stable three-way split, not a fantasy of one dominant player

Meituan held 65% in early 2025, 51% in February 2026 and about 50% now; Taobao Shangou about 43%, JD about 7%. The structure has held for three quarters—not because it makes sense, but because none of the three can fight any further.

Munger would make one point: stable share does not mean stable profit. A 50/43/7 split shows that no one can eat anyone else, not that everyone has stopped fighting. The moment one player reaches for another 2 points, subsidies return. The 2026Q3 profit recovery is the product of a temporary ceasefire, not a peace treaty.

He would also flag Douyin: Douyin is not in food delivery, it is in in-store. In-store is an 80% gross-margin business, and Douyin has taken 40% share on content traffic—precisely Meituan's profit foundation. The food-delivery war is fought over scale; the in-store war is fought over profit—and the latter is more lethal.

How would he judge it?

Munger would not run a DCF. He would ask: "If this business earns an 8% margin ten years from now, what is it worth today?" The answer: RMB 360 billion revenue × 8% = RMB 28.8 billion operating profit; after tax and minorities, about RMB 22 billion net profit; at 15x = RMB 330 billion; plus RMB 120 billion net cash = RMB 450 billion, or HKD 489.1 billion, or HKD 79 per share. At HKD 67.85, the stock sits 14% below even this "mediocre assumption."

Hence his conclusion: "It doesn't need to get better. As long as it doesn't get worse, this price doesn't lose money." He would add one caveat: "If it does get worse (rider costs, technological substitution), even HKD 79 is expensive."

So his move is: small position, long hold, no quarterly forecasts. That and Duan Yongping's "scaling in tranches" are two ways of saying the same thing.

Conclusion

Industry view: industry structure caps steady-state margins, and 20.9% in 2024 is an outlier. Three developments could permanently break the business: rider costs becoming employee costs, technology erasing density advantages, and a shift in the traffic gateway. None has happened yet, but all are on the way. On a mediocre assumption of an 8% margin in ten years, the stock is worth HKD 79; at the current HKD 67.85, it trades 14% below even that mediocre assumption.
