Risk Assessment (Li Lu's perspective) — Meituan

Will this business still exist in ten years, and is it worth holding across cycles?

Core view

Li Lu frames it as "civilizational scale plus institutional conditions": long-run endurance hinges on how deeply a business is embedded in society. Thirty-minute delivery within three kilometers has already become part of daily life in Chinese cities—this is not about any one company; it is a form of infrastructure.

Structural advantage: urban density dividend

On-demand delivery rests on three things: high-density cities, low-cost labour and mature mobile payments. China has all three at once, and no other country has them as completely. The US is vast and sparsely populated with expensive labour; European cities are dense enough but face high payment and labour costs; Southeast Asia is dense enough but has low purchasing power. So this is not "Chinese companies got lucky" — it is "China's urban form supports this business model."

How long that advantage lasts depends on two things: whether urbanisation continues (it does), and how fast labour costs rise (fast). Rider monthly income rose from RMB 5,000 in 2018 to above RMB 7,000 in 2026, and this trend will not reverse. The model will not disappear, but unit costs will keep rising — a line item that must be deducted over the long term.

The demand side matters more: once China's per capita GDP crossed USD 12,000, the share of services consumption has kept rising. Food delivery, in-store services, travel and hospitality, instant retail — all sit on the "services consumption" line. This is a ten-year tailwind, not a three-year fad.

Endgame: One Dominant Player or Three-Way Split

What Li Lu cares about most is the endgame.If it is "one dominant player taking excess profit," Meituan at its 2024 margin would earn over RMB 50 billion a year and be worth HKD 200; if it is "three players splitting the market at average profit," it is worth only HKD 80.That is a 2.5x gap.

The current position sits between the two.A 50% share means it is not being carved up, but it is not dominant either.The Q3 evidence — share stopped falling and profit turned positive — marks the first small step toward "dominance."But it is only one step and cannot be extrapolated.

Three indicators determine the endgame:first, whether share can return above 55%; second, whether the in-store share can hold at 60%; third, whether the industry marketing expense ratio can stabilize below 18%.If all three hold, the endgame is dominance; if two fail, it is a split market.

Going global: the only thing that could change the endgame

Keeta reached unit-economics profitability in Hong Kong in October 2025 and in Saudi Arabia in July 2026 — just 22 months — and Saudi Arabia already accounts for about 25% of Keeta's daily orders.This is the first time Meituan has proven that "this dispatch system can be replicated across markets."

Li Lu, though, would distinguish two types of market: Saudi Arabia is "high ticket size + low competition," Brazil is "low ticket size + strong rivals." Profitability in Saudi Arabia within 22 months does not mean Brazil can follow. Brazil carries a signed 5-year USD 1 billion investment agreement — a committed ceiling; the actual amount could be higher.

He would put a probability on it: no more than 40% that the Saudi model succeeds in Brazil. If it does, Meituan transforms from "a Chinese local-services company" into "a global instant-delivery company" and the valuation framework changes entirely (25x or more becomes justifiable); if it fails, the USD 1 billion is tuition, with no impact on the core business.

Overseas expansion is therefore an option, not the main line. Treat it as the main line and you overvalue Meituan; ignore it entirely and you undervalue its long-term upside.

Institutional risk and the one thing that worries him most

Institutional risk breaks into three layers: antitrust (RMB 3.442 billion fined in 2021, now settled), rider rights (new occupational injury insurance in 2026, cost is quantifiable), and algorithms and pricing (regulators demand transparency, limited impact). All three have either landed or are quantifiable; none is a "black swan"-level unresolved item. That is relatively rare among Chinese internet companies.

His biggest worry is management generational transition: Wang Xing is 47 and there is no clear number two. Mu Rongjun runs technology, Wang Puzhong the core business, Chen Shaohui capital — three people, one piece each. On a ten-year horizon this is the biggest uncertainty — not business risk, but people.

How would he bet? "With a position I can bear, buy a business that will most likely still be here in ten years, but whose earnings I cannot know." It is the same move as Duan Yongping's "tranches" and Munger's "small position, long hold."

Conclusion

Risk view: the structural advantage holds (urban density dividend); the endgame is undecided (one dominant player vs. split share, a 2.5x gap); overseas expansion is an option, not the main story; regulatory risk has already landed. The one variable that defies quantification is the management generational transition. Conclusion: this is a business that can be held across cycles, but only in an "affordable position" — that is, no more than 5% of the portfolio.
