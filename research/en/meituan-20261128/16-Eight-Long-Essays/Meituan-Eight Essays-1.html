① Cognitive reset: three misconceptions about Meituan — Meituan

First dismantle what is wrong, then discuss what is right

Core view

Three deeply held market beliefs about Meituan were each once right and are now outdated: "food delivery is the best business in the world," "the subsidy war will run until one side is knocked out," and "losses mean the business has deteriorated."

Misconception 1: Food delivery is the best business

That judgment held in 2021, when Meituan's core local commerce operating margin exceeded 18%, per-order food delivery profit topped RMB 1, and the market awarded it a 40x P/E on the thesis of "high frequency, rigid demand, two-sided network, winner takes all." Four years on, three things have overturned that judgment.

First, high frequency does not equal high profit. Food delivery runs over 20 billion orders a year on revenue of RMB 300 billion and posted an operating loss of RMB 17 billion in 2025. High frequency delivers traffic, not profit — profit depends on commission rates and advertising, and competition has capped both.

Second, a two-sided network does not equal winner takes all. Meituan held 65% in early 2025, 51% a year later and about 50% now. If it could truly take all, share would not have fallen 15 points. The reason is zero switching cost for users — three apps installed, whichever is cheaper wins.

Third, scale effects have geographic boundaries. Of 2,800 counties and districts, the top 300 generate most orders; the remaining 2,500 are "covered but unprofitable." Scale effects hold within a single city, not nationwide.

The correct view: food delivery is "a good business, but not a great one" — it has a real moat (density) but no pricing power; it earns steady profit but cannot earn excess profit. On that view, fair valuation is 15–20x, not 40x.

Misconception 2: The subsidy war runs until one side is knocked out

Through the 2025 battle, the market kept asking who would fold first. The consensus then: JD had the least cash, Alibaba had an e-commerce core to defend, Meituan had profit to protect—so one of the three would fight until another exited. In the end, none exited; the fight settled into a 50/43/7 stalemate.

Why did no outcome emerge? Because the input-output ratio of this battle was too poor. Gross profit per food-delivery order was RMB 1–2, capturing 1 percentage point of share cost over RMB 10 billion, and the payback period exceeded five years. After running the numbers, all three boards chose to "stop but not exit."

That is the new landscape of 2026: not peace, but a ceasefire. The marker of the ceasefire is the marketing expense ratio falling from 40% to 22.9%, not share returning to 65%. A ceasefire can be long (three to five years) or short (one quarter)—depending on whether anyone wants to chase data.

The correct read: this industry will long remain split three ways, and profitability depends on how long the "ceasefire" holds, not on "who wins." Investing in Meituan is a bet on the ceasefire lasting, not a bet on it taking all.

Misconception 3: Losses stem from a deteriorating business

A net loss of RMB 23.355 billion in 2025 has many calling time on Meituan. But look at gross margin: about 33% in 2025 versus 33.5% in 2024 — barely changed. Had the business been deteriorating, gross margin would have collapsed first. It didn't.

So where did the loss come from? Expenses. The marketing expense ratio jumped to 40% in 2025 from 16% in 2024, and the incremental spend was entirely subsidies. This is proactive spending, not passive losses — and proactive spending can be switched off, whereas passive losses cannot.

2026Q3 proved the point: the marketing expense ratio fell to 22.9%, gross profit held at RMB 40.06 billion, and operating profit swung from -RMB 17.8 billion to +RMB 1.71 billion. Same business, same users — just less cash burned, and profit came back.

The right lens: this is a company damaged by a price war, not obsoleted by the times. That distinction drives the valuation — a damaged company can return to its former state once repaired; an obsoleted one cannot.

After the reset, what problems remain?

Strip away three misconceptions and only three real questions remain: First, how long can the ceasefire hold? Second, how far can margins recover? Third, at this price, are the odds good enough?

The first has no answer, only tracking metrics (share, marketing expense ratio, competitor capex). The second has a range: 5.6% now, 10% as the neutral assumption, 20.9% the 2024 outlier. The third is calculable: HKD 67.85, 11.5x after net cash, 23% downside, 44% upside, odds of 1:1.9.

The odds are unremarkable, but the win rate is rising. That is the entire basis for "buy, in tranches" — not because we expect it to become a great company, but because at this price the cost of being wrong is smaller than the cost of being right.

Conclusion

Three perception resets: food delivery is a good business, not a great one; the subsidy war has reached a stalemate rather than produced a winner; and the losses reflect deliberate spending, not a deteriorating business. After the reset, only three questions remain: how long the ceasefire lasts, how high the margin goes, and whether the odds are sufficient. The answers: unknown, 10%, and 1:1.9 — enough to support a Buy, but not a heavy position.
