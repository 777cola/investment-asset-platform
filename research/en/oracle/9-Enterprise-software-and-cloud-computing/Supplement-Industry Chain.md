## Where in the value chain is the money

In this chain, profit pools concentrate at two ends: upstream, where supply is tightest, and the software layer, where lock-in is strongest. Nvidia's gross margin exceeds 70% because it supplies the "only choice"; Oracle's database gross margin is 90% because customers cannot switch away. Both ends are "no alternative" businesses.

The middle layer is the hardest. Server manufacturing gross margins sit in the single digits and pure compute leasing at 25%–40% — both are "one of several options" businesses where customers can switch suppliers at any time and price is set by marginal cost. Oracle's problem is not a lack of good positions, but that all of its incremental revenue sits in the difficult one. OCI contributed nearly all of this quarter's growth, and nearly all of its capex.

## Oracle's true position in the chain

It plays three roles at once. Upstream, it is a major NVIDIA customer: large purchase volumes, but no pricing power, so it can only absorb GPU price increases. Downstream, it is a supplier to AI labs, providing racks, power and networking at contracted prices — though customers can build their own. And for itself, it is a project financier, advancing capital through debt and equity while waiting for customers to pay monthly.

The combination means it absorbs both upstream cost volatility and downstream pricing pressure, earning only the engineering spread in between. When the industry is tight, that spread is fat — this quarter's OCI growth of 121% is evidence. When the industry is oversupplied, the spread gets squeezed from both ends. A true "sublandlord" at least has lease protection; its contracts carry termination clauses.

## Cycle position: construction peak; harvest period after 2028

2026 is the investment peak of this AI infrastructure cycle: combined capex at the four major cloud providers is on the order of USD 700 billion, with Oracle alone at USD 90–95 billion. Given an 18–24 month build cycle, concentrated commissioning runs from the second half of 2027 to the first half of 2028.

The cycle can be positioned as follows: demand is at the tail end of the acceleration phase, supply is on the eve of commissioning. The money is made when these two points diverge; the danger is when they converge. Oracle is now staking USD 90 billion on the assumption that supply stays tight even when they converge—yet every capital-intensive industry in history has seen oversupply at this stage. Judging the cycle position matters more than judging the company—because at this position, even good companies get dragged down by oversupply.

## Two easily overlooked links: power and grid connection

The scarcest resource in AI data centers is not GPUs but power. A single GB300 rack draws roughly 130–140 kilowatts, and a 1GW campus consumes as much electricity as a mid-sized city. Grid interconnection queues at major US utilities run 3–5 years, and transformer lead times exceed 2 years. Oracle built its own fuel cells in Abilene precisely to bypass this queue—not an environmental choice but time arbitrage.

The detail reveals two things. First, Oracle's engineering capability is genuinely strong, and it is willing to pay a premium for speed. Second, its cost structure carries a "speed premium" that turns into a disadvantage once power prices normalize. Running a data center on gas-fired generation typically costs 30%–50% more per kWh than grid power. In a tight market that money is well spent; in a glut, it becomes a burden.
