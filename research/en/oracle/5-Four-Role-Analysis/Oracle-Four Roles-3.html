Industry Research (Munger's Lens) — Oracle

In the compute business, who can actually make money structurally
Research date 2026/09/14  ·  Price $144.79 (-3.65%)  ·  Market cap About USD 440 billion  ·  PE(GAAP TTM) Approximately 23.5x

Core view

In the AI compute supply chain, only three positions make money: chips (NVIDIA), power (the few regions with grid-connection quotas), and cloud platforms with pricing power. Pure compute leasing is the least profitable position — it is a standardized product, scale is built with capital, and price is set by marginal supply. Oracle is staking 38% of its revenue on exactly this position.

Structure: who sits upstream in the food chain

NVIDIA takes the largest slice of profit in this chain because it supplies the "only option." Power companies take the second slice because grid-connection queues run three to five years, making land and electricity scarce resources. Cloud platforms take the third slice, not on the strength of compute itself but on bundling—enterprises buy Azure because Office, Entra and SQL Server all come together, not because its GPUs are cheap.

In this structure, pure compute leasing is priced by NVIDIA upstream, squeezed by model companies downstream, and must also compete with power companies for grid-connection quotas in between. Its margin is not set by itself but by when the previous GPU generation becomes obsolete. This is a classic triple-disadvantage structure: capital-intensive, homogeneous product, concentrated customers.

Demand: a genuine super-cycle, but supply is catching up

Capex across North America's four largest cloud providers is now guided up to the USD 700 billion range for 2026, with both AI training and inference demand surging; that trend is not in doubt. Oracle's USD 664 billion RPO shows it secured a position in the tightest window.

But the supply-side numbers are just as striking: industry-wide new data center capacity in 2026 is more than triple 2023 levels, and Nvidia has raised capacity for six consecutive quarters.Munger would ask a simple question: if everyone finishes building their data centers at the same time in 2027, which way do rents go?History gives the same answer for every capital-intensive industry—shortage first, then glut, then a price war. Fiber optics in 2000 and shale oil in 2015 both ran that course.

Competition: the real rival is not CoreWeave

The market likes to lump Oracle and CoreWeave together because both "borrow to buy chips." But they are the same kind of victim, not each other's rival. The real threat comes from three cloud vendors with positive free cash flow: they can use in-house chips (Trainium, Maia, TPU) to push cost per token down to 60% of Nvidia-based solutions, then subsidize the compute business with overall cloud gross margin.

The second threat comes from the customers themselves. OpenAI is already building its own data centers, Meta is building its own, xAI is building its own. When the biggest buyer becomes a builder, the sublessor's business is left with only a "fill the trough" function—filling in when the customer's own capacity falls short, with zero bargaining power.

Reverse checklist: what would make this industry crack first

① Model efficiency improves faster than expected, cutting the compute needed for the same training task by more than 30%; ② inference demand shifts to the edge, eroding utilization at centralized large clusters; ③ the power bottleneck eases and new entrants flood in; ④ a large AI lab fails to raise funding, triggering a chain reaction of contract renegotiations.

Not all four need to happen; one alone is enough to knock 15% off rents. And the industry's defining trait: when rents fall 15%, a company with 4x leverage sees net profit fall 60%. The operator structure makes profit extremely sensitive to price, which is exactly the kind of business Munger disliked most.

Conclusion

Conclusion: the industry is real, the position is poor. AI compute is the largest capex cycle of this generation, but the profit in the value chain does not sit with "the people building the data centers." Oracle proved with RMB 664 billion in orders that it can win the work, but what it won is contract work, not rent collection. In his framework, the fair valuation for this kind of business is around 1x replacement cost, not a growth-stock multiple.
