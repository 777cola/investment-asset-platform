Risk Assessment (Li Lu's perspective) — Oracle

Five variables that could wipe out this investment

Core view

The risk here is structural, not operational. Of the five risks, four hinge on switches Oracle does not control. This is a question of position rather than management capability: it leaves the company having to keep raising financing to deliver on orders.

Risk 1: A single customer accounts for half the order book

Of the USD 664 billion RPO, the market estimates more than half is tied to OpenAI (analyst estimate; the company has not disclosed it). Oracle's order quality is therefore only as good as OpenAI's credit. OpenAI is a private company, still lossmaking and dependent on continuous financing: its ability to pay hinges on the next funding round, not on revenue.

Li Lu would call this "underwriting a startup's credit with an investment-grade balance sheet." The structure has recurred throughout financial history, most notably in the 2000s, when telecom equipment vendors extended vendor financing to emerging carriers—the equipment was sold, but the money never came back.

Risk 2: Financing cost is the lifeline of this business

RMB 129.5 billion of debt, 4.3x leverage, BBB- rating: that combination means the company must refinance tens of billions every year. Every 100bp rise in rates adds RMB 1.3 billion of annual interest, equal to 27% of this quarter's net profit. A one-notch downgrade to BB+ would push it into high-yield territory, where coupons would likely reach 7.5%–8.5% and annual interest would jump from RMB 6.5 billion to over RMB 10 billion.

Project returns then turn negative: data centre asset yields are roughly 10%–12% against an 8% cost of capital, so after depreciation and operating costs shareholders barely make a profit. In other words, a one-notch downgrade turns this business from "thin margin" to "working for nothing."

Risk 3: Depreciation assumptions are the model's hidden trapdoor

The company depreciates GPUs and related equipment over six years. If the true economic life is four years — NVIDIA cycles every 18–24 months, and while inference cards stay usable longer, rental rates decline — annual depreciation would need to be 50% higher. On FY27 capex of USD 90 billion, that depreciation gap is USD 7.5 billion a year, exceeding this quarter's net profit.

Two figures disclosed this quarter are worth remembering: a 20% renewal premium on older cards and overall utilization of 97.9%. These two figures now support the depreciation assumption, and will later be the evidence that overturns it — once utilization falls to 85%, the premium disappears and rental rates and depreciation deteriorate together.

Risk 4: Overcapacity will arrive sooner than expected

New capacity added across the industry in 2026 is triple that of 2023. Assuming an 18–24 month construction cycle, concentrated commissioning runs from the second half of 2027 to the first half of 2028. If AI demand growth does not accelerate before then, the result will be data centers built and not enough customers.

For a company at 4.3x leverage with negative free cash flow, that timing is fatal—it has no reserves to survive a downturn. The three major cloud providers do; CoreWeave and Oracle do not. In Li Lu's framework, "can it survive the worst year" matters ten times more than "how much can it earn in the best year."

Risk 5: Historical analogy — fiber optics and railways

Between 1998 and 2001, more than 80 million kilometers of fiber optic cable were laid worldwide. Demand did explode, but supply exploded faster; Level 3 and Global Crossing ultimately went bankrupt, and Corning's stock fell from USD 113 to USD 1.1. Demand growing 100-fold does not mean the builders make money.

Railways tell the same story: from 1870 to 1890, the United States laid a quarter of the Earth's circumference in track and freight volume grew tenfold, yet half of the railroad companies went bankrupt in the 1893 crisis. The infrastructure pattern: society benefits, creditors lose, equity goes to zero, equipment vendors get rich. Oracle now stands in the position of "builder + borrower."

Conclusion

Conclusion: the risks are uncontrollable and highly correlated. These five risks are not independent events—a single variable drives them all: where we sit in the AI capex cycle. When the cycle peaks, customers cut orders, rents fall, ratings are downgraded and utilization declines all at once. When one factor decides survival, the only discipline is not to build the position at all—or to participate only with size you can afford to lose entirely.
