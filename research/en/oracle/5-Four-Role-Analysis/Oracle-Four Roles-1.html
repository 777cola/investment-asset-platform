Business Analysis (Duan Yongping's Perspective) — Oracle

What really drives this business's profits, and will that still hold in ten years?

Core view

Oracle now runs two businesses side by side: one sells "indispensability," the other sells "compute." The first carries a 90% gross margin with pricing power in Oracle's hands; the second carries a gross margin of around 30% with pricing power sitting with the customer. In fiscal 2026, the second overtook the first for the first time. That is where the whole problem begins.

Why customers buy—that decides everything

Customers who buy the database do not stay with Oracle because they think it is good; they cannot afford to switch. A major bank runs its core ledger on Exadata, and migrating would mean rewriting, downtime, redoing audits—a timeline measured in years and risk measured in hundreds of millions. That "cannot afford to switch" is the most valuable asset in the business: it lets Oracle raise prices for years on end without losing customers.

Customers who buy OCI are comparison shopping. For the same 1,000 GB300s, whoever offers cheaper power, brings capacity online faster and has the better network oversubscription ratio wins the order. The customer list reads OpenAI, xAI, Meta—companies with the strongest bargaining power and the ability to build in-house. The word "loyalty" does not appear in their procurement decisions.

The unit economics of this business are deteriorating.

Put the two businesses side by side and something awkward emerges: the fastest-growing segment is the one with the lowest return on capital. Each additional USD 1 of database revenue requires almost no new assets; each additional USD 1 of OCI revenue requires USD 1.47 of assets built first, then five to six years to depreciate. As the mix shifts from 29% recurring rental toward 60% capital-intensive business, group ROIC must decline.

Management will argue that unit costs fall as scale comes on. That holds for power and networking, not for GPUs: GPUs turn over every 18–24 months and rental rates on the prior generation fall, and no scale effect rescues that. A business whose technological progress makes its own assets depreciate faster is not a good business.

What will it rely on to make money in ten years?

Ten years out, the business will most likely still rest on three things: database maintenance fees, Fusion/NetSuite subscription fees, and rent from a batch of completed data centers. The first two are stable, but they account for only half of today's revenue and are growing at 0%–10%. The third rests on two premises: AI compute demand still exceeds supply in 2030, and Oracle's data centers rank in the top three on cost.

My view: in ten years it will still be alive and doing reasonably well, but it will have become an "IDC with a software business" rather than a "software company with a cloud business." The valuation gap between the two is more than double. Anyone buying today is paying a software company's price for a transformation story whose direction points precisely toward the lower-valued side.

Conclusion

Conclusion: this is not a business he would buy now. Not because he cannot understand it, but because once he does, he finds it is turning from "collecting rent" into "contracting". Contracting can also make money, but it depends on scale and cost of capital, and it is not the strongest in either. He will wait for one of two things: proof that OCI's gross margin and return on capital can reach above 20%, or the price falling to reflect only the value of the existing software business.
