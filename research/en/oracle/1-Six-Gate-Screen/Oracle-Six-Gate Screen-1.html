Six-Gate Screen — Oracle

Oracle (ORCL) | 01 · Six-Gate Screen
Research date 2026/09/14  ·  Price $144.79 (-3.65%)  ·  Market cap About USD 440 billion  ·  PE(GAAP TTM) Approximately 23.5x
← Back to the hub

Core view

The ten-minute read: demand is real, the business has deteriorated, and equity has been pledged away. FY27Q1 (June–August 2026) revenue was USD 19.345 billion, up 30% YoY; OCI was USD 7.388 billion, up 121%; RPO was USD 664 billion, up 46%. On any cloud company, those three lines alone would justify a round of chasing. Yet in the same set of results, capex was USD 28.499 billion (1.47x revenue), free cash flow was -USD 5.396 billion, total debt was USD 129.5 billion, the S&P rating is BBB-, and the company completed a USD 20 billion equity offering during the quarter. Three of six gates passed, composite 28/60. Buying at USD 144.79 is not buying cheap; it is buying an expansion plan that requires continuous refinancing to execute.

Gate one: you can read the software, but not the project financing

Score: 7. The three points off reflect a shift in the centre of gravity of this business. Twenty years ago Oracle was three sentences: sell database licences, collect annual maintenance fees, and any customer wanting to switch would have to spend years rewriting systems. With gross margins above 80% and capex near zero, this was a business that could expand with paper and discs.

Today the structure is cloud services at RMB 11.61 billion (60%), of which OCI accounts for RMB 7.39 billion — renting racks, renting GPUs, renting power — while software licences and support have fallen to just RMB 5.55 billion, -3% YoY.Analysing this through a software-company framework systematically understates capital intensity and depreciation risk. OCI's unit economics come down to how much is invested per megawatt, payback in how many years, what power price, and GPU depreciation over how many years — this has to be viewed through the lens of data-centre REITs and project finance; it is a different craft from looking at SaaS. I can model both, but mixing them into one income statement for valuation makes it very easy to get wrong.

Stage two: every USD 1 of earnings requires USD 1.47 of investment

This is the core disqualifier. Revenue was RMB 19.345 billion in the quarter, +30% YoY, against capex of RMB 28.499 billion, +235% YoY. Capex is growing 8x faster than revenue: this growth is not squeezed out, it is bought. FY26 free cash flow is already negative RMB 23.7 billion. FY27 capex guidance is RMB 90–95 billion, with a net cash capex ceiling of RMB 70 billion, while full-year operating cash flow at the current pace is only just over RMB 90 billion — leaving a gap in between.

A rough but useful test of a good business: does growth require shareholders to keep putting in money? Coca-Cola expands capacity through bottlers, Microsoft expands cloud through operating cash flow, and Oracle expands OCI through bond issuance, equity offerings and customer prepayments. None of these three kinds of money belongs to existing shareholders. The larger the scale, the larger the gap. This is not a curve where "it will be fine once scale arrives" — it is a J-curve that worsens before it improves, and no one can guarantee which year the inflection point falls in.

Stage three: the moat protects the legacy business, not the new one

Score 6, because the two businesses have to be assessed separately. The database side is real: the legacy systems of major global banks, telcos and governments run on Oracle, and migrating them takes years. Database-related revenue grew +353% YoY this quarter, and even AWS and Azure have been forced to connect to Oracle Database—a competitor's cloud having to connect to your database is the hardest evidence of a moat.

OCI is not. Customers compare price per GPU hour, speed to power and network convergence ratio; the supplier is Nvidia, and the rivals are three cloud players with positive free cash flow. Oracle's edge is "delivering a few months earlier than others"—a time window, not a structural barrier. That moat is enough to defend RMB 5.5 billion in software license revenue, but not enough to support delivery of RMB 664 billion in orders—and whether those orders can be delivered depends on whether Oracle can keep borrowing to build out data centers.

Stage 4: Execution A, capital allocation C+

Execution is not in doubt. The Abilene campus delivered 131,000 GPUs in a single quarter and completed acceptance in 24 hours—no other company worldwide can match that speed. New AI contracts signed this quarter exceeded USD 30 billion, confirming its standing among the most demanding customers. Management completed its transition in September 2025, with Clay Magouyrk and Mike Sicilia as co-CEOs and Safra Catz moving to vice chairman; there was no strategic wavering during the handover.

The deduction is for capital allocation. In the same quarter the company completed a USD 20 billion ATM equity offering, piled debt up to USD 129.5 billion and partly staked funding for coming quarters on customer prepayments. This is not "seizing the opportunity"—it is opening every financing channel to maximum at once. The old playbook of buying revenue with cash flow—NetSuite in 2016, Cerner in 2022—has now reversed: buying revenue with the balance sheet. A score of 5, with all deductions here.

The fifth hurdle: 17.9x PE is an accounting construct

USD 144.79 equates to 17.9x FY27 non-GAAP EPS (guidance USD 8.10) and 23.5x GAAP P/E (TTM). The gap reflects stock-based compensation, restructuring charges and, most critically, the fact that the non-GAAP measure excludes depreciation on USD 90 billion of capex. Depreciation is already running at roughly USD 1.9 billion per quarter (our rough calculation, based on FY26 depreciation of USD 7.6 billion plus annualised new assets), and full-year FY27 depreciation will most likely reach USD 11–13 billion.

On a second approach: market cap of USD 440 billion plus net debt of about USD 100 billion gives an enterprise value of USD 540 billion; against FY27 EBITDA of roughly USD 40 billion, that is about 13.5x EV/EBITDA. A company with negative free cash flow and a BBB- rating trading at 13.5x EV/EBITDA means the AI story is fully priced in ahead of time. There is no margin of safety at this price, and there is no ambiguity on this point.

Stage six: three preconditions, none of them within its control

The bull case rests on three premises: ① OpenAI and a handful of AI labs pay in line with contractual obligations; ② financing costs do not rise over the next two years and BBB- issuance remains open; ③ GPUs do not depreciate quickly, so the depreciation assumptions hold and rents do not collapse. If any one of the three breaks, the valuation switches from "AI growth stock" back to "heavy-asset operator."

Oracle controls none of the three. The first depends on customer financing, the second on rates and ratings, the third on Nvidia's iteration cadence. The discipline test is not "how high is the probability" but "would being wrong cause structural damage"—with negative free cash flow and 4.3x leverage, one mistake halves the equity. Score: 3.

Veto items and items to be verified

Deal-breaker: cash flow and financing structure. FY26 free cash flow was -RMB 23.7 billion, with FY27Q1 alone at -RMB 5.396 billion. Total debt stands at about RMB 129.5 billion, or 4.3x debt/EBITDA, and S&P cut the rating to BBB- in July 2026 — one notch above junk. The company is carrying the construction risk of hyperscale data centers on an investment-grade-edge balance sheet.

Items to verify (in order of importance):

  • RPO conversion — management says about 12% of RMB 664 billion (about RMB 76.6 billion) converts to revenue within 12 months; actual revenue recognition in Q2 and Q3 is the only validation;
  • Whether the capex/revenue ratio can fall from 1.47 to below 0.6 — the only hard indicator that expansion has entered the harvest phase;
  • Customer concentration — the market estimates more than half of RPO is tied to OpenAI (analyst estimate, not company disclosure); a cut in orders from a single customer would collapse the order story;
  • GPU depreciation life assumptions, and whether the 20% renewal premium on older cards and 97.9% utilization can hold;
  • The actual bond issuance rate under the BBB- rating — a direct deduction from project returns.

Conclusion

Conclusion: avoid; no buy price, only observation triggers. Three of six gates passed, 28/60. Demand is not in doubt—RMB 664 billion in orders, OCI up 121%, 131,000 GPUs delivered in a single quarter; these are real capabilities. The problem is structural: every additional dollar of growth now requires USD 1.47 of upfront spend, funded by creditors and new shareholders.Reassessment hinges on two paths only: a clear path to positive free cash flow, or the stock returning to around USD 100 (below 10x EV/EBITDA). Until then, USD 144.79 is not a low—it is just a price.
