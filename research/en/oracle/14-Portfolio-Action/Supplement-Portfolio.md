## Why no buy price?

Many will ask: how far does it have to fall before I can buy? My answer is **"price is not the main variable, cash flow is."** The company's equity value is extremely sensitive to two variables: financing cost and GPU depreciation. A 100 bp rise in financing cost cuts equity value by 15%; shortening the depreciation life from six years to five cuts it by 20%. **Neither variable can be compensated for by price.**

So I give a range rather than a point: below USD 100, you can hold a 1%–2% observation position, because EV/EBITDA there is about 9.5x, close to the floor for asset-heavy operators; but if free cash flow is still negative at that point, it can only be a small position, not a heavy one. **Cheap is not a reason to buy; cheap and able to support itself is.**

## Specific price levels for adding to, trimming and stopping out positions

Add: **open a tracking position below USD 100**, add once for every 15% drop, total position no more than 2%. Trim: **halve the position on a rebound above USD 180** (about 15x EV/EBITDA, already pricing in the neutral scenario). Stop-loss: **exit unconditionally on a break below USD 85** — that level implies about 7.5x EV/EBITDA, the market is pricing a distressed valuation for "heavy assets + high leverage", which means something is happening that we do not know about.

Note these levels are only valid on the premise that cash flow has still not turned positive. If free cash flow turns positive in the interim, the entire set of levels must be recalculated — **because the valuation framework shifts from EV/EBITDA back to PE.**

## Correlation with existing holdings

Put Oracle into a typical tech portfolio: correlation with Nvidia is about 0.75 (same AI capex factor), about 0.7 with Broadcom (custom ASIC and networking), about 0.65 with Micron (HBM cycle), and about 0.85 with CoreWeave (same business model, same customer base). **Correlation with Microsoft is lower (about 0.5), because Microsoft's cash flow is stable and represents defensive AI exposure.**

Conclusion: **Buying Oracle diversifies no risk; it only adds another layer of leverage to AI capex exposure.** If the portfolio already holds Nvidia + Micron + CoreWeave, adding Oracle means placing the same bet four times. **In that case, the right move is not to add position, but to check whether total exposure exceeds the 25% risk budget.**

## Things to do even without building a position

Three things: **1) Put the December Q2 earnings release on the calendar**, and watch only three numbers (RPO, capex, FCF); **2) Track BBB- credit spreads**, a leading indicator of the financing environment that reacts a quarter earlier than the share price; **3) If you hold AI compute exposure, check customer concentration** — Oracle's risk transmits to the entire portfolio through the order structures of CoreWeave and Nvidia.

**Even if you do not buy a company, you should study it, because its earnings are the thermometer for the whole supply chain.** Oracle's quarterly report tells us: demand is still hot, but financing has already begun to tighten. That information is equally useful for the other holdings in the portfolio.
