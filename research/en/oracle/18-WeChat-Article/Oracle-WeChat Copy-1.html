① RMB 664 billion in orders, negative RMB 5.4 billion cash flow: what is Oracle betting on
② A software company became a contractor in two quarters
③ Oracle down 41.7% — is it cheap

Body

Let me start with the conclusion: Oracle's earnings report is the most fractured one I have seen this year.

The top half looks impossibly good. FY27Q1 revenue was USD 19.345 billion, up 30% year on year; cloud infrastructure OCI USD 7.388 billion, up 121% year on year; remaining performance obligations (RPO) USD 664 billion, up 46% year on year; net income USD 4.76 billion, EPS USD 1.56. All four beat the consensus.

At its Abilene campus, it delivered 131,000 GPUs in a single quarter and completed acceptance in 24 hours. Microsoft, Amazon and Google have not done that.

The bottom half is too ugly to look at closely.

In the same quarter, capex was USD 28.499 billion, up 235% year on year, 1.47x revenue. Free cash flow was negative USD 5.396 billion, versus negative USD 23.7 billion for the whole prior fiscal year. Total debt is about USD 129.5 billion, debt/EBITDA 4.3x. In July, S&P cut its rating to BBB-, one notch above junk. In August, it completed a USD 20 billion equity offering, equal to 4.5% of market cap.

Read those lines together and something awkward emerges: a company with USD 4.76 billion of quarterly net income is selling stock and borrowing money to build the data centers that will fulfill its orders.

Why? Because its business has changed.

A decade ago, Oracle sold database licenses and collected annual maintenance fees. Customers could not switch—replacing a bank's core system costs USD 200-500 million, takes 3-5 years and fails often. So gross margin was 90%, capex was barely needed, and cash flow was like an annuity.

Today, 60% of Oracle's revenue comes from cloud services. OCI sells racks, GPUs and power. Customers comparison-shop, go with whoever is cheaper, and can migrate in weeks. Gross margin is around 30%, and every USD 1 of revenue requires USD 1.5 of assets built first.

So the story is not "a software company growing again"; it is "a software company turning into an IDC."

The transformation itself is no disgrace. The disgrace is buying IDC assets at a software company's valuation.

At USD 144.79, the stock trades at 17.9x FY27 non-GAAP PE, which looks inexpensive. But the denominator of that PE does not account for enough depreciation. This quarter's capex of USD 28.5 billion, amortized over six years, adds USD 1.2 billion of quarterly depreciation going forward, versus only USD 1.9 billion now. Once all the new assets are in service, quarterly depreciation doubles—the income statement will be caught by its own capex.

Another way to calculate: market cap USD 440 billion plus net debt USD 100 billion gives enterprise value of USD 540 billion; on a rough FY27 EBITDA of USD 40 billion, EV/EBITDA is about 13.5x. For a negative-free-cash-flow, BBB- heavy-asset company, 13.5x is not cheap.

Now the USD 664 billion order book.

It is real and legally binding. But it is a futures contract, not cash. Management says 12% converts within 12 months, or USD 76.6 billion. And the market estimates that more than half of that backlog is tied to OpenAI—a company that is still loss-making and surviving on round after round of financing.

What does that amount to? Oracle is using its investment-grade balance sheet to underwrite a startup's credit.

This has happened before, in telecom equipment. Lucent and Nortel extended vendor financing to upstart carriers, booked the revenue, never collected the cash, and both eventually disappeared.

Of course, I may be overthinking it. If AI compute demand does not become oversupplied before 2028, Oracle will complete the transformation smoothly, become a stable-cash-flow compute operator by 2030, and anyone buying now triples their money.

But look closely at how many conditions that "if" contains: OpenAI pays on time, financing costs do not rise, GPUs do not depreciate quickly, and the three big cloud providers do not start a price war. Four conditions, none of them in Oracle's own hands.

So my answer is: avoid.

No buy price, just two things to watch. First, free cash flow turns positive for a single quarter and stays positive for two consecutive quarters—that would signal expansion has entered the harvest phase. Second, the stock falls below USD 100, implying EV/EBITDA of about 9.5x—only then do the odds flip.

A 41.7% decline is not a reason to buy. A stock halving only means the market has changed its valuation framework; it does not mean the stock is cheap. Oracle's framework has shifted from "software" to "heavy-asset operator," and once that shift begins it will not reverse until the company generates cash on its own.

One last point, which may not be welcome:

If you are bullish on AI compute, do not buy Oracle. Buy Nvidia, because the pricing power sits with the seller; buy power equipment, because grid connection queues run five years; buy Microsoft, because it has positive cash flow to subsidize indefinitely.

Oracle carries both the "bullish on AI" and the "bearish on leverage" risk at once. That is the worst-odds position in the same bet.

Demand is real. But shareholders are not the highest-priority party in this trade.
