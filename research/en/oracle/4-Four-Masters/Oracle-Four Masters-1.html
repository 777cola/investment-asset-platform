Four Masters' Consensus

Four people, four entirely different vocabularies, pointing to the same action. Duan Yongping says this is not a differentiated business but a grind that pits capital against capital; Buffett says the moat sits in the old business while capex goes into the new one, and the two asset bases require two separate valuations; Munger says using leverage to chase a hot theme is a textbook losing game; Li Lu says the trend is right, but the builders will not earn excess returns. The three-scenario valuation yields -32% / +12% / +52%; on the surface the odds are not bad, but the optimistic scenario requires three external conditions to hold at once. Committee conclusion: avoid; wait for free cash flow to turn positive or for the price to fall below USD 100.

Duan Yongping: This is not differentiation, it is a capital contest

Duan Yongping first asks whether a company can explain in one sentence why it earns this money and why others cannot take it away. Ten years ago the answer was clear: enterprises cannot do without databases, they pay maintenance fees every year, and they accept price increases. Today the answer is: renting GPU racks, cheaper or faster than Microsoft, Amazon or CoreWeave.

The difference between these two statements is life-or-death. The first is differentiation; the second is cost competition. The premise of "dare to be last to the world" is that the latecomer can make something different; Oracle is a latecomer in OCI, but it makes the same thing as everyone else and can only buy time by burning money. He has a simple test: if a business needs continuous heavy capital investment to sustain growth, it is not a good business, at most "hard work that makes money." Add USD 129.5 billion of debt, and this is already beyond what he is willing to understand. His move is "don't touch," not "bearish"—don't touch because he does not understand it, not because he thinks it will fall.

Buffett: Two sets of assets, two valuations

Buffett would break this company into two balance sheets. The first is database and SaaS: customer migration costs measured in years, 90% gross margins, almost no capital expenditure.By his standards this asset is first-rate, worth 15–20x pre-tax profit, and he would want very much to own it.The second is OCI: USD 90 billion of capex a year, negative free cash flow, highly concentrated customers, and three cash-flow-positive giants as competitors. In his eyes this is "a utility plus leverage," fairly valued at 8–10x EV/EBITDA, and dependent on its rating.

Add the two sheets together and you get today's price. He would not say it is "absurdly expensive"; he would say it is buying an asset that is turning into a utility at a growth stock's price. The second thing he would watch is dilution: USD 20 billion of issuance this quarter, equal to 4.5% of market cap. He ran this math repeatedly at GEICO in the 1980s and at BNSF in the 2010s—"if a company has to keep selling stock to grow, the growth shareholders get is diluted growth."This is his old reason for avoiding airlines and asset-heavy cyclicals, and it applies perfectly here.

Munger: invert the question — what would make it halve again?

Munger would not bother calculating the RPO conversion rate. He would draw up a "how it dies" list: ① OpenAI's fundraising stalls or it cuts procurement, and growth in the RMB 664 billion order book stalls immediately; ② rates rise or the rating is cut one more notch, BBB- to BB+, issuance costs jump 200 basis points, and project returns turn negative; ③ GPU iteration pushes down rental rates on older cards, the depreciation assumptions are overturned, and accounting profit evaporates; ④ the three major cloud providers use in-house chips (Trainium, Maia, TPU) to drive down cost per token, forcing OCI to follow with price cuts.

If any one of these four occurs, the market's valuation of the company switches from "AI growth stock" back to "asset-heavy operator"; USD 144 becomes USD 90 without any earnings decline, merely a re-rating of the multiple. The conclusion of "invert, always invert" is this: the company's fate rests on four variables it cannot control, and that is the worst corporate structure of all — not because management is stupid, but because the structure itself has no margin for error. He would add one more line: no industry in history that used leverage to chase a hot theme has ended well — railways, airlines, fiber optics, shale oil, the list is long.

Li Lu: Right trend, wrong position

Li Lu would first acknowledge the trend: compute is the civilization-scale infrastructure investment of this generation, larger and more durable than 5G or fiber-to-the-home. He would also acknowledge that Oracle has secured a position — the Abilene campus delivered 131,000 GPUs in a single quarter and cleared acceptance in 24 hours; that kind of engineering capability is real skill, not luck.

But he would point to the historical pattern: the gains from infrastructure buildouts ultimately flow to capital providers and equipment suppliers, not to the builders themselves. In the railroad era, the money was made by Morgan and Carnegie, not by railroad shareholders; in the fiber era, by Corning and Cisco, not by Level 3; in the aviation era, by Boeing and aircraft lessors, not by the airlines. Oracle now plays both "builder" and "borrower" — the bigger the orders, the tighter the balance sheet. Unless it can lock in long-term pricing power in its contracts (not visible today; AI customers have far stronger bargaining power than traditional enterprise customers), this is trading shareholder money for scale. His conclusion would be milder than Munger's, but his action the same: wait until the business proves it can generate free cash flow.

Odds assessment: 1 to 1.6 payoff, but the win rate is not yours to set

Lay out the three scenarios: downside -32%, upside +52%, base +12%. Weighted at 30/50/20 probabilities, expected return is about +0.2%—essentially zero. The payoff ratio looks like 1:1.6, but that treats the optimistic scenario as an independent event with 20% probability, when in fact it requires three things to hold simultaneously: OpenAI honors its commitments, financing costs do not rise, and GPUs do not depreciate. These three are highly correlated (all point to "the AI capex cycle not peaking"), so the true joint probability is below the apparent 20%.

A better framework is to treat it as a quasi-bond: enterprise value of RMB 540 billion, FY27 EBITDA of about RMB 40 billion, EV/EBITDA of 13.5x, rating BBB-. In bond language, you are getting roughly 9% expected annualized return (if all goes well) while bearing the risk of equity halving on a downgrade to junk. That risk-reward is not a position worth sizing up. Only at USD 100 (EV/EBITDA of about 9.5x) does the payoff flip.

Conclusion

Conclusion: the four masters concur — avoid. Duan Yongping objects that it is not a good business, Buffett that the price was paid for the wrong assets, Munger that the structure has no margin for error, and Li Lu that the position cannot earn excess returns. Four different reasons, one same action. The conditions that would trigger a re-evaluation are stated plainly: free cash flow turns positive in a single quarter and holds for two consecutive quarters, or the share price falls below USD 100 (EV/EBITDA below 9.5x). If either condition appears, the business deserves to be re-underwritten; until then, any talk of the "RMB 664 billion order book" is already embedded in the USD 144.79 price.
