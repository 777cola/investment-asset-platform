# Oracle（ORCL）— Duan Yongping Supplement

- Research date：2026/09/14
- Price：144.79 USD
- Market cap：About USD 440 billion

## Notes

This module poses hypothetical questions on Oracle, framed around the investment principles Warren Buffett has publicly articulated: stay within your circle of competence, avoid what you do not understand, seek differentiation, dare to be last to act, and do not try to time the market. The answers are not his views and do not represent any actual position or statement.

## Q: Is this a good business?

Answer: Half good, half bad — and the good half is shrinking. The database is a good business: customers can't switch out, gross margin is 90%, and they accept price increases. That is classic differentiation. OCI is not: customers shop on price and go with whoever is cheaper, so you have to keep investing to retain them. What makes a good business? One that grows on its own without you putting money in. Oracle's current business does not grow unless money is spent.

Some will point to its RMB 664 billion order book. Orders are orders; business is business. Orders must be fulfilled through data centers, data centers must be built with borrowed money, and borrowed money must be repaid. If a business needs to keep borrowing to turn orders into revenue, its profits do not fully belong to shareholders.

## Q: Will it still be around in ten years?

Yes. Ten years from now the database will still be there, and banks, telecoms and governments will still be paying maintenance fees — on that I have no concern at all. The question is what will underpin its market cap in ten years. If by then it is an IDC with a software business attached, it should trade on an IDC valuation, not a software valuation.

I am not much good at forecasting EPS ten years out. I ask only one question: in ten years, will it need to spend USD 90 billion a year just to sustain revenue? If so, it is not the company I am looking for. The answer is very likely yes, because AI compute refreshes so fast that the cards bought this year are obsolete three years later.

## Can you understand it?

What I understand: it sells databases, sells cloud, collects maintenance fees—all of that I get. What I don't understand: how many years a GPU actually lasts, how much rents will fall next year, whether OpenAI can still raise money next year, whether a BBB- bond can be issued next year. These four things determine its value, and all of them lie outside my circle of competence.

Don't do what you don't understand—that is the baseline. Not because I think it will fall, but because I lack the ability to judge it. Many people lose money not because they bought the wrong company, but because they bought a company they didn't understand, and then didn't know what to do when it fell.

## Q: At what price would you buy?

Honestly, I probably would not buy at any price. This is not a question of price; it is a question of business model. If a business can only grow by continuously raising capital, then no matter how low its price, it is merely "cheap leverage," not a "cheap good company."

If I had to name a number: I would only start working out what it is worth once its free cash flow turns positive and stays positive for several consecutive quarters. Before that, whether the price falls to 100 or 80 makes no difference to me—that is gambling, not investing. I don't gamble.

## Q: What is the biggest fear

I don't own it, so I'm not worried about the downside. But if I did, my biggest fear wouldn't be OpenAI cutting orders or a ratings downgrade—those are visible. It would be "invisible depreciation." The company depreciates over six years; if the actual useful life is four, it understates cost by several hundred million dollars a year, and every number on the income statement is fiction—and that only surfaces two or three years from now.

Optimistic accounting assumptions are the hardest risk for investors to guard against. They don't arrive suddenly like bad news; they build slowly, then erupt all at once on some single day.

## Q: So would you short?

No. Shorting requires timing the market, and I never time the market. A poor business model does not mean the stock falls tomorrow—it could rise 50% on a single order or a single headline, and by then the short seller is dead.

My approach is simple: stay away, watch, and let it prove itself. The market is vast and good companies are plentiful; there is no need to spend time on a business you cannot understand. Time is best spent where you can understand things—that is the most worthwhile investment of all.
