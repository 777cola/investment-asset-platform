# Oracle（ORCL）— Duan Yongping Supplement

- Research date：2026/09/14
- Price：144.79 USD
- Market cap：About USD 440 billion

## Notes

This module is a hypothetical Q&A on Oracle derived from the investment principles Warren Buffett has publicly articulated (benben, don't do what you don't understand, differentiation, dare to be last to act, don't time the market). It is not his own view and does not represent any actual position or statement.

## Q: Is this a good business?

Answer: Half good, half bad — and the good half is shrinking. The database is a good business: customers can't switch out, gross margin is 90%, and they accept price increases. That is classic differentiation. OCI is not a good business: customers shop on price and go with whoever is cheaper, so you have to keep investing to retain them. What makes a good business? One that grows on its own without you putting money in. Oracle's current business does not grow unless money is spent.

Some will point to its RMB 664 billion order book. Orders are orders; business is business. Orders must be fulfilled through data centers, data centers must be built with borrowed money, and borrowed money must be repaid. If a business needs to keep borrowing to turn orders into revenue, its profits do not fully belong to shareholders.

## Q: Will it still be around in ten years?

Yes. Ten years from now the database will still be there, and banks, telecoms and governments will still be paying it maintenance fees — that part I have no concern about at all. The question is what will support its market cap in ten years. If by then it is an IDC with a software business, its valuation should be an IDC valuation, not a software company valuation.

I am not much good at calculating EPS ten years out. I only ask one question: in ten years, will it need to spend USD 90 billion a year just to sustain revenue? If so, it is not the company I am looking for. The answer is very likely yes, because AI compute technology refreshes so fast that the cards bought this year are obsolete three years later.

## Can you understand it?

What I understand: it sells databases, sells cloud, collects maintenance fees—all of that I get. What I don't understand: how many years a GPU actually lasts, how much rents will fall next year, whether OpenAI can still raise money next year, whether a BBB- bond can be issued next year. These four things determine its value, and all of them lie outside my circle of competence.

Don't do what you don't understand—that is the baseline. Not because I think it will fall, but because I lack the ability to judge it. Many people lose money not because they bought the wrong company, but because they bought a company they didn't understand, and then didn't know what to do when it fell.

## Q: At what price would you buy?

Honestly, I probably would not buy at any price. This is not a question of price; it is a question of business model. If a business can only grow by continuously raising capital, then no matter how low its price, it is merely "cheap leverage," not a "cheap good company."

If I had to name a number: I would only start working out what it is worth once its free cash flow turns positive and stays positive for several consecutive quarters. Before that, whether the price falls to 100 or 80 makes no difference to me—that is gambling, not investing. I don't gamble.

## Q: What is the biggest fear

I do not hold it, so I am not afraid of it falling. But if I did hold it, what I would fear most is not OpenAI cutting orders, nor a ratings downgrade—those are visible. What I fear most is "invisible depreciation." The company depreciates over six years; if the actual life is four, it underrecords several hundred million dollars of cost each year, and every number on the income statement is fake—and this will only surface two or three years from now.

Optimistic accounting assumptions are the hardest thing for investors to guard against. They do not appear suddenly like bad news; they accumulate slowly, then erupt all at once on some day.

## Q: So would you short?

No. Shorting requires timing the market, and I never time the market. A company with a poor business model does not mean its stock will fall tomorrow—it could rise 50% on a single order or a single piece of news, and by then the short seller is dead.

My approach is simple: stay away, watch, and let it prove itself. The market is vast and good companies are plentiful; there is no need to spend time on a company you cannot understand. Time should go where you can understand things—that is the most worthwhile investment.
