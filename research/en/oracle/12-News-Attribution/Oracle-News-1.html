Attribution of unusual moves

Oracle saw six notable events between mid-June and mid-September: FY26Q4 results, the S&P downgrade, the USD 20 billion equity offering, FY27Q1 results, database business +353%, and the mid-September pullback. The stock fell from a high of USD 248.15 to USD 144.79, down 41.7%, yet revenue growth rose from just over 20% to 30% over the same period—a classic "valuation kill," not an "earnings kill." Of the six, only the downgrade and the equity offering are genuine signals; the rest are noise or already priced in.

First, the qualitative take: this is a valuation de-rating, not an earnings miss

From USD 248.15 to USD 144.79 — a 41.7% decline. What happened to the fundamentals over the same period? Revenue growth accelerated from just over 20% in FY26Q4 to 30% in FY27Q1, OCI growth from the 60% range to 121%, and RPO from RMB 638 billion to RMB 664 billion. Fundamentals are accelerating while the stock has halved — there is only one explanation: the market has re-rated the valuation multiple.

Why did the multiple change? Because the market finally understood the structure of these results: this is not a software company but an IDC that builds data centers with leverage. Software companies command 25x P/E; IDCs command 10x EV/EBITDA. This de-rating is not sentiment — the pricing framework has changed. Once the framework changes, even the best quarterly numbers cannot pull it back.

Real signal: rating and share placement

S&P's downgrade to BBB- on 29 July was the single most important event of the past three months. It does not change current-period profit, but it changes the return on every future project: the rating sets the coupon, and the coupon determines whether a project can make money. At BBB-, Oracle sits just one notch above junk, so the next downgrade would trigger mandatory-sale clauses at some funds — many investment-grade funds cannot hold high-yield debt.

The USD 20 billion share issuance in August is the second signal, and Oracle's first large-scale equity financing in twenty years. A company that spent the past decade buying back hundreds of billions of dollars of stock with free cash flow has suddenly started selling stock — the move itself says management believes cash is no longer sufficient, and will not be sufficient any time soon.

Noise: why +353% and +121% fail to move the share price

Database revenue growth of +353% looks striking, but it carries two problems: the base is tiny, and the growth is not sustainable, having come from one-off large-customer signings and upgrades of existing customers. The market did not pay for it, because the market knows it will not repeat next year.

OCI +121% likewise failed to lift the stock, coming as it did alongside RMB 28.5 billion of capex.The market has done the math clearly this time: every extra USD 1 of OCI gross profit requires USD 1.5 of capex first, and that arithmetic does not pay at a 5% interest rate.This is not market short-termism; it is the market finally starting to do the math.

Which are the real signals, and what to watch going forward

Three signals, in order of importance: ① Rating actions — downgrades from Moody's and Fitch would push financing costs sharply higher, and that is the real inflection point; ② Funding method — another share issuance next quarter would signal a cash-flow gap larger than disclosed; ③ RPO conversion — whether 12-month conversion in the Q2 results reaches the pace implied by RMB 76.6 billion.

Ignore the noise: single-day moves, AI-sector β, broker target-price changes and any rumour that "OpenAI signed another big deal" — none of these change cash flow. The December Q2 results are the next real test; everything else is background noise.

Conclusion

Conclusion: the 41.7% drop is a de-rating, not a collapse — but the de-rating is not over. The market has shifted its valuation framework from "software" to "asset-heavy operator," and once that shift begins it does not reverse until free cash flow turns positive. Two of the six events are genuine signals (the rating and the share placement), and both point to the same conclusion: financing structure is now this company's most important variable — more important than orders or growth.
