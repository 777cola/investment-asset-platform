Rule one: look at cash flow first, then orders

The most easily misread aspect of this company is that orders and cash flow point in opposite directions—orders of RMB 664 billion, free cash flow of -RMB 5.4 billion. The larger the orders, the worse short-term cash flow, because the data centers must be built first. The anchor for any decision must therefore be cash flow, not orders.

There is only one specific test: free cash flow turns positive on a single-quarter basis and stays positive for two consecutive quarters. If this holds, expansion has entered the harvest phase; if not, the company is still burning cash. Orders can rise from RMB 664 billion to RMB 800 billion, but as long as cash flow remains negative, the conclusion is unchanged.

Rule 2: Price only matters after cash flow improves

Many will ask, "how far does it have to fall before I can buy?" For a company with no cash flow, the answer is: price only determines the odds, not the probability of winning. Buying at USD 100 gives good odds, but the probability of winning still depends on OpenAI's financing and GPU depreciation.

So the price rule must be tied to the cash-flow rule: below USD 100 and cash flow still negative → 2% at most; below USD 100 and cash flow already positive → up to 4%. Same USD 100, but the position size differs by a factor of two — that is the value of rules.

Rule 3: Three red lines — exit on trigger

① Rating cut to BB+ or below — financing costs jump, project returns turn negative, equity becomes an option. ② Free cash flow below -RMB 8 billion for two consecutive quarters — the gap widens, dilution accelerates, and per-share value is continuously eroded. ③ A single major customer contract is disclosed as terminated or sharply reduced — the foundation of the order book collapses, and the RMB 664 billion must be revalued.

The purpose of these three red lines is not prediction but capping maximum loss. Companies with this kind of high leverage share one trait: low-probability events cause large losses, and the point of rules is to cage the low-probability losses.

Rule 4: If you are bullish on AI compute, do not buy this one

This is the most important point. Bullish on AI compute ≠ buy Oracle. The former is a judgment on demand; the latter is an investment in a highly leveraged contractor. To express a demand view, buy the links where bargaining power sits with the seller—chips (Nvidia), power equipment (transformers, gas turbines), cloud with positive cash flow (Microsoft).

Oracle simultaneously carries risk in two directions—"bullish on AI" and "bearish on leverage"—giving it the worst risk-reward. In investing, choosing "which instrument to express a view with" matters as much as whether the view itself is right.

One last question: is there a better use for this money?

Put in USD 144.79, the base case three years out is USD 162 (+12%), or 4% annualized; the bear case is USD 98 (-32%). The same money buys Microsoft at 8%–10% annualized over three years, Nvidia at 15% annualized (if the AI cycle does not peak), or short-dated bonds at a risk-free 4.5%. Oracle's base-case return is lower than the risk-free rate.

This is the final layer of the decision: not "will it go up," but "is this risk premium enough." The answer is no. Avoiding it is not a bearish call; it is that this money has better places to go.

Conclusion

Conclusion: rules beat opinions. Of the four rules, the first (cash flow) decides whether to buy, the second (price) decides how much to buy, the third (red lines) decides when to exit, and the fourth (tools) decides how to express the view. Where we stand now: the first does not hold, so position is 0% and the other three do not yet apply.
