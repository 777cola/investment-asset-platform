Nature of the business: from rent collector to contractor in just two quarters — Oracle

The quality of a business depends on whether it needs constant reinvestment to grow.

Core view

Oracle runs two businesses at once: one collects rent, the other takes on contract work. In fiscal 2026, contract revenue overtook rent for the first time. This is not financial engineering but a change in the nature of the business — rent depends on customers being unable to leave you, while contract work depends on being faster and cheaper than everyone else.

Where does the moat in the rent-collection business come from?

In the database business, the money is not made from technology; it is made from being impossible to replace. When a bank's core ledger system runs on Exadata, replacing it means rewriting applications, migrating data, redoing audits, filing downtime windows with regulators, and retraining DBAs. The cost of that process is not software fees; it is business risk. As long as the risk of migration exceeds the cost of price increases, customers will keep paying.

This kind of business generates excellent cash flow, but it has a ceiling: the customer count is not growing, so revenue can only come from price increases and add-on modules. Software licenses and support fell 3% this quarter, showing that even price increases cannot offset customers migrating to the cloud. The rent-collection business is slowly leaking air, which is why Oracle must find a second curve.

How contractors make money

OCI's money comes from three spreads: the power price spread (self-built generation vs grid purchases), the delivery timing spread (powering up three months earlier than rivals, for which customers pay a premium), and the scale procurement spread (buying GPUs in bulk at a unit price a few points lower). All three are operational, not structural—a competitor willing to lose money can erase them.

And the contracting business has one fatal trait: its costs come first, its revenue comes later. Data centers must be built upfront (RMB 28.5 billion per quarter) while customers pay monthly (RMB 76.6 billion per year). The gap in between is filled with debt and equity issuance. That explains why a company with RMB 4.76 billion in net profit runs free cash flow of -RMB 5.4 billion.

A one-sentence summary of the business essence

If you had to sum up what Oracle is doing in one sentence: it has turned itself into an infrastructure outsourcing company for AI companies, fronting the capital with creditors' and shareholders' money and earning the spread between engineering speed and scale procurement. This business can make money, and in times of tight demand it can make a lot of money, but its profits accrue not to "brand" or "technology" but to "capital + execution."

The valuation ceiling for this kind of business is 1.2x replacement cost, and the floor is liquidation value. It will never get the 25x PE of a software company, because its growth requires continuous capital investment—and the market has always valued businesses that "need continuous capital investment to grow" as cyclicals.

Where is the ceiling for this business?

The ceiling on the contract-engineering business is "society-wide compute capex." Industry-wide that is USD 700 billion in 2026, with Oracle at roughly 13%. If it could lift its share to 25%, revenue would double; but every point of share has to be bought with capex, and its cost of capital is higher than rivals'. That is the ceiling: not insufficient demand, but its inability to afford a larger share.

Conclusion

Conclusion: the business is essentially "capital-intensive engineering outsourcing," not "high-margin software licensing." This characterization determines its valuation ceiling, its cyclicality, and its sensitivity to financing costs. Get the characterization wrong and every number after it will be wrong.
