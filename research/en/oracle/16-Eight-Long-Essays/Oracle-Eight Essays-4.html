Core view

GAAP net income for the quarter was USD 4.76 billion and EPS USD 1.56, up 62.6% year on year, twice the revenue growth rate. There is no fabrication in this number, but it is far from "this company earned USD 4.76 billion in cash." This piece breaks down every step from revenue to cash to see where the money got stuck.

Operating profit, interest, pre-tax profit, depreciation and working capital are derived estimates (back-solved from disclosed margins and debt levels), not line-item disclosures by the company. Operating cash flow = free cash flow + capital expenditure.
The softest line in the income statement: depreciation

Depreciation this quarter was about USD 1.9 billion. What asset base does that imply? At six-year depreciation, 1.9 billion/quarter × 4 × 6 = USD 45.6 billion of gross assets. Yet the company's capex over the past four quarters totaled about USD 100 billion.In other words, a large block of newly added assets has not yet begun to depreciate—it sits in construction in progress, or is in its first year of depreciation.

Amortized over six years, once the USD 100 billion of new assets is fully in service, it adds 16.7 billion of depreciation a year, or 4.2 billion a quarter annualized—more than double the current level.Operating profit would then fall from 8.1 billion to about 5.8 billion, and net profit from 4.76 billion to 3.3 billion.That is why 17.9x PE is an accounting illusion—it uses earnings that depreciation has not yet weighed on.

The hardest line in the cash flow statement: financing

Operating cash flow of RMB 23.1 billion looks strong, but broken down, RMB 16.4 billion came from working capital changes (customer prepayments, deferred revenue). This money was not "earned" — it was "paid upfront by customers." The upside is that it is free and interest-free; the downside is that it is unstable — once new contract signings slow, this portion reverses into an outflow.

Capex of RMB 28.5 billion, free cash flow of -RMB 5.4 billion. The gap is filled by two things: debt (total debt RMB 129.5 billion) and equity (RMB 20 billion raised this quarter). Put these three numbers together and the company's business model can be described precisely as: using customer prepayments and external financing to convert cash into data centers, then collecting rent from those data centers to repay debt. This loop holds when demand grows and breaks when demand stalls.

ROIC: the only metric that tells you whether it is worth it

Rough calculation: NOPAT of about RMB 23.7 billion (EBIT RMB 30 billion × 0.79), invested capital of about RMB 200 billion (net debt RMB 100 billion + equity RMB 20 billion + other), ROIC of about 11.8%. Weighted cost of capital of about 7% (debt 5.5%–6%, equity 9%). A 4.8-point spread shows it is still creating value today.

But that 11.8% is inflated: the EBIT in the numerator has not been charged enough depreciation, and the assets in the denominator are still under construction. Calculated on the true return of new assets — RMB 90 billion of capex at an assumed 12% asset return — that generates RMB 10.8 billion of EBIT a year; after RMB 6 billion of interest and RMB 2.5 billion of tax, shareholders get RMB 2.3 billion. Against a market cap of RMB 440 billion, that is a 0.5% return. That is the problem: returns on the existing asset base are decent, but the return on new assets is extremely low.

Accounting quality: three warning signs

Signal one: operating cash flow growth far outpaces net profit growth (RMB 23.1 billion vs RMB 4.76 billion).This is usually a good sign, but here it mainly reflects customer prepayments, not operational improvement.Signal two: capitalization ratio rises.A higher capitalization ratio for construction in progress and intangible assets defers expense recognition.Signal three: the gap between non-GAAP and GAAP widens (RMB 5.02 billion vs RMB 4.760 billion).The gap itself is not large, but the trend is widening.

None of the three signals constitutes "fraud"; they are "aggressive accounting choices."Aggressive accounting choices flatter the statements during expansion and make them suddenly ugly during contraction—and the hit is one-off.

Conclusion

Conclusion: the profit is real, but it is profit that depreciation has not yet caught up with. Cash flow is negative, and financing fills the gap. The only valid metric for judging whether this company is worth owning is the return on incremental capital, not PE. A rough calculation based on the current capex pace puts the shareholder return on incremental capital at less than 1%, and that number matters more than any order announcement.
