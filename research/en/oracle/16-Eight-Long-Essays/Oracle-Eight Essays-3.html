Moat anatomy: one real, one fake — Oracle

A moat depends on which revenue stream it protects

Core view

Oracle has two moats, one real and one fake. The real one protects 29% of revenue; the fake one carries 60% of revenue and all of capex. Lumping the two together and saying "Oracle has a moat" is the most common error in this kind of analysis.

The real moat: how switching costs work

A database moat does not live in the code; it lives in customers' balance sheets and regulatory filings. When a bank replaces its core system, the budget is typically USD 200–500 million, the cycle runs 3–5 years, and the failure rate is extremely high (multiple banks have failed at core-system replacements historically, with losses in the billions). For a CIO, replacing an Oracle database is not a technology decision but a career-risk decision. As long as "not switching" carries no accountability, "switching" will never happen.

This moat has a counterintuitive piece of evidence: database-related business grew 353% year on year this quarter, while both AWS and Azure have onboarded Oracle Database. When competitors' clouds voluntarily make themselves compatible with your database, your database is an asset customers refuse to give up. This is the hardest kind of moat — not "I am better than you," but "customers will not let you disappear."

False moat: why compute has no barrier

What OCI sells breaks down into three things: racks, power, and network. All three are commodities. For a customer to migrate to another cloud, they do not need to rewrite applications—only redeploy containers and migrate data, a cycle of a few weeks at a cost of a few million dollars—nothing like a database migration in magnitude.Low switching costs mean no pricing power.

Oracle's advantages in OCI are real: the Abilene campus delivered 131,000 GPUs in a single quarter, 13.1 万张 GPU accepted within 24 hours, and self-built fuel cells that bypass grid-connection queues.But these are "execution advantages," which can be caught up; a moat requires "structural advantages," which cannot be replicated.Execution advantages are valuable when demand is scarce and worthless when there is a surplus—because in a surplus, customers do not lack suppliers, they lack cheapness.

Moat Transferability: Why Old Moats Cannot Save New Businesses

Many will argue: Oracle has database customers, and those customers will naturally use OCI when they move to the cloud. That logic sounds smooth, but the data does not support it—of OCI's RMB 7.388 billion in revenue, the increment mainly comes from AI labs, not from traditional database customers. When traditional customers moved to the cloud, most chose AWS or Azure, because the application ecosystem there is more complete.

A moat has boundaries. The database moat protects "where the data sits," not "where the compute sits." In the cloud era these two things are separate—customers can perfectly well leave the database with Oracle and put training workloads on AWS. This report calls this "the moat does not migrate," and it is the key to understanding Oracle's valuation: the old moat is worth RMB 300 billion; the new business must be valued separately.

The Depreciation of Moats: Why Old Moats Are Losing Value

The database moat has not disappeared, but it is narrowing. Two reasons: first, customers are putting new applications on the cloud, and new applications do not necessarily need Oracle Database; second, open-source databases (PostgreSQL) are replacing Oracle among small and mid-sized customers. Software support revenue fell 3% this quarter, the first negative growth in this moat.

A moat does not vanish overnight; it retreats like a glacier. The valuation implication: this business should be priced as a "declining annuity," not as "perpetual growth." Discounted at a 5% annual decline, the software business is worth 220.0 billion, not 350.0 billion.

Conclusion

Conclusion: the real moat defends the old business; the fake moat props up the new. The investment implication is not "no moat" but "the revenue the moat covers is shrinking, and the growing revenue has no moat." Under this structure, the company's valuation should equal the sum of the separate valuations of the two businesses, not a growth-stock multiple applied to the whole.
