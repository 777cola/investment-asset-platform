Management: execution A, capital allocation C+ — Oracle

Two things matter when evaluating management: can they run the business, and will they share the wealth

Core view

Oracle's management is the strongest team in this AI infrastructure cycle. But "strong" and "shareholder-friendly" are two different things. In fiscal 2026, it did three things at once that it had not done in decades: issue debt at scale, raise equity in size, and concentrate its order book on a single customer.

Execution: flawless

The Abilene campus delivered 131,000 GPUs in a single quarter and completed acceptance in 24 hours — a speed none of the three major cloud providers achieved. Its self-built fuel cells bypass the grid-connection queue, compressing time-to-power from 3–5 years to under 1 year — a demonstration of engineering capability that capital cannot simply buy. In an industry where every competitor is racing for time, delivery speed is itself a competitive advantage.

That is also why it grew RPO from RMB 638 billion to RMB 664 billion in two quarters. AI customers choose it not because it is cheap, but because it can deliver in windows where others cannot. This deserves full credit.

Capital allocation: three pivots, each more aggressive than the last

The first pivot was "buying revenue" from 2005 to 2022: PeopleSoft at RMB 10.3 billion, Sun at RMB 7.4 billion, NetSuite at RMB 9.3 billion, Cerner at RMB 28.3 billion. The logic of this playbook was to use cash flow to buy software assets that were already profitable, then migrate customers onto its own tech stack. It worked until 2022.

The second pivot was "buybacks + dividends" from 2023 to 2025: return most free cash flow to shareholders, shrinking the share count all the way. The third pivot is 2026: buybacks stall, a RMB 20 billion equity raise, debt piled up to RMB 129.5 billion, all poured into AI infrastructure. Of the three pivots, the third carries the highest risk, because it stakes the company's fate on an external variable—the length of the AI capex cycle. This is not a question of capability but of choice, and choices can be criticized.

Governance: a company where one person calls the shots

Ellison holds about 40%, is executive chairman and CTO, and is still driving strategy at 82. The upside of this structure is fast decisions and stable strategy; the downside is that no mechanism can constrain his judgment—the board, co-CEO and CFO are all execution layers. Betting the company on AI infrastructure in 2026 was his decision alone.

Two other things to remember: in 2020 the company settled with the SEC over FCPA violations by subsidiaries in Turkey, India and the UAE, paying USD 23.2 million in penalties and disgorgement; and it has long collected fees from customers through license audits. Neither is illegal, but both show that between "customer success" and "collecting for itself," this company always chooses the latter. Such companies usually make money, but they rarely win customers' genuine loyalty.

Incentive structure: what they are accountable for

The co-CEOs are measured mainly on OCI revenue, RPO and delivery milestones, with no free cash flow metric. This is not an oversight but a choice — the board believes the task at this stage is to grab share, not to make money. That choice is reasonable during expansion, but it explains why the company draws down every financing tool at once.

The implication for investors: management's KPIs and shareholder returns are not the same thing. They are accountable for "scale"; shareholders are accountable for "value per share". When RMB 20 billion of new shares are issued, the two point in opposite directions.

Conclusion

Conclusion: execution A, capital allocation C+, governance B-. Investment implication: do not use "strong management" to hedge "a tight balance sheet." Strong-execution management is an asset in expansion and a liability in contraction—because they are bad at contracting, and what this company needs most after 2028 is precisely contraction.
