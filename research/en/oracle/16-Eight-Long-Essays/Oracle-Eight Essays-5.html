Industry and competition: where the profit in the value chain went — Oracle

Where an industry makes money and where it grows are often not the same place

Core view

AI compute is the largest capex cycle of this generation, with industry-wide investment running at the USD 700 billion level in 2026. But profit distribution across the value chain is highly uneven: chips take the largest share, power the second, the software layer the third through lock-in, and pure compute leasing comes last. 60% of Oracle's revenue sits in that last slot.

Supply and demand: 2027 is the watershed

Demand is not the problem. The four major cloud providers will spend a combined USD 700 billion on capex in 2026; AI training and inference are both surging, and Nvidia has raised capacity for six consecutive quarters yet remains unable to meet demand. Oracle's ability to sign USD 664 billion in RPO shows it secured a position during the tightest window.

The supply-side numbers deserve more attention: industry-wide new data center capacity in 2026 is more than triple 2023 levels, and given an 18–24 month construction cycle, it will come online in a concentrated wave from the second half of 2027 to the first half of 2028. If demand growth does not accelerate further before then, we will see "data centers built, customers missing." In a glut, the lowest-cost player survives—and Oracle's cost of capital is roughly 300 basis points higher than Microsoft's.

Competition: three types of rivals, three approaches

The first category is the three major cloud providers. They use in-house chips (Trainium, Maia, TPU) to push cost per token down to 60% of Nvidia's solution, subsidize compute with cloud gross margin, then trade compute for long-term contracts. Oracle lacks the ability to cross-subsidize and has only higher leverage. The second category is neoclouds. CoreWeave and Nebius compete for the same customers with the same leveraged model; a price war compresses both — they die together rather than kill each other. The third category is customer self-build. OpenAI, Meta and xAI are all building their own data centers. When the largest buyers become builders, the sublandlord is left with only a "fill-the-valley" role.

Of the three, the third is the most lethal because it directly eliminates incremental demand; the first is the most enduring because it can subsidize indefinitely; the second is the loudest but does not change the landscape. The market keeps watching the second category because its stocks swing the most.

Cycle position: at the tail end of the construction peak

There is a simple way to judge where we are in the cycle: look at whether the most aggressive company in the industry is still adding leverage. Oracle's FY27 capex is USD 90–95 billion, debt is USD 129.5 billion, and it needs to raise another USD 30–40 billion—the most aggressive player is still flooring the accelerator, which says the cycle has not peaked, but also that it has no way back.

History shows that overcapacity in capital-intensive industries always lags the share-price peak by 6–12 months. The stock falls first (because valuation gets hit first), and overcapacity arrives later (because projects already under way cannot be stopped).Oracle shares are already down 41.7% from their high, but overcapacity has not happened yet—that time gap is where the risk lies.

A counterintuitive fact: the fastest growth, the lowest valuation

Oracle's OCI grew 121%, the fastest in the industry; yet its PE (23.5x) is lower than Microsoft's (33x), Amazon's (35x) and Google's (26x). The market is not blind to the growth; it simply will not pay a premium for "growth bought with leverage."

That discount will persist until the company proves it can grow without raising capital. Historically, all asset-heavy companies have traded at a valuation center of 8–12x EV/EBITDA; Oracle now sits at 13.5x, already above peers. The claim of "fastest growth, lowest valuation" is an illusion born of mixing PE and EV/EBITDA comparisons.

Conclusion

Conclusion: strong industry, unfavorable position, elevated cycle. AI compute is a real super-cycle, but the profit does not sit with "the people building the data centers"; Oracle occupies the thinnest link in the value chain and has loaded up on leverage just before concentrated capacity comes online. These three factors combined constitute the core risk in this stock.
