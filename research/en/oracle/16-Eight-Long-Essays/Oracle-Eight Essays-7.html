Risk checklist: five variables that could wipe out equity value — Oracle

Core view

The risks facing this company are not diversified but highly correlated—five risks driven by a single variable: where we are in the AI capex cycle. When the cycle peaks, customers cut orders, rents fall, ratings are downgraded and utilization declines all at once. These are not five independent events but five facets of one event.

Risk one: lending investment-grade credit to a startup

Of the RMB 664 billion RPO, the market estimates more than half relates to OpenAI. This is not demand risk but credit risk — Oracle has already built the data centers per contract (capex has been incurred), but collection waits five years out. If the counterparty's financing stalls, Oracle is left holding a stack of built data centers and a stack of unperformed contracts.

This structure appeared in telecom equipment: Lucent and Nortel extended vendor financing to upstart carriers, revenue was recognized, cash never came back. The difference is that Lucent did not have to carry RMB 129.5 billion of debt; Oracle does.

Risk 2: Financing cost is the invisible hand

RMB 129.5 billion of debt, 4.3x leverage, BBB-. Every 100 bp rise in rates adds RMB 1.3 billion of annual interest; a one-notch downgrade to BB+ pushes the coupon to 7.5%–8.5% and annual interest from RMB 6.5 billion to over RMB 10 billion. Yet data-center asset returns run roughly 10%–12%, so at an 8% cost of capital shareholders barely make money.

Worse is the maturity mismatch: debt rolls every three years, while assets depreciate over six years and take twenty to pay back. That mismatch is harmless when rates are stable and lethal the moment credit tightens.

Risk 3: Depreciation assumptions are the model's hidden trapdoor

The company depreciates GPUs and related equipment over six years. If the true economic life is four years, it would book 50% more depreciation each year; based on FY27 capex of USD 90 billion, the one-year depreciation gap alone is USD 7.5 billion, exceeding this quarter's net profit. And once the depreciation assumption is lowered, it is a retrospective adjustment that would wipe out several quarters of profit in one hit.

The "20% renewal premium on older cards, 97.9% utilization" disclosed this quarter is the evidence supporting the six-year assumption, and also the most fragile evidence — if utilization falls to 85%, the premium disappears. This is the most easily overlooked and potentially most fatal point.

Risks four and five: overcapacity and regulation

The timing of overcapacity is closer than expected: industry-wide new capacity in 2026 is triple that of 2023, with concentrated commissioning starting in the second half of 2027. For a company at 4.3x leverage with negative free cash flow, that timing is fatal—it has no reserves to survive a downturn.

Regulatory risk is relatively small but present: if AI compute export controls extend to cloud services, Oracle's overseas campus revenue would suffer; the TikTok joint venture gives it a unique data-hosting qualification, but also exposes it to political cycles. Such risks do not change the long-term thesis, but they amplify volatility.

The overlooked sixth risk: accounting and auditing

One more item not on the earlier list: when depreciation assumptions, capitalization policy and non-GAAP adjustments all lean optimistic at the same time, pressure on the auditor rises. If a qualified audit opinion or a restatement appears in any future quarter, that would be a more severe shock than a ratings downgrade—because it directly undermines the credibility of all historical figures.

There is no evidence now that this will happen, but it is a risk that belongs at the tail of the list and deserves a check every quarter.

Conclusion

Conclusion: five risks, four outside the company's control and highly correlated. For this kind of position, the only discipline is "do not build a position, or use only a position you can afford to lose entirely." The value of a risk list is not prediction but thinking through in advance: if it happens, what will I do. The answer is already written—do not buy.
