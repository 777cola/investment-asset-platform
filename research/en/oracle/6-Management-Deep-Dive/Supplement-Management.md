## Ellison is still here, and more critical than ever

In 2026 Ellison is 82 and still executive chairman and CTO. He is no figurehead — the OCI roadmap, the OpenAI partnership and the Abilene campus site selection were all driven by him personally. **This is both the biggest asset and the biggest single-point risk.** He holds roughly 40% of the shares, which amounts to a veto over major decisions; no mechanism for the board to constrain management holds against him.

The September 2025 succession arrangement was clever: split the company into "cloud infrastructure" (Magouyrk) and "industry applications" (Sicilia), with the two as co-CEOs, Catz moving to vice chairman to oversee capital markets, and Ellison stepping back to chairman and CTO to oversee strategy. **The problem is that no one in this structure is fully accountable for overall capital allocation** — Magouyrk must win OCI deals, Sicilia must defend margins in industry cloud, and Catz must raise financing; the three are not fully aligned in incentives.

## Capital allocation record: from "buying revenue" to "buying time"

Over the past two decades, Oracle's capital allocation followed one clear throughline: **use free cash flow to buy software assets that were already profitable, then migrate customers onto its own technology stack and raise maintenance fees.** The USD 10.3 billion hostile takeover of PeopleSoft in 2005, the USD 7.4 billion purchase of Sun in 2010 (acquiring Java and hardware), the USD 9.3 billion purchase of NetSuite in 2016, and the USD 28.3 billion purchase of Cerner in 2022. This playbook worked through 2022, with revenue rising from USD 11.8 billion in 2005 to USD 57.4 billion in 2025.

Cerner marks a turning point. The healthcare IT business bought for USD 28.3 billion integrated slower than expected and delivered weak revenue growth; in fiscal 2026 it was folded into industry cloud and is no longer disclosed separately — **ceasing separate disclosure usually means the numbers don't look good.** The new playbook in 2026 is the exact reverse: no longer buying revenue, but borrowing to buy time, deploying USD 90 billion of capex to seize the AI compute window. **This path's return has yet to be validated even once.**

## Integrity and governance: the legal but unseemly parts

Three things belong on the table. First, **license audits**. Oracle has long recovered license fees by auditing customers' use of Oracle software — a contractual right, but also its standard lever for lifting revenue. The customer experience is dire, and for years it has been the most criticized company in CIO circles. Such revenue is legal, but it shows that between "customer success" and "getting paid," this company always chooses the latter.

Second, the **2020 FCPA settlement**. The US SEC found that its Turkey, India and UAE subsidiaries channeled benefits to government officials through intermediaries. Oracle settled for USD 23.2 million, neither admitting nor denying the charges. This is an internal-controls issue, not a strategy issue, but it shows that in fast-growing markets, compliance gives way to closing deals.

Third, **participation in the TikTok US joint venture at the end of 2025 (roughly a 15% stake)**, alongside becoming its cloud and data hosting provider. The political value of this deal far exceeds its financial value: it gives Oracle a position in Washington that others lack. **For shareholders, whether this is a plus or a risk depends on how you view "a tech company doing business with the government."**

## Succession: structurally resolved, substantively not

The co-CEO structure answers "who runs the day-to-day" but not "who sets strategy." Magouyrk is an engineer by background and excels at delivery; Sicilia comes from sales and excels at industry accounts. Neither has Ellison's decisiveness and connections to "change the company's fate with a single USD 300 billion contract."

**The real succession question comes after 2029**: if the AI compute cycle peaks in 2028, the next management team will face USD 100 billion of debt and a stack of data centers not yet fully depreciated. What will be needed then is the ability to "contract," yet this team's track record is all about "expansion." **Handing an oversupply cycle to an expansion-minded team is the most common way such companies die.**
