Core view

Oracle delivered its best-looking quarterly report in history, and also its worst-looking cash flow statement in history. That both happened in the same quarter is what matters most about this company right now.

I. The good half

FY27Q1 revenue was USD 19.345 billion, up 30% YoY; OCI USD 7.388 billion, up 121% YoY; RPO USD 664 billion, up 46% YoY; GAAP net income USD 4.760 billion and EPS USD 1.56. All four beat the consensus. The Abilene campus delivered 131,000 GPUs in a single quarter and completed acceptance in 24 hours—a delivery speed none of the three major cloud providers has achieved.

Look only at this half and the conclusion is: Oracle is the biggest dark horse in this round of AI infrastructure, transformed from a stagnating software company into a compute supplier growing at 30%. That judgment is not wrong.

II. The Ugly Half

In the same quarter, capex was USD 28.499 billion, up 235% YoY, 1.47x revenue. Free cash flow was -USD 5.396 billion, and -USD 23.7 billion for full-year FY26. Total debt is about USD 129.5 billion, debt/EBITDA 4.3x, and S&P downgraded the rating to BBB- in July — one notch above junk. To close the gap, the company completed a USD 20 billion equity offering this quarter, equal to 4.5% of market cap.

In one sentence: it is using money from selling stock and issuing debt to build the data centers that will fulfill those orders. The business itself has not yet begun to generate free cash flow; the bigger it gets, the bigger the gap.

III. How to view these two halves together

The key is that the two businesses are fundamentally different in quality. Database and software support revenue was RMB 5.55 billion, down 3% year on year, with a 90% gross margin and almost no capital expenditure; OCI was RMB 7.388 billion, with a gross margin of around 30%, and every USD 1 of revenue requires USD 1.5 of assets. All the growth comes from the latter, and the latter is a commodity business—customers compare prices, competitors are the three major cloud providers with positive cash flow, and the supplier is Nvidia.

So the story of this company is not "a software company's second growth curve" but "a software company transforming into an IDC." The transformation itself is not shameful; what is shameful is buying IDC assets at a software company's valuation. USD 144.79 corresponds to 13.5x EV/EBITDA. For a heavy-asset company with negative free cash flow and a BBB- rating, this multiple has already priced in the entire AI story.

IV. What would change my view

Two signals; recalculate if either appears: first, free cash flow turns positive in a single quarter and stays positive for two consecutive quarters, which would show expansion has entered the harvest phase; second, the share price falls below USD 100, equivalent to about 9.5x EV/EBITDA — only then do the odds flip.

Until then, the RMB 664 billion order book commands respect, the RMB 129.5 billion of debt warrants caution, and together they add up to one word: "avoid." Demand is real, but shareholders are not the highest-priority party in this deal.

Three sentences for non-specialist readers

If you do not have time to read the full piece, three sentences are enough: First, its orders are real — USD 664 billion, and the customers are the world's richest AI companies. Second, its cash flow is negative — a USD 5.4 billion loss in a single quarter, plugged by borrowing and selling stock. Third, the share price has fallen from 248 to 145 and looks cheap, but on an enterprise value to operating cash flow basis it is more expensive than Microsoft.

Conclusion

Editor's conclusion: avoid. The order story holds; the balance sheet does not. Wait for cash flow, or wait for the price.
