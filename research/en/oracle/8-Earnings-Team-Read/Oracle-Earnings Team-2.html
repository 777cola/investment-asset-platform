Masterclass Close Read (Buffett): Only One of the Three Statements Looks Good — Oracle

The income statement tells a story; the cash flow statement tells the truth
Research date 2026/09/14  ·  Price $144.79 (-3.65%)  ·  Market cap About USD 440 billion  ·  PE(GAAP TTM) Approximately 23.5x
← Back to the hub

Core view

Start with the cash flow statement. Single-quarter operating cash flow came in at RMB 23.1 billion against capex of RMB 28.5 billion, leaving free cash flow negative at RMB 5.4 billion — and that was alongside a RMB 20 billion equity raise. Put those three numbers together and you know what keeps it alive.

Income statement: how much of the RMB 4.76 billion net profit is "borrowed"

Net profit of RMB 4.76 billion and EPS of USD 1.56, up 62.6% YoY — twice the revenue growth rate. The driver is not gross margin expansion — gross margin is in fact falling — but expense control and one-off items. Neither is sustainable.

Depreciation matters more. Capex this quarter was RMB 28.5 billion; amortised over six years, that adds roughly RMB 1.2 billion of depreciation per quarter going forward, against current depreciation of only RMB 1.9 billion per quarter. Part of the cost base is therefore understated on the income statement, and that cost will hit in concentrated fashion in 2028–2030. Buyers of this company are effectively buying an asset whose earnings will be revised down over time.

Balance sheet: what does 4.3x leverage mean?

USD 129.5 billion of debt, 4.3x EBITDA, BBB- rating. My read on that combination: it is one downgrade away from having to live off the capital markets. The asset-heavy businesses Buffett bought (BNSF Railway, BHE Energy) also carried meaningful leverage, but their cash flows were predictable, protected by regulation and locked into fixed rates with very long duration.

Oracle's cash flow depends on its AI customers' willingness to pay, and its duration depends on GPU lifespans. Leverage in both cases, but one is utility leverage and the other is tech-cycle leverage — entirely different risks. That is why I will not assign it a utility valuation just because it "looks like a utility": it lacks a utility's demand stability.

Shareholder returns: the direction is reversed

Oracle was a serial repurchaser over the past decade, leaving shareholder equity razor-thin. In fiscal 2026 the picture reverses: buybacks halt and USD 20 billion of stock is issued. The company is simultaneously maintaining an annualised USD 2.00 dividend (yield about 1.4%) and, with free cash flow negative, that payout is effectively funded with debt.

Paying a dividend with borrowed money is the one thing I least want to see. It signals management will not let the market see that "we cannot even cover the dividend" — a mindset that turns into disaster in a downturn. I would rather it suspend the dividend and preserve cash.

A comparison: IBM in 2011

When I bought IBM in 2011, the thesis rested on three pillars: customers can't switch away, buybacks shrink the share count, and the cloud transition will succeed. Seven years on, the verdict: customers indeed can't switch away, but the new growth went elsewhere, and buybacks merely split a shrinking pie among fewer shareholders. Oracle today looks a lot like IBM back then, except it also carries RMB 129.5 billion of debt. That comparison has made me more skeptical of "transformation stories."

Conclusion

Conclusion: good assets, wrong price. I would pay up for the database business; for OCI I would demand a 50% discount. Together they do not justify a RMB 440 billion market cap. Wait for free cash flow to turn positive, or for the price to fall to a level that reflects the software business alone.
