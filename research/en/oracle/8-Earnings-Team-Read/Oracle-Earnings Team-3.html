Masterclass Close Read (Munger): First Figure Out How It Dies — Oracle

No forecasts, only failure modes

Core view

I will not model 2028 EPS. I ask instead: if this fails, how is it most likely to fail? The answer is clear: customers stop paying, ratings get cut, rents fall, all three at once.

Death knell one: customer concentration

Of the RMB 664 billion in orders, more than half may come from a company that is still loss-making. Structurally, this amounts to: you are using an investment-grade balance sheet to underwrite the credit of a startup. This has happened before in telecom equipment—Lucent and Nortel extended vendor financing to emerging carriers, revenue was recognized, cash was never collected, and in the end both companies were gone.

Death knell two: leverage meets interest rates

RMB 129.5 billion of debt, 4.3x leverage, BBB-. A 100 bp rise in rates adds RMB 1.3 billion a year in interest; a one-notch downgrade adds RMB 2.5–3.5 billion. The company's project returns run roughly 10%–12%, so once its cost of capital reaches 8%, shareholders earn nothing.

The danger of leverage is not its height but its mismatch with asset duration. Debt rolls over every three years; assets depreciate over six years and pay back over twenty. Duration mismatch is the standard path to bankruptcy for every asset-heavy company.

Death mode 3: everyone builds data centers at the same time

Industry-wide capex rises to the USD 700 billion level in 2026, with 2027–2028 the concentrated commissioning period. If demand growth has not accelerated by then, we get "data centers built, customers missing." In a glut, the lowest-cost player survives—and Oracle's cost of capital is 300 basis points above Microsoft's.

Every capital-intensive industry in history has traveled this road: railroads, shipping, fiber optics, shale oil. No exceptions. This time will be no exception either; only the timing is uncertain.

A historical reference: Cisco in 2000

In March 2000, Cisco's market cap reached USD 555 billion, the largest in the world. Its order book stretched two years out, gross margins stood at 60%, and everyone said "internet traffic doubles every year, and Cisco is the toll booth." Traffic did indeed rise tenfold, yet Cisco's stock took twenty years to return to that high. Demand materialized; the valuation did not. Oracle's position today is structurally very similar to Cisco's back then.

Conclusion

Conclusion: stay out. Not because I think it will collapse, but because its survival hinges on a variable it cannot control. A company like this can be traded, not invested in.
