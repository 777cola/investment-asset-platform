Master Class Close Read (Li Lu) — SK hynix

How does a company with a 5% R&D expense ratio hold its ground for a decade?

Core view

Li Lu would read this earnings report over a ten-year horizon: what is its return on reinvestment, and can the moat keep that return in place.

Reinvestment returns: exceptionally high over the past three years, average over ten

From 2021 to 2026, SK hynix's cumulative capex was about KRW 130 trillion, while cumulative operating profit over the same period was about KRW 110 trillion. In other words, every 1 unit invested returned 0.85 units of operating profit—an extremely high return during an upcycle, but unremarkable over a ten-year span, because it includes the huge loss in 2023.

Li Lu is not focused on the return rate over these three years, but on the "reinvestment return rate of the next decade." That depends on two things: whether HBM can sustain its premium, and whether general-purpose DRAM will be driven to commodity pricing by ChangXin. Neither question has an answer yet.

R&D expense: modest as a share of revenue, but focused in the right direction

Within the 7.5% expense ratio, R&D accounts for roughly 5 percentage points (rough estimate, about 12 trillion annualized). That ratio is not high for semiconductors (TSMC around 8%, Nvidia around 10%), but it is reasonable for a memory maker — memory R&D is not about exploring new principles but about process scaling and packaging stacking, which relies on production-line trial and error, with the money spent in capex rather than in R&D expense.

The direction is right: 1c DRAM yield has reached 80%, more than half of capacity will convert to 1c within the year, HBM4 12-layer is in mass production, and MR-MUF packaging is developed in-house. These are all capabilities to "sell the same piece of silicon at a higher price," and they are precisely the source of the moat.

Long-term agreements: the only thing that turns a cyclical stock into a contracted stock

In this earnings report, what Li Lu values most is not profit but the "multi-year supply agreements" the company mentions. If half of 2027 capacity is locked into long-term contracts with price floors and ceilings, the cash-flow volatility of this business will fall markedly, and the valuation framework can move from 6x to 10x—that is the real re-rating case, far more important than HBM price increases.

But long-term contracts have a downside: locked prices mean no upside when prices rise. KIS cut its Q2 forecast on 13 July for exactly this reason—HBM long-term contracts lock in prices, so the company cannot capture the gains from rising commodity DRAM prices. The price of stability is flexibility; this trade-off must be calculated clearly before discussing a re-rating.

Conclusion

Li Lu-style conclusion: this is the memory company that monetizes engineering capability best, but its ten-year return depends on supply discipline, not technology. Long-term agreement coverage is the only structural variable worth tracking.
