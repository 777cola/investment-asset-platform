Core thesis

Four of the seven criteria passed. The three failures all point to the same conclusion: this is a business with mediocre ten-year average returns and extreme volatility. The impressive numbers of the past six months reflect the cycle, not evidence of long-term capability.

The four passes show it is not a bad company: net cash, a five-year revenue CAGR of 47%, no repeated dilution of the share count, and operating cash flow keeping pace with profit. The negative screen concludes that the stock "can go on the watchlist, but cannot be priced as a growth stock."

The three that fell short all point to the same thing.

The 10-year average ROE of 12% sits below the 15% threshold, gross margin has swung from -1% to 79% — an 80-percentage-point range — and cumulative free cash flow over ten years is less than two quarters of 2026 operating cash flow. All three tests fail, but they are not three separate problems: capex is eating this business's long-term returns.

The arithmetic: from 2016 to 2025, SK hynix booked cumulative net profit of about KRW 105 trillion against roughly KRW 130 trillion of capex over the same period. Earnings did not cover fab construction; the gap was funded by borrowing and this ADR offering. Every time the company makes a big profit, it must immediately reinvest it — shareholders receive "the next round of capacity," not cash.

This is not unique to SK hynix; Samsung and Micron are the same. The difference is that SK hynix's capacity is more concentrated in DRAM and HBM, giving it greater elasticity and greater volatility — the KRW 9.1 trillion loss in 2023 is the other side of that elasticity.

The four points it passed show it is not a bad company.

Operating cash flow keeps pace with profit: 2025 operating cash flow was about KRW 50 trillion against net profit of KRW 42.95 trillion, a ratio of 1.2x. Even with a net loss of KRW 9.1 trillion in 2023, operating cash flow stayed positive at around KRW 4 trillion. Heavy depreciation and large non-cash items are hallmarks of a capital-intensive industry and the buffer that lets it ride out cycles.

A clean balance sheet: it swung from net debt to net cash in 2025, and the July ADR raise added USD 26.5 billion (about KRW 37 trillion), putting rough net cash near KRW 95 trillion. Five-year revenue CAGR of 47% is inflationary growth with limited reference value, but at least it shows the company has not fallen behind. Share count is essentially unchanged over five years, with 2.1% cancelled in February — rare among Korean chaebol-affiliated companies.

Is it fatal? No, but it dictates the valuation method

None of the three is fatal. Low ROE is a denominator problem: equity is eroded by losses at the cycle trough and rapidly padded by profits at the peak, which naturally drags the average down. Gross margin volatility is an industry trait, and thin free cash flow is inevitable during capacity expansion.

But they dictate the valuation method: DCF is unusable, PEG is unusable; only cyclical normalized profit times a multiple works. On 2026 operating profit of RMB 23 trillion, any price looks cheap; on 2029 normalized profit of RMB 12 trillion, the current price is over 10x. Which denominator you pick is the investment decision itself.

Three reasons to add it to the watchlist

First, the industry structure is three players plus one chaser, not the five-way melee of a decade ago, and supply discipline is better than in the last cycle. Second, HBM moves the company one step from "standard-product supplier" toward "joint-development partner" — though only one step. Third, valuation is no longer expensive — 17.2x TTM, and a pullback to 1.65 million implies 6x 2027.

But the negative screen only answers "is it worth a look," not "buy now." Whether to buy is the sixth gate; the answer is to wait for July 29.

Why is the threshold set this way?

The seven thresholds are not arbitrary; each answers a question. ROE asks whether the long-term return is sufficient; gross margin, whether pricing power is stable; operating cash flow/net profit, whether profit is just paper wealth; net debt/EBITDA, whether survival will be a problem; revenue growth, whether it has fallen behind; cumulative FCF, what shareholders have actually received; and share dilution, whether management has used shareholders' money to experiment.

The last of the seven is the most easily overlooked. Many cyclical stocks issue equity at the top and buy back at the bottom—the direction reversed—leaving shareholders diluted twice. SK hynix bought back and cancelled shares in February, then issued equity at the July peak, which in a sense also got it half wrong—except the money raised was genuinely invested in capacity, not used to plug a hole.

Conclusion

Four of seven criteria pass. The failures — average ROE, gross margin stability and cumulative free cash flow — all point to the same conclusion: this is a cyclical stock. It belongs on the watchlist, but it cannot be valued as a growth stock. A reasonable entry point is 5–6x normalized 2027 profit, equivalent to KRW 1.40–1.65 million.
