## The three that fell short all point to the same thing.

The 10-year average ROE is 12%, below the 15% threshold; gross margin has ranged from -1% to 79%, an 80-percentage-point swing; cumulative free cash flow over ten years is less than two quarters of 2026 operating cash flow. All three fail, but they are not three problems — they are one: this business's long-term returns are being eaten by capex.

Do the math: from 2016 to 2025, SK hynix booked cumulative net profit of about KRW 105 trillion against roughly KRW 130 trillion of capex over the same period. The money earned was not enough to build fabs; the gap was covered by borrowing and this ADR offering. So every time it makes a big profit, it must immediately invest it — shareholders receive "the next round of capacity," not cash.

This is not unique to SK hynix; Samsung and Micron are the same. The difference is that SK hynix's capacity is more concentrated in DRAM and HBM, giving it greater elasticity and greater volatility — the KRW 9.1 trillion loss in 2023 is the other side of that elasticity.

## The four points it passed show it is not a bad company.

Operating cash flow keeps pace with profit: 2025 operating cash flow was about KRW 50 trillion against net profit of KRW 42.95 trillion, a ratio of 1.2x; even with a net loss of KRW 9.1 trillion in 2023, operating cash flow stayed positive at around KRW 4 trillion. Heavy depreciation and large non-cash items are hallmarks of a capital-intensive industry, and the buffer that lets it ride out cycles.

A clean balance sheet: it swung from net debt to net cash in 2025, and the July ADR raise added USD 26.5 billion (about KRW 37 trillion), putting rough net cash near KRW 95 trillion. Five-year revenue CAGR of 47% is inflationary growth with limited reference value, but at least it shows the company has not fallen behind. Share count is essentially unchanged over five years, with 2.1% cancelled in February — rare among Korean chaebol-affiliated companies.

## But is it fatal: not fatal, but it determines the valuation method

None of the three is fatal. Low ROE stems from the denominator (equity) being eroded by losses at the cycle trough and rapidly padded by profits at the peak, which naturally pulls the average down; gross margin volatility is an industry trait; thin free cash flow is inevitable during capacity expansion.

But they dictate the valuation method: DCF is unusable, PEG is unusable; only cyclical normalized profit times a multiple works. On 2026 operating profit of RMB 23 trillion, any price looks cheap; on 2029 normalized profit of RMB 12 trillion, the current price is over 10x. Which denominator you pick is the investment decision itself.

## Three reasons to add it to the watchlist

First, the industry structure is three players plus one chaser, not the five-way melee of a decade ago; supply discipline is better than in the last cycle. Second, HBM pushes the company one step from "standard-product supplier" toward "joint-development partner" — though only one step. Third, valuation is no longer expensive — 17.2x TTM, and a pullback to 1.65 million implies 6x 2027.

But the negative screen only answers "is it worth a look," not "buy now." Whether to buy is the sixth gate; the answer is to wait for July 29.

## Why is the threshold set this way?

The seven thresholds are not arbitrary; each answers a question: ROE asks "is the long-term return sufficient"; gross margin asks "is pricing power stable"; operating cash flow/net profit asks "is profit just paper wealth"; net debt/EBITDA asks "will survival be a problem"; revenue growth asks "has it fallen behind"; cumulative FCF asks "what have shareholders actually received"; share dilution asks "has management used shareholders' money to experiment".

The most easily overlooked of the seven is the last. Many cyclical stocks issue equity at the top and buy back at the bottom—the direction reversed—and shareholders get diluted twice. SK hynix this time bought back and cancelled shares in February and issued equity at the July peak, which in a sense also got it half wrong—except the money raised was genuinely invested in capacity, not used to plug a hole.
