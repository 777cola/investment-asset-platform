Core view

HBM is not simply "stacking DRAM". Five hurdles stand in the way: stacking, TSV, packaging, thermal management and joint validation with customers. SK hynix leads on yield and packaging, has been caught up on stack layer count, and still holds the edge in joint validation with customers.

First layer: the bare die. 1c is the foundation of cost

HBM is still built on DRAM dies, and the more advanced the process and the smaller the die, the more dies can be cut from a single wafer and the lower the cost. SK hynix's 1c (sixth-generation 10nm-class) yield has reached 80%, and the company plans to shift more than half of its DRAM capacity to it within the year, reaching about 190,000 wafers per month by year-end.

This is the "cost moat": a 10 percentage point yield advantage translates into 10%–15% lower unit cost, which in a price war determines who ends up losing money. Samsung is catching up on 1c and Micron's 1γ is already in mass production; the gap is narrowing but has not disappeared.

Stacking and packaging come second and third — areas where SK hynix has a genuine edge

HBM stacks 8 or 12 DRAM layers vertically, connects them with TSVs (through-silicon vias) and fills the gaps with packaging material. Two things are hard: aligning the stack of thin dies (the thinner the die, the more it warps) and heat dissipation (a 12-layer stack has extremely high heat density).

SK hynix uses its in-house MR-MUF (mass reflow molded underfill), which offers single-pass molding, good thermal conductivity and high yield. Samsung uses TC-NCF (non-conductive film), which requires more process steps and is prone to voids. That is the source of the "12% lower wafer cost," and the reason SK hynix's 12-layer HBM4 yield holds level with the prior generation.

But the packaging edge is not permanent: Samsung resolved the overheating problem in 2026, overtook on qualification progress and passed Nvidia certification in June. The technology gap has narrowed from "one generation" to "half a year."

The fourth moat: customer joint testing — the closest thing to a true moat

HBM is not plug-and-play: it has to be tuned alongside the GPU's packaging substrate, power delivery, firmware and thermal design. Nvidia worked with SK hynix from the definition stage of its Vera Rubin platform, revising more than a dozen versions. This is not a procurement relationship; it is co-development.

How high are switching costs? Full-system validation at the customer takes three to six months, during which no shipments can go out. Nvidia will therefore not easily replace its primary supplier, but it will allocate share to second and third suppliers — Samsung's roughly 20% initial share in 2026 and 28% for the full year reflect exactly this mechanism.

The barrier thus protects share, not price: SK hynix can hold above 50%, but it cannot hold a 3x premium.

The depreciation of moats: how much will remain in three years

Map the three thresholds onto a timeline: a 2-year die advantage (process catch-up), a 1.5-year packaging advantage (Samsung has already closed most of the gap) and a 3-year customer co-validation advantage (switching suppliers carries the highest cost). Taken together, the moat's effective life is about 3 years.

By 2029, in the HBM4E/HBM5 era, the three will be closer on technology and pricing will be closer to cost-plus. SK hynix's operating margin will most likely fall back to 35%–45%, the industry's normal level. Investment implication: the premium paid today can be collected for at most three years; after that, value the business as a normal manufacturer.

A counterexample: Micron's shallower moat makes it more resilient

Micron holds only 18% of HBM share and trails by half a step technologically, yet its stock swung less than SK hynix's in 2026. That is not because it is the better company, but because its "expectations gap" is smaller — the market never valued it as an HBM monopolist, so it fell less when the HBM premium narrowed.

SK hynix's problem is precisely that it is "too good": the market prices it as the "HBM monopolist," so any share loss gets magnified into a lower valuation centre. The wider the moat, the higher the market's expectations, and the steeper the drop when it disappoints. This is the layer most easily overlooked in investing.

Conclusion

Moat conclusion: three and a half of the four thresholds are cleared, giving the moat a shelf life of roughly three years. Over that window the stock warrants a one-notch premium to Samsung and Micron; beyond it, it does not. Any DCF assumption extending past three years is therefore paying for something that does not exist.
