## Tier one: revenue mix — 80% DRAM, 20% NAND, with HBM the profit engine

2026Q1 revenue was 52.58 trillion, split roughly 80% DRAM and 20% NAND (rough estimate). Within DRAM, HBM accounts for around 40%, with the remainder spread across server DDR5, LPDDR5X, GDDR7 and consumer products. Within NAND, enterprise SSDs and high-capacity QLC products are gaining share, and 321-layer products now lead by output share.

The key to the mix shift is not the ratio but capacity allocation: management explicitly states that "capacity is prioritized for HBM and high-density server modules of 128GB and above." In plain terms, the best capacity goes to the most profitable customers, and only the remainder flows to the consumer market. That is why consumer DRAM contract prices can rise 45%–50% while the company's own blended ASP rises only 30% — it ceded the market with the steepest price increases in exchange for the long-term contract stability of HBM.

## Second layer: gross margin at 79%, no upside left in that number

A 79% gross margin means materials, labour, depreciation and electricity together absorb only RMB 21 of every RMB 100 of revenue. Memory's historical gross margin peak was around 55%; this cycle has gone straight to 79%, driven by two factors in combination: the structural premium on HBM (unit price roughly 3x) and a broad shortage of commodity DRAM.

Is there room to go higher? Almost none. Wafer cost is rigid, and a further 10% price increase would lift gross margin only to 81%. To the downside, a 30% price decline would push gross margin below 65% and halve operating profit. The significance of 79% is not "it can go higher" but "it can only go lower".

One more detail: this quarter's depreciation is calculated on the old production lines. Yongin Phase 1 starts production in 2027 and M15X reaches full production by end-2026, so depreciation will step up markedly from 2028. By then, even with prices flat, depreciation will consume 3–5 percentage points of gross margin.

## Tier 3: expense ratio of just 7.5% — the other side of operating leverage

Gross margin 79%, operating margin 71.5% — a gap of just 7.5pp. R&D, sales and G&A combined account for 7.5%, an extremely low expense ratio for semis (TSMC around 12%, Samsung Electronics around 20%). The reason: no brand building, no channel and only a handful of customers, so sales expense is naturally low.

The upside is operating leverage: revenue up 60%, operating profit up 96% (19.17 → 37.61). The downside is the same leverage: revenue down 30%, operating profit down more than 70%. Low expense rigidity means costs are almost entirely variable (materials) and quasi-fixed (depreciation), with no "soft cost" buffer to cut.

Net profit of KRW 40.35 trillion is KRW 2.7 trillion above operating profit, the difference coming from revaluation of equity investments (the indirect Kioxia stake held through a consortium) and tax items. The two measures must be read separately: operating profit measures the business, net profit measures per-share value.

## Tier four: cash flow tracks profit, though it will thin in 2H

Single-quarter free cash flow was RMB 1.867 trillion, up 582% YoY. Capex this quarter came to only about RMB 1 trillion (rough estimate); the full-year pace of RMB 3.8–4.0 trillion implies RMB 1.0–1.1 trillion per quarter over the next three quarters, which would compress free cash flow to around RMB 1 trillion.

The balance sheet holds no hidden concerns: the company turned net cash in 2025, with net cash of about RMB 5.8 trillion in 2026Q1 (rough estimate), rising to around RMB 9.5 trillion after the USD 26.5 billion ADR raise in July, equivalent to 7% of market cap. Inventory and receivables are both rising, but that is normal in a price upcycle—inventory is carried at cost, so market-price gains are not yet reflected on the books, effectively concealing part of profit.

The real risk is not cash-flow quality but cash-flow sustainability: a substantial portion of this quarter's RMB 2.8 trillion in operating cash flow is "passive working-capital appreciation from price increases," not volume growth. Volumes did not grow while cash flow surged; money of this kind reverses the moment the cycle turns.

## Three accounting details beyond the four layers

Detail 1: Inventory valuation. DRAM is carried at the lower of cost or net realisable value. When prices rise, market price exceeds cost but book value is not written up, so part of profit stays hidden; when prices fall, write-downs are required and profit is hit twice. In the 2022–2023 downturn, inventory write-downs were a major driver of widening losses.

Detail 2: FX. Revenue is denominated in USD and costs in KRW. A weaker won translates the same USD revenue into more won and passively lifts margins; appreciation does the opposite. The won was weak in the first half of 2026, an invisible tailwind.

Detail 3: Government grants and tax credits. The Korean government offers tax credits for semiconductor investment and the US CHIPS Act provides subsidies. Both lower the effective tax rate and flatter net profit relative to operating profit. To judge true profitability, look at operating profit.

## A reminder after reading

The easiest way to misread this earnings report is to treat it as evidence that "the company has become stronger." In fact, every record in 2026Q1 decomposes into two factors: price and structure. Price (DRAM +60%, NAND +70%) drove more than 80% of the increment; structure (HBM mix, 1c yield, 321-layer NAND) drove the remaining 20%.

Price will reverse; structure will not. To judge this company's future, therefore, watch the progress of the structural factor—the ramp speed of HBM4, the proportion of 1c capacity conversion, the cost advantage of 321-layer NAND—not how much money it made this quarter.Quarterly profit is the result; structural change is the cause.
