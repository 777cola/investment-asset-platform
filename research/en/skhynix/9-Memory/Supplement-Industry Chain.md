## Upstream: the real bottleneck is ASML's production schedule

Equipment accounts for more than half the cost of a DRAM fab, and EUV lithography is the one link in the chain with no substitute. ASML ships only a few dozen EUV units a year and allocates them by quota; Samsung, TSMC, SK hynix and Micron are all in the queue. SK hynix's 2026 capex is KRW 38–40 trillion, a large share of it for EUV and the accompanying advanced packaging equipment.

Materials are less tight: wafer capacity (Shin-Etsu, SUMCO, SK Siltron) is ample, photoresist and specialty gases have multiple suppliers, and packaging substrates (Ibiden, Unimicron) are tight on AI demand but not a bottleneck. So the upstream constraint on SK hynix is the pace of capacity ramp, not cost — you want to go faster, but you can't, because the machines can't be delivered.

That also explains why 2027 supply growth is only 13% (JPMorgan estimate): not that manufacturers don't want to expand, but that equipment and cleanrooms can't keep up. This is the bull case's strongest argument, and also the bears' easiest point of attack — equipment delivery delays only postpone, they don't cancel.

## Midstream: HBM turns "standard products" into "custom parts"

Commodity DRAM is a standard part: all three suppliers can make the same part number, and customers buy on price. HBM is not. It has to be designed alongside the GPU's packaging, power and thermal envelope, customers are engaged from the product-definition stage, and qualification cycles run for quarters. That shift turned SK hynix from a "supplier" into a "co-development partner" — and it is why the company can command 3x the price.

The flip side of custom parts is lock-in. SK hynix's HBM4 capacity is purpose-built for Nvidia's Vera Rubin, so if Nvidia's roadmap changes — say, a shift toward more in-house design or a different packaging approach — that line is hard to repurpose. This is the real cost of customer concentration: not weak bargaining power, but high asset specificity.

NAND is a different story altogether: five competitors, homogeneous products, and YMTC still adding capacity. SK hynix has carved out an enterprise SSD position through Solidigm, but margins in that business will never catch up with DRAM. The 70% NAND price increase in Q1 2026 came from shortage, not from structure.

## Downstream: demand hinges on a single variable — AI capex

Within SK hynix's downstream demand, AI servers (including HBM) account for more than 40% of DRAM revenue, phones and PCs around 20% each, and consumer and automotive the remainder. Phone and PC shipments have not grown for three consecutive years; the real demand driver is AI — a single AI server requires 3–5x the memory of an ordinary server, and HBM capacity per system keeps rising.

Pricing power across the entire chain therefore ultimately rests on the capex decisions of Nvidia and the four major cloud providers. Nvidia runs a 70% gross margin, and cloud providers are willing to pay a premium for compute; part of that premium flows down to HBM, and part of HBM's share flows further up to equipment. SK hynix ranks third in this distribution chain, behind Nvidia and ASML.

## Cycle position: stage three, the "capacity expansion race," where stocks typically peak

The cycle has run six quarters: inventory destocking through 2H24, a price surge in 2025, and now a wave of capacity expansion announcements for 2026. The current signature: prices still rising but at a decelerating rate (general-purpose DRAM +100% in Q1, +58%–63% in Q2), and margins at record highs with slower QoQ growth (71.5% → brokers estimate 65%).

The 2018 cycle is the template. From Q4 2017 to Q1 2018 the pattern was the same — prices rising, margins at record highs, everyone expanding capacity. The stock peaked in May 2018; prices began falling in October 2018, a five-month gap. Equities peak half a year ahead of prices; this is the historical rule for memory stocks. SK hynix's KRW 2.919 million on June 22 is very likely that peak.

The next observation point is 1H27: Yongin Phase 1 cleanroom commissioning in February, Samsung's HBM4 capacity doubling, and ChangXin's 420,000 wafers/month coming online. With all three supply sources arriving at once, prices will settle the question.

## A counterargument: if a genuine surplus emerges in 2027, who breaks first?

If supply truly overshoots in the second half of 2027, prices will fall in this order: commodity DRAM first (standard products from CXMT, Samsung and Micron), then NAND (five competitors, homogeneous products), and HBM last (three players, long-term agreements, certification barriers).

So SK hynix will not be the first to collapse, but it will be hit hardest — because four-tenths of its profit comes from the HBM premium, and once HBM prices start falling, they drop straight from 3x to 2x, not a slow slide.

This sequence is useful for spotting the inflection point: when commodity DRAM contract prices turn down, HBM has three months left. Both 2018 and 2022 followed this pattern.
