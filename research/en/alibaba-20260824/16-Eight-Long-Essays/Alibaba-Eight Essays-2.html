The nature of the business: three revenue streams, three fates — Alibaba

Core view

Split Alibaba's RMB 268.95 billion quarterly revenue into its parts and three distinct businesses emerge: commission-taking (good gross margin, capital-light), selling compute (mid gross margin, extremely capital-heavy) and selling fulfillment (thin gross margin, scale-dependent). Their fates diverge, and forcing a single multiple across all three is bound to go wrong.

Lesson one: commission-taking, the best business of all

Customer management revenue of RMB 82.55 billion — merchants pay advertising fees and commissions for exposure and transactions. The financial characteristics of this business are near perfect: deferred revenue, zero inventory, zero fulfillment, gross margin above 70%. The more merchants there are and the more transactions they make, the more Alibaba earns, with almost no additional capital required.

But it is losing pricing power. Reported basis -7%, like-for-like +1% — both numbers say the same thing: merchants are unwilling to pay more. The reason is not complicated: traffic went from "only Taobao has it" to "Taobao, Douyin, Pinduoduo and Xiaohongshu all have it." When a resource goes from scarce to abundant, its rent inevitably falls.

That is why the e-commerce group's RMB 39.75 billion EBITA only merits 9x — it is a good dairy cow, but milk output has peaked and the pasture is being divided up by others.

Second: selling compute, the heaviest of them

Revenue of RMB 48.44 billion, EBITA of RMB 5.63 billion, an 11.6% margin. The economics are "build first, lease later": a one-time outlay on 3–5 years of equipment, then rent collected by the hour or by the month. Two key variables: utilisation and unit price.

Utilisation is strong today, with AI demand spilling over and customers queuing for compute. Unit price is firm today, with the whole industry short of cards. When both hold at once, cloud is a money printer (+45% growth, +133% profit). But neither is a steady state. As industry-wide capacity comes online in 2027–2028, utilisation will fall and unit price will loosen, and the margin may then retreat from 11.6% to 8%–9%.

The value of this business lies in scale effects: more customers mean lower unit cost and more room to cut prices to win customers, forming a virtuous cycle. Alibaba Cloud's 38.1% share plus T-Head's in-house chips are the fuel for this cycle. Assigning 6x PS means: acknowledging it is a good business, but not acknowledging it is a perpetual motion machine.

Lesson three: selling fulfillment, the hardest one

Instant retail: RMB 53.30 billion, +45%. The business model: a user places an order, the platform delivers within 30 minutes, and it earns the product markup plus the delivery fee. Costs are riders, front warehouses and shrinkage. Gross margin is 10%–15% and rigid.

Its moat is "fulfillment density" — the denser the orders in a given area, the lower the per-order cost. Meituan has spent ten years building exactly that, with the highest density and the most mature UE (52% share); Taobao Shangou used subsidies to reach roughly 41% share in short order (JD 7%), but that is density bought, not density grown. Whether orders fall away once subsidies stop is the only real question in this business.

So why is Alibaba still in it? Because "delivery in 30 minutes" has become users' default expectation, and without it Taobao would lose its gateway value. This is a defensive war, not an offensive one — so it is destined not to make money, only to lose less.

Lesson four: AI applications are not yet a business

Revenue of RMB 3.34 billion, a loss of RMB 13.86 billion. The model is "users pay nothing, the company pays the electricity bill": the Qwen app only launched paid value-added services in August, and Qwen Office has just been released for enterprises. Globally, OpenAI loses several billion USD a year, Anthropic has just completed the largest IPO in history, and the entire industry is still in the "buying users" phase.

Alibaba's logic here is not "making money" but "owning the gateway": if AI assistants become the next-generation gateway to search and shopping, e-commerce players without a gateway get demoted to "suppliers." So this RMB 13.86 billion is not buying revenue; it is buying a ticket.

But the price of that ticket must be reckoned clearly: RMB 55.4 billion annualised, equivalent to 35% of the e-commerce group's EBITA. That ratio is bearable for two years, not for five. Management has given no timetable, and that is the biggest information gap.

Conclusion

Business quality verdict: Alibaba is a composite of "a good business + a heavy business + a grueling business + a lottery ticket." The good business generates cash (RMB 159 billion/year EBITA), the heavy business consumes it (RMB 270 billion/year capex), the grueling business burns it (instant retail subsidies), and the ticket bets on the future (RMB 55.4 billion/year). Together the four sum to free cash flow of -RMB 44.67 billion. This is structural, not accidental.
