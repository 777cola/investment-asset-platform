## Signal one: the 50% drop on 25 June marked the completion of the valuation regime shift

The stock fell intraday to HKD 90.05 that day, exactly half its October 3, 2025 high of HKD 185.17. Media put it down to "external negatives compounded by a subdued 618." But from a valuation standpoint, what really mattered was that the market had finished re-rating Alibaba from a platform stock to an infrastructure stock.

The evidence: the halving came before the earnings release, when there was no new bad news on fundamentals—fiscal 2026 full-year revenue actually grew 2.74%. The multiple fell, not the performance. From HKD 185 to HKD 90, P/E dropped from the 40x range to the 20x range; the market priced in "negative free cash flow" in advance.

That also explains why the August 20 earnings release failed to lift the stock: the good news on cloud (+45%, 11.6% margin) was already partly anticipated at HKD 90, while the bad news from the placement was new. The market is insensitive to "known good" and acutely sensitive to "unknown bad."

## Signal two: the August 20 earnings report, where the market priced the good and the bad separately

The results themselves were a mixed bag: revenue of RMB 268.95 billion, slightly above the Bloomberg consensus of RMB 268.52 billion, but Non-GAAP net profit of RMB 20.72 billion came in well below the RMB 25.35 billion consensus — an 18% miss. The market's reaction that day was typical: on August 21 the stock opened up more than 2%, reversed to close down 3.09% at around HKD 122.6, while the ADR fell nearly 5% overnight before reversing to close up 1.3%.

This gap-up-then-fade pattern cuts both ways: 1) the bulls have a case — cloud +45%, margin 11.6%, AliExpress turning positive — but could not sustain buying through the full session; 2) the bears have a case too — Non-GAAP 18% below consensus, AI losses of RMB 13.86 billion, a surging tax rate. Neither side won, and the stock settled at HKD 123 — the market's version of a neutral price.

Several major brokers raised ratings after the results: Dongwu maintained Buy, Morgan Stanley named it a top pick and BofA called it one of the most attractive China AI plays. Sell-side looks at cloud, buy-side looks at cash flow — that is where the disagreement lies.

## Signal three: the August 23 placement put "short of cash" in writing

710 million shares at HKD 112.70, HKD 80 billion gross, HKD 79.7 billion net proceeds, 3.47% dilution, 100% into full-stack AI. The deal priced at an 8.4% discount to the August 21 close of HKD 123.0, with a 90-day lock-up and CICC, HSBC, Morgan Stanley and UBS as underwriters. Execution was strong: nearly 3x oversubscribed in under an hour, total subscriptions above HKD 200 billion, with sovereign and long-only funds taking over 40%.

The market, though, sees only one thing: this is the first equity financing since its 2019 Hong Kong secondary listing. For a company long known for buybacks and cancellation, suddenly selling stock with the share price 39% below its high reads as "management thinks current cash is not enough," however solid the rationale. The August 24 drop of 8.54% and the break below the HKD 112.70 placement price express exactly that judgment.

The same day, management bought HKD 120 million (Joseph Tsai 720,000 shares at about 112, Eddie Wu 350,000 shares at about 111.6), equal to 0.15% of the placement size. The purpose is not to support the price but to leave a piece of evidence for the narrative that "management stands with shareholders." The effect was limited: the stock still closed at 112.5 that day.

## Noise checklist: what to ignore

Product launches are noise. The Taobao Qianwen AI shopping assistant (May), the paid Qianwen App service and Qianwen Office (August) mark genuine progress, but their revenue contribution is negligible — AI applications revenue totals just RMB 3.34 billion. To track products, watch call volume and paid conversion rate, not launch events.

Organisational changes are noise. Partner comings and goings (Shao Xiaofeng exits; Xu Hong and Zhou Jingren join) shift the internal power structure, not cash flow. What is genuinely informative is who controls the money and who spends it: with Xu Hong (CFO) and Zhou Jingren (AI) both joining the partnership, decision-making power over capex has become more concentrated.

Management's HKD 120 million share purchase is noise. A 0.15% stake offers no substantive hedge. The price anchor matters, not the amount — they chose to buy at HKD 112. Treat it as a sentiment indicator, not a valuation basis.
