Core view

Li Lu would read this earnings report through the twenty-year narrative of "compute becoming infrastructure." At that scale, the -76% net profit does not matter, nor does the 11.6% cloud margin; what matters is "whether the position bought with this RMB 67.68 billion is still there twenty years from now."

Alibaba is China's only full-stack player across all four layers

Alibaba is the only Chinese company with all four layers—model, chip, cloud and application: Qwen3.8-Max, with 2.4 trillion parameters, open source and 300,000 derivative models; T-Head's fully in-house stack from GPU to network chips, with Zhenwu M890 serving more than 650 external customers; Alibaba Cloud holding 38.1% of China's AI cloud market; and applications spanning Taobao, DingTalk, Amap and Fliggy. Globally, only Google, Amazon and Microsoft have this combination.

That is why Li Lu is willing to tolerate RMB 67.68 billion in quarterly capex: a position in infrastructure is bought outright, and once the window closes it cannot be bought again. In a market where compute demand doubles every year, not investing is the biggest risk.

But a position is not rent. His next question would be: how much are the users of this position willing to pay?

Rent: the true level of Chinese companies' ability to pay

Cloud's quarterly revenue of RMB 48.44 billion annualizes to RMB 193.7 billion, or about USD 26.6 billion. That puts it fourth or fifth among global cloud providers, at just one-tenth of AWS. The gap is not technology but willingness to pay: Chinese enterprises' IT spending as a share of revenue is far below that of US peers, and customers willing to pay a premium for compute cluster in only three industries: internet, financial services and autonomous driving.

In Li Lu's framework, infrastructure returns are ultimately set by "users' ability to pay," not by "technological progress." Railways, electricity and telecom all followed this pattern: the more advanced the technology, the more pricing resembles a utility. If cloud ultimately moves toward utility-style pricing, the 11.6% EBITA margin is a reasonable ceiling, not a starting point.

That judgment carries major valuation implications: if 11.6% is the ceiling, cloud is best valued on EV/EBITDA rather than PS, at 8–10x EBITDA rather than 6x PS (about 52x EBITA). Our use of 6x PS is conservative, and is a mark of respect for that judgment.

Timing: depreciation cycles mismatch investment cycles

The most easily overlooked line in this earnings report is capital expenditure of RMB 67.68 billion. Equipment is depreciated over 5 years while customer contracts run 1–3 years, leaving a 2–4 year mismatch window in between. Li Lu would read this as "funding short-term revenue with long-term assets" — leverage in a demand upcycle, a burden in a downcycle.

He would also endorse management's claim that "AI capital expenditure can be recovered within 2 to 3 years." That claim holds mathematically (an 11.8% static return implies an 8.5-year payback, but 3–4 years once revenue growth is included), provided that "cloud revenue keeps growing 40%+." Translated: the credibility of this investment decision rests entirely on cloud growth.

His verdict on this report would therefore be: right direction, good position, rushed pace. In an industry measured over two decades, covering five years of ground in two carries the cost of violent cash-flow swings and the benefit of a firmer position. That choice suits long-term capital, not capital judged on a quarterly cycle.

Conclusion

Li Lu's verdict: this is a report that trades today's profit for an infrastructure position twenty years out. He buys the direction but sees no urgency on price — the best entry point for an infrastructure stock is never the investment phase, but the six months after it ends, when cash flow turns positive again and the market has yet to reprice it. Those six months have not arrived.
