Management — Alibaba

Core thesis

The division of labour is clear: Joseph Tsai runs capital and external affairs, Eddie Wu runs strategy and technology, Jiang Fan runs e-commerce, and Toby Xu runs the books. Execution since 2023 has been adequate—non-core businesses have been cut, resources concentrated on cloud and AI, and the result is 45% cloud growth at an 11.6% segment margin.

Marks are deducted entirely for capital allocation and communication cadence: a HKD 80 billion placement was launched just two days after the earnings call, with no prior market expectation; the placement price of HKD 112.70 was an 8.4% discount to the August 21 close and 39% below the October 2025 high. Issuing equity at a low point is the ugliest entry on this management team's ledger.

Key person: who actually makes the decisions

Alibaba's decision-making is a "chairman + CEO" dual-core structure, not one man holding sway. Joseph Tsai oversees capital and external affairs, Eddie Wu oversees strategy and technology—a division put in place in 2023 and unchanged for two and a half years. On August 24, 2026, Joseph Tsai bought 720,000 shares (about HKD 80 million) and Eddie Wu bought 350,000 shares (about HKD 40 million). The simultaneous purchases signal a coordinated division of labour and are a gesture in their own right.

Three people actually drive results: Eddie Wu (the capex cadence for cloud and AI), Jiang Fan (e-commerce share and instant-retail subsidies) and Toby Xu (how the losses are framed). Eddie Wu's annual compensation is about RMB 495 million (including equity incentives), among the highest for Hong Kong-listed tech companies—the KPI behind that pay is most likely cloud revenue and AI progress, not free cash flow. Remember: where the incentives are, there the behaviour follows.

The partnership system remains Alibaba's implicit power centre. In the June 2026 reshuffle, Shao Xiaofeng exited and Zhou Jingren and Toby Xu joined, bringing the CFO and the AI scientist into the partnership at the same time—giving "the one who spends" and "the one who controls the purse" equal decision-making seats. That is a balance, but it also means capex discipline rests on these two checking each other rather than on the system.

Capital allocation record: four major deals in four years

① Buybacks and cancellation (2024–2025): positive. Alibaba is one of Hong Kong's most active buyback names, repurchasing and cancelling large amounts over the past two fiscal years — a key support behind the share price recovery from its lows to HKD 185 in 2025. ② Spinoffs and focus (2023–2025): positive. Exiting non-core businesses and concentrating resources on cloud and AI is what delivered today's 45% cloud growth. ③ RMB 380 billion AI infrastructure (from February 2025): to be validated. About RMB 190 billion invested by June 2026, past the halfway mark; annualised cloud EBITA of RMB 22.5 billion implies a static return of 11.8%. ④ HKD 80 billion placement (August 2026): negative. Equity issued at 39% below the high diluted holders by 3.47%, rolling back part of the prior three years' buyback progress.

Two less conspicuous but important items: the roughly USD 800 million investment in Kimi (Moonshot AI) for about a 36% stake (disclosed in the FY2026 annual report), and ownership of its own properties (about 18.7 million square meters). The former is an option on the AI ecosystem; the latter is the other face of asset-heavy expansion — both push in a "heavier" direction.

Taken together, the capital allocation preference of this management team is clear: willing to pay for "position", unwilling to wait for "cash flow". Buybacks mean buying its own stock (when cheap); placements mean selling its own stock (when it needs money). Doing both within 24 months shows decisions are opportunity-driven, not discipline-driven.

Integrity and governance: no blemishes, but habits

No hard integrity issue: no financial fraud, no controlling-shareholder fund appropriation, no related-party transaction scandal. Audit opinions are standard, disclosure is dual-listed in Hong Kong and the US, and the governance framework ranks among the more standardised in Hong Kong stocks — the independent directors include Jerry Yang, Shan Weijian, Irene Lee, Anthony Wu, Kabir Misra and Wan Ling Martello, a solid lineup.

Two "habits" are worth flagging, however. The first is surprise financing. Results were released after market close on 20 August 2026 and the placement was announced on the evening of 23 August, leaving only one trading day in between. The placement itself is compliant (general mandate, no shareholder meeting required), but it hits the secondary market with a sudden dose of information asymmetry — the -8.54% fall on 24 August, breaking below the placement price, was the market voting with its feet. The second is flexibility in disclosure basis. Customer management revenue was -7% as reported and +1% on a like-for-like basis, with both figures in the same announcement; compliant, but it leaves outsiders hard-pressed to judge the true trend.

A provision of about RMB 4.3 billion for the EU fine was booked this quarter, settling a historical compliance issue rather than flagging a new one. Overall judgement: governance passes, communication habits do not; no deduction on integrity, a deduction on communication.

Succession: how many more years can this leadership serve?

Joseph Tsai was born in 1964 and is 62 this year; Eddie Wu was born in 1975 and is 51; Jiang Fan is 40 and Xu Hong is 53. The real question is who comes after Eddie Wu, and right now there is no visible answer. Jiang Fan is the successor candidate for the e-commerce line, but no number-two has surfaced on the cloud and AI main line — Zhou Jingren is the technology lead, not the business lead.

The risk in this structure is not that people will leave but that the roadmap will break. The RMB 380 billion capex plan and the full-stack AI roadmap depend heavily on Eddie Wu's personal judgment. If he hands over around FY2028, the first thing the new CEO faces will be whether to keep investing or pull back — a harder decision than the one today.

From an investment perspective, this is not a current-period variable but a valuation variable: companies with high key-person dependence should command a lower multiple over the long term. This is not deducted in the optimistic scenario, and is one reservation behind my HKD 171 target rather than a higher one.

Management score: execution 8, capital allocation 5, communication 4

Scoring across three items is fairer. Execution 8: lifting cloud growth from 26% to 45% and margin from 7.2% to 11.6%, while carving AI out into a separately accounted unit, is an organisational move that takes courage. Capital allocation 5: the RMB 380 billion points in the right direction, but the pace is too aggressive and it is funded by share issuance rather than operating cash flow, shifting risk onto shareholders. Communication 4: the surprise placement, dual-basis disclosure and the absence of a timeline for narrowing AI losses all make the company hard to model from the outside.

Weighted (execution 40%, capital allocation 40%, communication 20%), the score is 6.2, consistent with the 6/10 management judgment in the six gates. What that score means: you can trust them to run the business, not to refrain from spending.

The operational implication for investors is direct: treat "capex discipline" as a standalone tracking metric, not as a quality of management. Watch the numbers, not the statements: whether quarterly capex decelerates sequentially, whether a second placement appears, whether depreciation growth outpaces revenue growth. If two of those three deteriorate, cut the rating by one notch.

Conclusion

Management combines execution capability with a willingness to spend and poor communication. The "Under Review" rating is in part a verdict on them: a good asset paired with management willing to issue equity at low prices deserves a valuation discount to peers. Track three numbers: QoQ capex, whether there is a second placement, and depreciation growth vs revenue growth.
