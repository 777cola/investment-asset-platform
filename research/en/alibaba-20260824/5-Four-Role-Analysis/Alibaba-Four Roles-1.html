Business analysis (Duan Yongping's perspective) — Alibaba

Who exactly does this business make its money from?

Core view

Alibaba's business has three layers, each with a completely different way of making money: e-commerce commission (RMB 205.9 billion revenue, RMB 39.75 billion EBITA, 19.3% margin), cloud computing power sales (RMB 48.44 billion, RMB 5.63 billion EBITA, 11.6%), and instant retail fulfillment (RMB 53.3 billion, loss-making). The first two layers are good businesses; the third is currently buying share with rent.

Where the money comes from: cash-flow characteristics of the three-layer business

The first layer is Taobao Tmall's customer management revenue of RMB 82.55 billion — merchants pay advertising fees and commissions, paying first and gaining exposure later, and Alibaba ties up almost no capital. This is the only part of the company where "raising prices means making more money," but it is shrinking: reported basis -7%, like-for-like +1%. For a platform with GMV on an USD 800 billion scale, room to lift monetization is already thin.

The second layer is cloud: of RMB 48.44 billion in revenue, AI-related revenue was RMB 12.376 billion, growing triple digits for 12 consecutive quarters. This layer's trait is "invest first, collect rent later": a data center has a 5-year depreciation cycle while customer contracts run 1–3 years, leaving a mismatch window in between. This quarter's EBITA margin of 11.6% shows scale effects have already outweighed depreciation, but this comes at a moment when the whole industry is scrambling for compute, with tight supply and firm pricing.

The third layer is instant retail, with RMB 53.3 billion in revenue, +45%, of which Taobao Shangou used subsidies to capture roughly a 40% share of order volume. The economics of this business have yet to prove out: fulfillment costs are rigid, average order value is low, and rivals' unit economics are more mature (Meituan holds 52% share). Share bought with subsidies must be repriced the moment subsidies stop.

Will it still be here in ten years: three certainties, one uncertainty

Three things are certain: Chinese consumers will keep shopping online, and Taobao's transaction mindshare will not disappear within a decade (88VIP's 64 million members, growing double digits year on year, are the hardest evidence); enterprises will keep buying compute, and China's AI cloud is more concentrated than e-commerce, with the top three taking most of the share; Alibaba will not run short of money, with RMB 474.51 billion in cash and liquid investments as its backing.

One thing is uncertain: whether Alibaba can still defend its pricing power in e-commerce. Pinduoduo's low prices, Douyin's content commerce and instant retail's speed are squeezing monetization on three fronts at once. A decade from now Taobao will most likely still be here, but it may become "a public utility that cannot raise prices" rather than "a cash machine".

The Duan Yongping-style judgment: a business still being here in ten years does not mean it will still be profitable in ten years. What you want to buy is "still here and still profitable", so what really needs tracking is monetization rate, not GMV.

The fundamental change in the business: from asset-light to asset-heavy

This is the most important sentence of this issue: Alibaba's valuation framework is shifting from a "platform stock" to an "infrastructure stock." Platform stocks are priced on discounted free cash flow; infrastructure stocks on EBITDA multiples plus the capex cycle. During the transition, the same company gets pulled back and forth by two sets of standards, and the share price swings more than the fundamentals—from HKD 185.1 to HKD 112.5, a 39% drawdown, half of which stems from the valuation-framework shift, not from deteriorating results.

The cost of this shift is clear: FY2026 revenue growth of 2.74% and an operating margin of 6.2%, both worse than three years ago. The payoff is equally clear: cloud growth of 45% at an 11.6% margin, something that did not exist three years ago. The problem is time—asset-heavy returns take three to five years to materialize, while the market demands answers every quarter.

So the nature of this business has not worsened; it has become heavier. A heavier business demands a cheaper price, because its margin for error is smaller: one misstep in capex means a three-to-five-year depreciation burden.

The parts I understand and the parts I don't

Understandable: e-commerce revenue, cloud revenue, capex, depreciation policy, competitive landscape. All of these can be tracked with public data, with little margin of error.

Not understandable: what the RMB 3.34 billion of revenue and RMB 13.86 billion of losses in AI labs and applications are actually buying. The company says "Qwen App inference costs are rising"; the Qwen App launched paid value-added services in August, and Qwen Office targets enterprises. These are all real products, but none of them allows a clear LTV/CAC calculation. A quarterly loss of RMB 13.86 billion means burning RMB 4.15 for every RMB 1 of revenue—a ratio seen in internet history only during the wildest days of community group buying and long-form video.

Duan Yongping's approach is simple: value what you don't understand at zero, or even at a negative value. This report assigns a negative value at 3x annualized losses (-RMB 166.2 billion), which already assumes "things will get better." At a zero valuation, the neutral target price would be HKD 7 higher; at 5x losses, HKD 12 lower. That is how the range is derived.

Conclusion

The business itself is not broken; it has become heavier, slower, and more dependent on capital-expenditure discipline. Duan Yongping's conclusion would be: this is a company you can understand but must wait on — wait for the AI economics to become clear, and for cloud growth to be confirmed by a second quarter. The current price of HKD 112.5 is not "expensive"; it is "not cheap enough to ignore the uncertainty."
