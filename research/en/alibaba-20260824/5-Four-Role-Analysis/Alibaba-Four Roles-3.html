Industry Research (Munger's Lens) — Alibaba

E-commerce and cloud computing are its two legs, and each faces one lethal rival

Core view

Alibaba's two main battlegrounds have completely different competitive dynamics. E-commerce is a zero-sum game: Taobao Tmall, Pinduoduo and Douyin carve up the market between them, and Alibaba defends share at the expense of monetisation rate; AI cloud is incremental expansion: Alibaba Cloud ranks first with a 38.1% share, but Volcano Engine, Tencent Cloud and Huawei Cloud are all stepping up investment, and they are chasing the same pool of enterprise customers.

E-commerce: a defensive battle with no winners

Incremental growth in Chinese e-commerce is over. Alibaba's customer management revenue was flat (reported -7%, like-for-like +1%) as Pinduoduo moved price-sensitive shoppers over in bulk on low prices and Douyin captured the "browsing" occasion through content. All three are still alive, but all three are seeing margins trend down. It is a classic stock game: whoever cuts prices loses profit, whoever doesn't loses share.

Instant retail is the new battleground in this defensive war, and Alibaba picked the fight. Taobao Shangou used subsidies to take roughly 40% unit-volume share, at the cost of instant retail thinning group gross margin and pushing up fulfillment costs. Munger would ask one question: if the subsidies stop, how much of that 40% share remains? Meituan's answer is "more mature UE, smaller per-order loss"; Alibaba's is "traffic sits on Taobao, so there is no need to buy volume separately." Both could be right, but only the day subsidies stop will tell.

AI cloud: an incremental market, but a capital-intensive one

The one piece of good news: AI cloud is incremental. China's AI cloud market is still expanding rapidly in 2026, and Alibaba Cloud leads with a 38.1% share. But three features would make Munger uncomfortable: 1) customer concentration — large model companies, internet platforms and government/enterprise clients are all buyers with strong bargaining power; 2) commoditization — compute itself carries no brand, and customers compare on price and supply stability; 3) no cap on capex — anyone can buy GPUs; the only difference is who can afford to buy more.

Alibaba's real differentiation lies in chips: T-Head develops its full stack in-house, from GPUs to network chips, and Zhenwu M890 already serves more than 650 external customers across over 20 industries. That is a hard advantage while Nvidia remains restricted — not because performance is stronger, but because "being able to buy" is itself a barrier.

Munger would add, though: a barrier built on "being able to buy" disappears the day supply recovers. The shelf life of this moat therefore depends on how long export controls last, not on Alibaba's efforts.

Alibaba sits in the upswing of its capex cycle

Position Alibaba within the industry cycle: e-commerce at the tail end of maturity, instant retail mid-way through the subsidy war, AI cloud on the upswing of the capex cycle, with everyone still scrambling for compute, prices firm and demand overflowing. The upside of the upswing is good-looking revenue; the downside is depreciation that arrives later. Single-quarter investment of RMB 67.68 billion, depreciated over 5 years, adds RMB 54 billion of cost per year five years out—equivalent to 86% of FY2026 operating profit (RMB 62.98 billion).

The industry-level comparison is clear: Tencent's 2026Q2 capex was RMB 52.8 billion, +176% YoY, also on the same upswing, while the four major North American cloud players sharply raised 2026 capex. Everyone expanding capacity at once usually means oversupply and price wars two to three years later. If cloud pricing starts to loosen in 2027–2028, that is the signal this cycle has peaked.

Munger-style multi-factor checklist

Fighting three battles at once — defending e-commerce share, grabbing instant-retail share and securing AI cloud capacity — requires three pools of cash. Alibaba's answer is to bleed e-commerce dry and issue new shares on top. That works in an upcycle; in a downcycle it takes three hits at once. This is a classic "inverse Lollapalooza": three negative factors reinforcing each other rather than offsetting each other.

The positives stack up too: AI demand spillover, in-house chip supply, 88VIP member stickiness and RMB 474.5 billion in cash. Two of the three are externally given (demand, export controls); only one is self-built (members and use cases). Success that rests on external factors isn't a business model, it's luck.

Munger's verdict would be that the odds here depend on capex discipline, not on the AI narrative. Management that can restrain capex deserves a premium; management that treats capex as a trophy deserves a discount.

Conclusion

Industry position good, competitive position average. Alibaba is a top-tier player in both battlegrounds, but neither has a winner-takes-all structure. The result is decent revenue growth, mediocre margins and perpetual price wars. For a company like this, the multiple should sit below a pure platform (Tencent) and above pure hardware (server makers); 6x PS roughly maps to that position—consistent with the neutral-scenario assumption.
