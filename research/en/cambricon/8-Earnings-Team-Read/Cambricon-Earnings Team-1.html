Core view

Duan Yongping reads financial reports for only three things: whether revenue is real, whether gross margin holds, and whether the cash ultimately returns to the books. Cambricon passes the first two; it fails the third.

Revenue is real because customers are paying advances.

Contract liabilities rose from RMB 610,000 at the start of the year to RMB 188 million — a figure that says more than the revenue growth rate: customers are willing to pay first and wait for delivery, which shows demand is not fabricated. Revenue was RMB 5.996 billion, up 108.13% year on year; non-recurring net profit was RMB 2.166 billion, 93.7% of net profit, so profits did not come from asset sales. On these two points, I'm convinced.

But the RMB 188 million of advance receipts covers only 2.3% of RMB 8.248 billion in inventory. The company funds the rest itself. A business that makes suppliers collect first while it collects later is essentially financing others. Duan Yongping would say: this isn't a bad business, it's a "heavy business."

Gross profit held. That is the most valuable line.

55.25%, down only 0.68 points year on year, and up 1.77 points quarter on quarter in Q2 alone. In a year when domestic chips are engaged in a price war, holding 55% shows that what it sells is not a standard product but "the certainty of being able to get supply." Gross margin is the thermometer of business quality, and this temperature is normal.

What bears watching is the sustainability of this number. It comes from supply scarcity (the Entity List + SMIC capacity allocation), not from a technology gap. Once supply loosens, 55% will drift toward 45%. This is not alarmism; it is the four-decade-old rule of the semiconductor industry.

The money hasn't come back, so I won't buy.

Net profit RMB 2.311 billion, operating cash flow RMB 311 million, a ratio of 0.135. Duan Yongping's threshold is 0.7 or above to count as "actually making money." The shortfall sits in inventory (RMB 8.248 billion) and prepayments (RMB 2.914 billion). He has said profit can be adjusted; cash flow is hard to adjust. This report shows that directly.

He would also ask: for the money this business earns ten years from now, does it need to front capital like this every year? If the answer is "yes," its free cash flow will never improve, and valuation can only rest on a growth story. Holding up is luck; not holding up is common sense.

Conclusion

Duan Yongping-style conclusion: Real revenue, stable gross margin, poor cash. This is a good business that "requires constant cash float"—worthy of respect, but not worth buying at a 218x P/E. Wait until cash content returns above 0.5 before discussing price.
