## Duan Yongping: Who ultimately pockets the money from this business?

Duan Yongping would first offer a fair point: AI chips are one of the most certain tracks of the next decade, and Cambricon's position in domestic substitution is scarce — that is not in dispute. Then he would turn to the line he cares about most — "the essence of a business is whether it can keep generating cash."

Look at the numbers: first-half net profit of RMB 2.311 billion, operating cash flow of RMB 311 million, inventories up RMB 3.304 billion, prepayments of RMB 2.914 billion. Where did the profit go? Stuck in the warehouse and with suppliers. He would say this business could be great, but you cannot pay 218x PE for profit that has not yet turned into cash. All his life he has talked about "benfen" — doing one's duty. Benfen means not making money you do not understand — a 218x PE requires you to understand 2028, and that is not benfen.

He would also ask something more basic: will this business earn several times more a decade from now than it does today? If the answer is "I don't know," the price must leave room for error. RMB 1,156 leaves no room.

## Buffett: the moat is in the water, the cash flow is on the shore

Buffett would ask three questions. Why don't customers switch? Because switching suppliers means rewriting operators and retuning performance, and the adaptation cost between BANG and Neuware is real—this moat exists, but it is far shallower than CUDA's. Can the product raise prices? Gross margin of 55.25%, up another 1.77 points quarter on quarter in Q2, suggests it can for now. Is free cash flow good? This one fails: cash flow of RMB 311 million against net profit of RMB 2.311 billion means the company earned no cash.

His stance on semiconductors has always been cautious: fast technology cycles, capital intensity, customer concentration. He bought TSMC and later sold it, on the grounds that "this is not a business you can hold comfortably for twenty years"; Cambricon's certainty is a notch lower than TSMC's—TSMC is the foundry for everyone, while Cambricon must fight Huawei for the same customers.

He would offer a balanced verdict: this is a company with a moat but no water in it. A moat keeps out new entrants, but it does not stop customers from wanting a second supplier, nor does it stop valuation from reverting.

## Munger: invert the question — under what circumstances would it fall 60%?

Munger does not forecast upside; he only lists the ways to die. First, if Huawei's Ascend opens up capacity and fills out its ecosystem, customers will have no incentive to keep a second supplier — Ascend has cloud, policy resources and full-stack solutions; Cambricon has only a single card. Second, if cloud providers slow capex by one quarter, orders are recognized later; Q2's 7.84% sequential decline was already a preview. Third, a 10% writedown on RMB 8.2 billion of inventory would wipe RMB 820 million off profit, cutting half-year earnings by 30%.

The fourth is the easiest: market style rotates from the "AI narrative" back to "cash flow," and the multiple goes from 218x back to 80x. This one requires the company to do nothing wrong — only for capital to change direction. From June 30 to August 17, the stock fell from RMB 1,595.55 to RMB 1,156, a 27.6% drawdown, and the P/E compressed from 369x to 218x — the market has already run this playbook once.

His conclusion would be harsh: "This isn't investing; this is pricing with imagination." Imagination can last a long time, but it pays no dividend and buys back no stock.

## Li Lu: Industry value does not equal shareholder value

Li Lu would give it the highest industry assessment. Compute power is the electricity of this technology revolution, and China must have its own supply; after the US Entity List and TSMC's supply cutoff, Cambricon is the only company besides Huawei with scaled products and real CSP deployments. That position is valuable on a ten-year horizon and hard to replace—SMIC's N+2 capacity is limited, and few design companies can secure an allocation.

But he would immediately separate two concepts: industry value depends on how many problems it can solve for the nation; investment value depends on how much revenue it can convert into free cash flow. The former is fully priced in (75.6x P/S); the latter has no evidence yet (cash content 0.135).

He would also flag one thing: China's tech industrial policy excels at "building industries up," not at "making shareholders money." Panels, solar, and LEDs are all precedents—the industry won, shareholders did not. For Cambricon to prove it is the exception, it must first prove two things: gross margin does not collapse, and cash collection accelerates. Both are harder than the "compute gap."

## Three scenarios: how the neutral case of RMB 830 is derived

The base case assumes 2027 revenue of RMB 17.5 billion—1.3x the 2026 incentive target of RMB 13.5 billion, and below the RMB 27 billion implied by working backwards from the incentive assessment line.Why the discount?Because Q2 growth was only 7.84% QoQ; to hit RMB 13.5 billion for the full year, H2 must deliver RMB 7.5 billion, up 25% QoQ; doubling again to RMB 27 billion the following year requires capacity, HBM and customers to all break right simultaneously—a low-probability outcome. RMB 17.5 billion is the "smooth but unspectacular" path.

At a 37% net margin, net profit is RMB 6.5 billion; applying 80x (roughly 0.5x PEG for 2027, neutral among A-share growth stocks) gives a market cap of RMB 520 billion, or RMB 827 per share, rounded to RMB 830. Bear case: RMB 13 billion revenue, RMB 4.2 billion net profit, 60x, RMB 400 per share; bull case: RMB 22 billion, RMB 8.5 billion, 110x, RMB 1,450 per share.

One sensitivity must be spelled out:if the incentive assessment line is fully met (2027 revenue of RMB 27 billion), 80x implies over RMB 1,300 per share. In other words, the current price already embeds "full achievement of the assessment target." I do not rule that out, but treating it as the base case means treating the company's KPI as your own margin of safety.

## Odds assessment: 20% upside, 60% downside

Put the three scenarios side by side: bull +25%, base −28%, bear −65%. Weighted 25/50/25, expected return is 6.25 − 14 − 16.25 = −24%. Even raising the bull probability to 40% and cutting the bear to 20%, expected return is still −13%.

Only two paths turn this trade into positive expectancy: the price falls below RMB 800 (the base case alone then offers 4% upside), or 2027 revenue actually reaches RMB 23.6 billion (what the current price's 80x multiple requires). The first is waited for, the second is worked for — neither requires buying now.

The only defense is that "the scarcity premium can persist long term" — the A-share market does pay for "the only one," and the halo of being the STAR Market's first trillion-market-cap stock is not handed out for free. But the premium holds only if growth does not stall, and Q2's 7.84% sequential growth already signals a downshift. The premium is rented, not bought.

One more angle for self-check: if you bought around RMB 200 in 2024, what would you do now? Most likely trim rather than add — because the fivefold gain in eighteen months came from valuation, not earnings. A trade that makes money on valuation always exits on "the valuation has been realized," not on "the company has deteriorated." That is the situation at this level: whoever takes the baton bears the full time cost after the valuation is realized.
