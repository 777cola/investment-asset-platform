Business analysis: what kind of business is selling cards? — Cambricon

Core view

The business in essence: fabless, hand-designed chips go to foundries, and the resulting cards are sold to a handful of large customers, earning a "scarcity rent." It builds no fabs, makes no systems and touches no operations, so it looks asset-light; in reality, however, it is squeezed by both upstream and downstream at once.

Business model: one card, two customer types, three charging methods

Cambricon does one thing: designs cloud AI chips (Siyuan 590/690), turns them into accelerator cards, and sells them to customers. Three revenue models — selling cards (primary), selling IP licensing (legacy business, now marginalized), and selling software and technical services (a very small share). Of the RMB 5.996 billion in revenue in the first half of 2026, accelerator cards were the overwhelming majority.

There are only two customer types. The first is internet cloud providers, which buy them to run large-model training and inference — high volume, demanding requirements, strong bargaining power. The second is telecom operators and local intelligent-computing centers, which buy them to build computing infrastructure — slow decision-making, high unit prices, heavily policy-driven. What both customer types share: neither is an end consumer; both are "intermediary buyers." The intermediary buyer's money comes from its own capex budget.

Unit economics: how much profit per card

Public sources put SiYuan 590 on a 7nm process with 256 TOPS FP16 — roughly 70% of Nvidia A100 performance at about 60% of its cost. SiYuan 690, which enters mass production in early 2026, uses a dual-die chiplet design with HBM3 and more than doubles FP16 throughput versus the 590. On first-half revenue of RMB 5.996 billion and a 55.25% gross margin, every RMB 100 of revenue carries RMB 55 of gross profit and RMB 38 of net profit.

But that RMB 38 is not free cash flow. Inventories rose RMB 3.304 billion and prepayments RMB 2.914 billion — every RMB 100 of revenue earned therefore requires more than RMB 100 paid upfront in materials and capacity deposits. As Duan Yongping put it, this is not "asset-light" but "working-capital-heavy." The plant is light; the cash is heavy.

Bargaining power: strong versus downstream, negligible versus upstream

Downstream, it has pricing power. A 55.25% gross margin, up a further 1.77pp QoQ in Q2, shows customers accept its price—unable to secure Ascend capacity and unable to buy Nvidia's top-end parts, they see it as "the only second option still able to supply."

Upstream, it has almost no bargaining power. Wafers must queue for SMIC N+2, HBM depends on allocations from SK hynix, Samsung and ChangXin, and advanced packaging capacity is tight. Its response is to buy certainty with cash: prepayments of RMB 2.914 billion (+291.37%), inventory of RMB 8.248 billion. That is the entire playbook for a fabless company in a year of capacity shortage.

The real structure of this business, then: downstream gives it 55% gross margin, upstream takes its cash flow. The difference in between ends up as wafers in the warehouse.

Ten years out, the model shifts from "selling cards" to "selling compute"

Two possibilities. First, it remains a chip supplier, grows revenue to RMB 30–50 billion and sees gross margin fall back to 45%–50%, becoming "China's second-tier Nvidia." Second, it moves downstream into complete systems, intelligent computing clusters or even compute leasing—a different business with lower margins and heavier capital, but stickier customers.

On the destinations of its 2025 private placement (RMB 2.054 billion for the chip platform, RMB 1.452 billion for the software platform), the company has chosen an enhanced version of the first path: build chips and the software stack together in depth, and lock in customers through the ecosystem. The path is right, but slow. Duan Yongping would add: slow businesses must be bought cheap; only fast businesses deserve expensive prices.

One easily overlooked fact: its revenue contains almost no "service fees." Mature chip companies (Nvidia, Broadcom) derive a substantial share of revenue from software licensing and technical support, with higher margins and better cash flow. Cambricon's software platform is currently a cost centre, not a profit centre. This means its 55% gross margin is already near the ceiling—unless software starts being charged separately, mix improvement will struggle to push it higher.

Conclusion

Business conclusion: A chip business defined by high gross margin, heavy turnover and pressure from both ends. On the plus side, scarcity supports a 55% gross margin; on the minus side, capacity ties up cash flow. The Duan Yongping-style verdict: acceptable business, unacceptable price.
