Core view

A moat is not a yes-or-no question: it is which one, how deep, and who it keeps out. Cambricon has five candidates, and only one genuinely keeps rivals out.

The hard one: access licence (9/10)

The December 15, 2022 entity list, TSMC's 7nm supply cutoff and SMIC's allocation quota together created a "licence market" in which no more than five design companies can secure advanced process nodes and no more than two can produce training cards at scale.

The nature of this moat must be seen clearly: it is not a technology barrier but an institutional one.An institutional barrier has a defining trait: as long as the rules stay unchanged, it is extremely solid; the moment the rules change, it vanishes overnight. It therefore keeps out new entrants but cannot withstand a policy shift. That is also why "US-China détente" is a negative for it.
Four sources of moat: switching costs, brand, cost, ecosystem
Switching costs 5/10: The BANG instruction set and Neuware already support DeepSeek, Qwen, Hunyuan, GLM, Kimi and MiniMax, though migrating customers must rewrite operators and retune performance. CUDA's advantages—"complete documentation, easy hiring, mature tuning tools"—are precisely where Cambricon trails on all three. Brand 6/10: Top-tier in capital markets (the first STAR Market stock to reach a RMB 1 trillion market cap), average among engineers. Cost 4/10: Fabless, sub-scale versus Huawei, no procurement discounts. Network effects 3/10: The weakest developer ecosystem—ecosystem is the endgame moat in chips, and it is precisely Cambricon's thinnest link.
A moat stops some competitors, not others

What it can block: new entrants (unable to secure capacity and quotas) and small-customer churn (high migration costs). What it cannot block: direct competition from Huawei Ascend (larger capacity, more complete ecosystem), cloud providers' in-house chips (ByteDance and Alibaba are both doing it), or valuation mean reversion (a moat has nothing to do with the P/E ratio).

That explains the 27.6% decline from June 30 to August 17: the moat did not change, yet the stock fell. The moat determines whether a company survives; valuation determines whether shareholders make money. The two are often conflated.

How long can the 55% gross margin hold: a path to reversion

Treat gross margin as a "quota rent" and model it forward: 55.25% in 2026, 55.15% in 2025, and higher before 2024 (low base, few products). It has barely moved in three years, which means the rent is still being collected. But if the supply landscape shifts, the rent will decay along the path below.

Phase one (2026–2027): Ascend capacity stays tight and Nvidia remains constrained, so gross margin holds at 53%–56%. Phase two (2027–2028): SMIC expands capacity, CXMT's HBM ramps, and new players such as Moore Threads scale up, pushing gross margin down to 48%–52%. Phase three (after 2029): domestic training chips enter full competition, gross margin converges toward 45%, approaching Hygon's current level of below 55%. The valuation center must step down in every phase.

Implication for investors: do not extrapolate 2028 profits from today's 55% gross margin. In my base case I use a 37% net margin (corresponding to about 52% gross margin), which already applies a discount; extrapolating from a 55% gross margin would put the target price more than 20% higher—that is the truly dangerous assumption.

Why Customers Don't Switch: Sizing Real-World Migration Costs

Take a cloud provider moving a thousand-card cluster from Ascend to Cambricon. Model code needs operator changes (tens of person-months), performance needs retuning (several months), the ops toolchain needs rebuilding and the team needs retraining. On a rough estimate, the one-time cost equates to 3–6 months of compute budget.

That is the true scale of switching costs—not low, but not unbearable. Customers therefore typically run dual suppliers: Ascend as primary, Cambricon as backup, on a 70%/30% split. Hence Cambricon's ceiling is that of a second supplier, not a first.

Conclusion

Moat conclusion: 6/10. One hard institutional moat plus one moderate switching cost, with the rest weak. That is enough for the company to survive and defend a 55% gross margin, but not to justify a 218x P/E.
