② Business essence: one card, two customer types, three gates — Cambricon

Core view

To reduce Cambricon to a business, you need only answer four questions: what it sells, to whom, how much it earns, and who holds it back.

What is sold: accelerator cards, not chips

What it sells is "accelerator cards that can be plugged into servers to run large models," centered on the Siyuan series: Siyuan 590 (7nm, FP16 256 TOPS, roughly 70% of A100 performance at about 60% of the cost) and Siyuan 690 (mass production in early 2026, dual-die Chiplet, HBM3, compute power more than double the 590). Customers are not buying bare dies; they are buying a combination of "card + driver + software stack."

That combination determines its cost structure: the chip accounts for the largest share, HBM next, packaging and board next, and software is pure investment with no direct revenue. Its gross margin ceiling is therefore set by procurement prices for "chip + HBM," not by R&D spending — this is the fate of a Fabless company: gross margin depends on upstream suppliers.

Who to sell to: three buyers, each stronger than the last

Internet cloud providers (ByteDance, Alibaba, Tencent, Baidu) buy to run training and inference, with large volumes and demanding requirements; telecom operators and local intelligent computing centers buy to build computing power bases, with slow decision-making and policy influence; industry customers (financial, energy, government) buy to run vertical models, at small scale.

In 2025, the top five customers accounted for RMB 5.76 billion in sales, or 88.66%; in Q1 2025, a single large customer once accounted for 96.48%. This is not "customer concentration" — it is another way of saying "only one customer." The upside is large orders and simple delivery; the downside is that bargaining power sits with the other side, and one customer's acceptance schedule can determine a quarter's financials.

How much it earns: per RMB 100 of revenue, RMB 55 gross profit, RMB 38 net profit, and only RMB 13 in cash

First half of 2026: revenue RMB 5.996 billion, gross profit RMB 3.313 billion (55.25%), net profit RMB 2.311 billion (38.54%), operating cash flow RMB 311 million (13.5% of net profit). Three numbers map to three layers of reality: pricing power in the product, scale effects in expenses, and cash taken by upstream suppliers.

Why so little cash? Inventory +RMB 3.304 billion, prepayments +RMB 2.17 billion (+291.37% YoY). It has staked the money it earned, together with the money it borrowed, on wafers and HBM. This is no operating misstep but an inevitable choice in a year of capacity shortage — without the bet, no supply; without supply, no revenue next year.

Bottlenecked by: foundry, HBM, packaging

Wafers: SMIC N+2, capacity prioritized for Huawei, Cambricon queuing. HBM: led by SK hynix, Samsung and Micron; ChangXin just ramping, supply short of demand in 2026. Packaging: dual-die requires 2.5D, TSMC CoWoS essentially closed to mainland China, domestic alternatives still climbing the ramp.

Three gates, each controlled by someone else.That is why its gross margin is high and its cash flow poor—high gross margin comes from downstream scarcity, poor cash flow from upstream queuing. The essence of the business is to be a middleman between these two ends: profitable, but cashless.
Procurement decision chain: how a card gets selected

Real procurement is not about "comparing specs" but about four gates: can you buy it (supply), will it run (ecosystem), who maintains it (service), and how much does it cost (price). Cambricon is weak on two of the first three gates.

It clears the supply gate — this is its biggest advantage, since customers' second supplier must have inventory. It barely clears the ecosystem gate — mainstream open-source models have all been adapted, but customers must migrate their own legacy code, a heavy engineering lift. It trails Huawei on service (Huawei has local teams and full-system partners), and it has no edge on price (55% gross margin means it is not cheap).

So it usually wins deals because "the customer needs a non-Huawei backup," not because "the customer thinks it is better." This means its orders are "allocated," not "won" — allocated orders are stable but carry no pricing power. This is the commercial root of its 88.66% customer concentration.
The shape of things ten years out: selling chips or selling compute

Two endgames. The first: it becomes "China's second-tier Nvidia" — revenue of RMB 30–50 billion, gross margin of 45%–50%, collecting rent off its ecosystem. The second: it turns into a "compute supplier" — selling complete systems, selling clusters, even selling compute services, with lower gross margin but stronger customer stickiness.

Judging by the 2025 private placement allocation, the company has chosen an enhanced version of the first path (RMB 2.054 billion for chips + RMB 1.452 billion for software). The ceiling on this path is Nvidia, the floor is Hygon — whether it reaches the upper limit depends on the ecosystem, not on the chip itself. And the ecosystem is precisely its weakest link.

Conclusion

Business conclusion: A chip business with "high gross margins, heavy turnover, and pressure from both ends." Its moat lies in licenses, not technology; its cash flow sits upstream, not in its own hands. Understand this, and you will not value it using the framework for consumer companies.
