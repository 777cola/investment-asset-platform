## Gate one and gate two: why this business is easy to understand, and where its strengths lie

It scores 9 on the circle-of-competence test because its revenue structure is simple enough to state in one sentence: charge users in more than 190 countries a monthly fee, sell pre-roll ads to advertisers, and use that money to buy content. No inventory, no receivables cycle, no commodity prices, and no reliance on government subsidies. To judge where it will be in ten years, you need only judge two things: whether people still watch television, and whether they are willing to pay for it every month.

The score on the second test comes from the Q3 numbers. Revenue of USD 12.874 billion, up 11.8% year on year; operating profit of USD 4.459 billion, an operating margin of 34.6%, 6.4 percentage points higher than a year earlier and above the company's own guidance. Full-year guidance is 31.5%, another step up from 29.5% in 2025. It is the only company in the media industry to have lifted its operating margin for three consecutive years.

The mechanism behind this leverage deserves spelling out: the production cost of a series is fixed, so it costs the same whether 10 million people watch it or 100 million. The more subscribers, the lower the unit content cost and the higher the margin. Netflix now reaches close to 1 billion viewers, and this leverage has already run through its steepest stretch — margin expansion will slow each year from here, +2pct in 2026 and only about +1pct in 2027. That is not bad news; it is what maturity means.

## Stage three: of the four moats, the shortest one is being dug away

The 7 score reflects the need to break the moat into parts. Global distribution, content library, recommendation algorithm, brand — all four are real and reinforce one another: distribution amortizes content cost, content sustains the brand, and the algorithm pushes content to the right people, reducing churn.

But the width of the moat must be tested on two things. First, can it keep raising prices — it cleared this hurdle in 2026, with the company saying user response was broadly in line with past price increases and churn down year on year. Second, can it defend viewing time — this hurdle is being lost. US viewing share is 7.8% versus YouTube's 14%, nearly double.

That gap cannot be closed by buying a few more series. YouTube's content is user-uploaded, with zero marginal cost and unlimited supply; Netflix pays for every additional hour of content. So Netflix can only choose "don't fight the viewing-time war, fight on monetization efficiency per unit of time" — it earns more than double YouTube's revenue per hour of content. The strategy is right, but it defines the ceiling: Netflix is a premium paid service, not a mass entertainment platform. The 7 score deducts for that ceiling.

## Level four: why management gets only 6 points

This is the only uncomfortable part of the entire report. On October 13, the two co-CEOs sent an internal letter announcing layoffs of about 6%, close to 1,000 people, the largest since 2022; in the same round of restructuring, Chief Content Officer Bela Bajaria was replaced and film division head Dan Lin departed. Content acquisition and production are Netflix's core assets, and replacing the heads of two business lines within a year looks less like a proactive upgrade and more like a move under performance pressure.

Look further back. In February 2026, the company abandoned its USD 82.7 billion acquisition of Warner Bros. Discovery, and Paramount won with USD 31 per share, a total of about USD 110 billion. Netflix's stock rose at the time—the market read it clearly: it does not need M&A to solve its problems. The converse also holds: if organizational efficiency were good enough, it would not have pursued an USD 82.7 billion acquisition.

A score of 6 is not a denial of capability. The dual-CEO structure of Ted Sarandos and Greg Peters has run for six years, taking the company from 200 million members to nearly 1 billion viewers—the results are solid. The deduction is for "repetition": the strategic direction (originals + advertising + sports) is right, but three organizational overhauls in one year at the execution level show it still has no stable answer for "how to fight once growth slows to 12%."

## Gates five and six: the margin of safety comes from the price; discipline is something you hold to yourself

What does 18x 2026 expected PE mean? Netflix's PE has ranged between 18x and 45x since 2023, peaking near 45x in 2024. It now sits at the bottom of that range. Excluding the one-off Q1 gain it is about 21x, which is not expensive either. In other words, the market is already pricing it as a mature company growing 10% with 31% margins.

Free cash flow of USD 12 billion is 4.4% of the USD 270 billion market cap; Q3 buybacks totaled USD 4.8 billion and 63.9 million shares at an average of about USD 75 (rough calculation), with USD 22.3 billion remaining on the authorization. That combination provides a solid floor: even with no re-rating, 6%–7% annual share cancellation still moves per-share value forward.

The sixth gate scores 6 because discipline has to be self-imposed, not granted by the company. The buy case rests on three legs — subscription price increases, advertising growth, and buyback-driven share cancellation. The advertising leg is already limping (cut from USD 3 billion to USD 2.7 billion), but the other two can still sustain double-digit growth. That is the difference between buying in tranches and going all in: not because it is cheap, but because it still stands with one of three legs broken.
