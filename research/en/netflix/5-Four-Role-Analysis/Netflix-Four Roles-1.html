Business analysis (Duan Yongping's perspective) — Netflix

Where the money comes from, why it keeps coming, and whether it will still be there in ten years
Research date 2026/10/23  ·  Price $65.85 (-1.79%)  ·  Market cap About USD 270 billion  ·  PE(2026E) ~18x (including one-off gains, ~21x excluding them)

One-liner: a subscription business that monetises fear of missing out

Netflix's business model boils down to one plain sentence: charge a monthly fee so subscribers have something to watch at night, and keep making more of whatever they can't finish. The upside is predictable revenue, fast cash collection and declining marginal cost; the downside is that content must be invested in continuously — stop for a year and subscribers leave. This is not a "money-for-nothing" business, then, but a good business that "must keep running."

The most important piece of evidence in 2026: after the price increase, churn did not rise but fell. That says more about its pricing power than market share or content volume does. Where does pricing power come from? Not exclusive content — no single show can now keep someone from cancelling for a year — but its position as the "default option": when people want to watch TV, it is the first thing they open.

What makes this a good business: prepayment, zero marginal cost and a default position

Three factors combined to deliver the 31.5% operating margin. First, prepayment: users pay at the start of each month, so cash arrives before the service is delivered—no receivables, no bad debt. Second, zero marginal cost: once a show is produced, the cost is identical whether 10 million or 100 million people watch it. Third, default position: when users switch on the TV, it is the first thing they look for, and that position is valuable in itself, because the cost of replacing it is not money but the hassle of "searching again for what to watch."

The default-position point is the most underappreciated. When Netflix raised prices in 2026, user reaction was broadly consistent with previous increases, and churn actually fell YoY. The implication: users are not calculating "is this USD 20 worth it" but "what will I watch if I cancel." As long as the second question has no good answer, the first question does not arise.

But this business carries a hard constraint: content spending is mandatory. In 2026 the company stepped up its sports push—NFL and live boxing—and these rights are paid annually and locked in by event, so once signed they become rigid costs. Sports serves not to acquire users but to reduce churn: among subscribers, some stay only for a few live events, and these users contribute the most stable slice of revenue.

Where the business falls short: content spending cannot stop, and it keeps getting more expensive

This is where Netflix and Apple diverge most. Apple could halt R&D for a year and still be Apple the next; if Netflix stopped content spending for a year, subscribers would start leaving the year after. Its free cash flow must therefore always deduct a large chunk of "maintenance content spending" — the USD 12 billion FCF on the books is not all cash shareholders can take out.

Price is the bigger problem. Sports rights inflate far faster than series: the sellers of assets like the NFL and boxing are the leagues themselves, a naturally monopolistic supply. Netflix's higher sports spending in 2026 directly lowered its Q4 operating margin guidance (26.0%, partly reflecting 1.2 percentage points of severance costs). If sports rights keep getting more expensive, Netflix must either give up or accept peak margins.

This is why I call it a good business that "must keep running." It is not an asset you can stop and collect rent from; it is a machine that needs constant refueling. A 30x P/E for this kind of business is wrong, and 15x is too low — 18 to 22x exactly matches a state of "can run but can't run fast."

Will it still be here in ten years: three judgments

Judgment 1: Subscription will not be displaced. Over the past decade every "free plus advertising" platform has eventually launched a paid tier, and no paid platform has successfully gone fully free. Payment is the positive feedback loop that funds content quality, and that mechanism is unchanged.

Judgment 2: Netflix will not be the only winner, but it will be the most profitable one. Disney has IP, Amazon has distribution and YouTube has watch time, but only Netflix has built all three at once: global distribution, originals and ad tech. The company that makes the most money in an industry need not be the biggest, only the one with the best cost structure.

Judgment 3: Growth will come from "each user paying more," not "more users." With reach of nearly 1 billion viewers, penetration is already close to its ceiling. Over the next five years, revenue growth will come mainly from price increases, ad-tier upgrades and converting shared accounts into paid accounts. That implies growth settles at 8%–12% rather than returning to 20%. If that holds, 18x P/E is a fair price, not a cheap one.

Conclusion

The business is understandable and has pricing power, but it is the type that "must keep running." That characterisation directly sets the valuation ceiling: 18–22x is fair, below 18x is an opportunity, above 26x is overextended. At USD 65.85, the stock trades at 18x 2026E P/E, right at the lower edge of the fair range. You buy it not because it is absurdly cheap, but because this price pays no premium for "growth," and there is still some growth left.
