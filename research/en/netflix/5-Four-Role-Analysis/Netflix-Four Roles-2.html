Financial analysis (Buffett's perspective) — Netflix

Is the cash flow real, is the money being spent wisely, and can the balance sheet hold up?
Research date 2026/10/23  ·  Price $65.85 (-1.79%)  ·  Market cap About USD 270 billion  ·  PE(2026E) ~18x (including one-off gains, ~21x excluding them)

The cash is real, spending is a touch aggressive and the balance sheet is very clean

Buffett looks at three things: whether profit converts into cash, how management spends money, and whether the balance sheet can hold up. Netflix passes all three, but the second deserves a discount—Q3 buybacks were USD 4.8 billion against free cash flow of just USD 2.884 billion, a 1.66x multiple.

The profit-quality point is interesting: Q3 net income was USD 3.664 billion versus operating cash flow of USD 3.162 billion, so net income exceeded cash flow by USD 500 million. That is not a bad thing—it reflects a quarterly mismatch between content amortization and cash outlays—but it is a reminder: for this company, cash flow is more reliable than profit.

Profit quality: why net profit above operating cash flow is not a red flag

A company's operating cash flow should normally exceed net income, since depreciation and amortisation carry no cash cost. Netflix inverted that in Q3: net income of RMB 3.664 billion against operating cash flow of RMB 3.162 billion, a gap of RMB 500 million.

Content accounting explains it. Netflix capitalises content spending and amortises it over several years in line with viewing — the income statement deducts amortisation, while the cash flow statement captures actual cash outlays. When cash spending in a quarter outruns amortisation, cash flow drops below profit. That is a timing mismatch, not fraud. Persistent over several consecutive quarters, however, it would signal accelerating content purchases and profit flattered by "slow amortisation."

How to verify? Look at the annual, not the quarterly. 2026 free cash flow guidance of USD 12 billion against normalised net income of USD 12.9 billion gives a ratio of 93% — a healthy number. Below 70%, the story would change. My read: the RMB 500 million Q3 gap is noise; the annual 93% is the signal.

Capital allocation: buybacks outsize cash flow

This is the point I care about most. Q3 buybacks totaled USD 4.8 billion, or 63.9 million shares, at an average price of about USD 75.12 (rough calculation)—14% above the current USD 65.85. In other words, the company bought back its own stock at USD 75 a month ago, and the market now prices it at just USD 65.85. Near term, that looks like money poorly spent; longer term, as long as intrinsic value exceeds 75, it is not a mistake.

Scale is the bigger concern: annualised at the Q3 pace, buybacks run to USD 19.2 billion, against full-year FCF of only USD 12 billion. Where does the USD 7.2 billion gap come from? Cash on the books of USD 8.2 billion, plus the debt market. Net debt is just USD 7.1 billion, 0.44x operating profit—entirely manageable. But three consecutive years at this pace would push net debt above USD 20 billion, and at that point buybacks shift from "confidence" to "leverage."

I score this a 7: the direction is right (buying back stock at a low valuation is optimal capital allocation), but the intensity is on the heavy side (the portion above FCF relies on debt). The red line sits here: once net debt/operating profit exceeds 1x, buybacks have begun eroding financial flexibility—and at that point the move is to trim the position, not add.

Balance sheet: clean, but no longer net cash

Debt of USD 15.3 billion against USD 8.2 billion of cash gives net debt of USD 7.1 billion — just 0.44x the USD 16.1 billion of annual operating profit, a negligible leverage. One change is worth flagging: Netflix used to be a net cash company and no longer is. With buybacks ramped up in 2026, cash is trending down.

On debt structure, Netflix's borrowings are mostly long-term and fixed-rate, much of it issued at the low-rate trough in 2022, so interest expense for the next two years is locked in and immune to rising rates. That underpins its confidence to buy back stock in size.

The last question Buffett would ask: "If revenue stops growing, can it hold up?" Run the numbers — revenue of USD 51.1 billion, operating margin of 31.5% — and even with zero revenue growth it still generates USD 16.1 billion of operating profit and USD 12 billion of free cash flow a year, more than enough to cover USD 7.1 billion of net debt. The answer is yes. This is the core basis for my view that its downside is limited.

Conclusion

All three tests pass: the cash is real (annual FCF/net income 93%), capital allocation points the right way but is sized too aggressively (buybacks 1.66x FCF), and the balance sheet is clean (net debt 0.44x operating profit). Conclusion: this is a company you can buy at 18x P/E, but watch the buyback pace—it is the one place where a good balance sheet could be put to bad use.
