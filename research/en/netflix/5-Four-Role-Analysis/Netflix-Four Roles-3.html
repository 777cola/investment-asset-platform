Industry Research (Munger's Lens) — Netflix

Three battlegrounds, four weapons, one battle already over
Research date 2026/10/23  ·  Price $65.85 (-1.79%)  ·  Market cap About USD 270 billion  ·  PE(2026E) ~18x (including one-off gains, ~21x excluding them)

One line: only two battles remain in this industry — Netflix has won one and cannot win the other

The streaming industry has three battlegrounds: watch time, subscription revenue, and advertising revenue. The watch-time war is over—YouTube commands 14% of US viewing share on zero-marginal-cost content, Netflix 7.8%, and that gap will not narrow. Netflix won the subscription war: global distribution plus in-house production, and no second player has both. The advertising war has just begun—Netflix at USD 2.7 billion, up against the mature ad systems of Google and Meta.

The Munger-style conclusion: don't ask "will the streaming industry grow," ask "whose pocket will the growth flow into." The answer is that money flowing out of cable TV and traditional ad budgets will go to players with "global distribution capability + an ad-tech stack." Only Netflix and Amazon meet both conditions.

The duration war is over, and there will be no comeback.

This is the most widely misread thing in the industry. Many compare Netflix with Disney and assume the side with stronger IP wins. But the real rival is YouTube, and YouTube's advantage is not IP but supply structure: content is uploaded by users, the platform pays no production costs, marginal cost is zero, and supply is infinite.

Every additional hour of content costs Netflix money, so its library has boundaries; YouTube's library has none. On the dimension of "total hours," a paid platform can never beat a UGC platform — this is structural, not a matter of execution.

Munger would say: if you can't win, don't fight. Netflix's correct strategy is "make more money from fewer hours" — push monetization efficiency per hour to more than double that of rivals. In 2026 it did: a 7.8% share of viewing hours translated into quarterly revenue of USD 12.874 billion. YouTube's 14% share of viewing hours translates into advertising revenue, with monetization efficiency per hour at less than half of Netflix's.

Netflix won the subscription war, but its victory is fragile

One metric decides the subscription war: who can raise prices without losing users. Netflix raised prices in 2026 and churn did not rise; Disney+ also raised prices, but has clearly less room, because its library leans on a handful of IPs like Marvel and Star Wars—users watch and leave, and retention rests on "waiting for the next one."

Netflix retains users by "always having something new." Financially, this shows up as: Netflix can raise prices 5%–8% a year, Disney only 2%–3%. Pricing power is the deciding factor in the subscription war.

The fragile point: this advantage depends on steady content output. Cutting 6% of staff and replacing the content chief in October 2026, if it thins the 2027 slate, will show up in pricing power in 2028—the lag in the content business stretches the time gap between cause and effect to 18 months. That is also why I give management only 6 points.

Ad war: inventory is exclusive, technology lags

Advertising is the only battleground still offering incremental growth. Global TV advertising runs about USD 170 billion a year and is migrating from linear TV to streaming.Netflix's advantage is exclusive inventory—advertisers do not want "some platform," they want "that cohort of high-income, high-attention users on Netflix," and there is no substitute for this inventory.

The weakness is technology. Netflix Ads Suite only expanded programmatic buying in Q3 2026, connecting to external demand-side platforms such as MercadoLibre and AdForm.By contrast, Google's and Meta's ad systems have run for fifteen years, and their targeting, attribution and bidding are all more mature.At USD 2.7 billion, revenue in this industry counts only as "just getting started."

So I do not see the cut from USD 3 billion to USD 2.7 billion as a demand problem but a delivery-capability problem—the company's own wording is that "some ad product features rolled out slower than planned."Expanding to 15 new countries in March 2027 is the first hard checkpoint for whether this curve can take off.If it still fails to take off by then, advertising will forever remain a 5% side dish, not a second curve.

Conclusion

The industry structure is clear: time spent lost, subscriptions won, advertising just beginning. Taken together, these three things determine that Netflix's ceiling is a "high-margin premium paid channel," not a "mass entertainment platform." Valuing against that ceiling, 18x PE is a reasonable starting point; valuing against the "streaming disrupts everything" narrative, 45x is also "reasonable"—that is what the market gave in 2024, and it is now merely correcting.
