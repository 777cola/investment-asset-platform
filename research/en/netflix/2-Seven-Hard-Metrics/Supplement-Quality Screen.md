## The only item it fails: no dividend, but that is not a flaw

Netflix has not paid a single cent of dividends in over two decades since its IPO. By the "consecutive dividend" screen, it should be excluded. But the intent of that metric is to filter out companies that earn money without sharing it with shareholders, and Netflix puts its money into buybacks: USD 4.8 billion in Q3 alone, 63.9 million shares, a record, with USD 22.3 billion remaining authorization, equal to 8.2% of market cap.

Mathematically, retired buybacks and cash dividends have the same effect on per-share value. **The difference is tax: US investors owe tax immediately on dividends, while share-price gains from buybacks can be deferred.** For a company still in its growth phase with cash flow expanding rapidly, buybacks are the better distribution method. So this "fail" should be reinterpreted: it is not a failure to reward shareholders, but a switch in tool.

But one caveat bears watching. **Q3 free cash flow was USD 2.884 billion, while buybacks consumed USD 4.8 billion—1.66x the quarter's FCF.** Annualized at that pace, 2026 buybacks would reach about USD 19 billion, against full-year FCF guidance of only USD 12 billion. Where does the gap come from? USD 8.2 billion of cash on the balance sheet, plus the debt market. This is not a crisis (net debt is only USD 7.1 billion), but it shows that buyback sustainability depends on two things: FCF not being revised down further, and interest rates not spiraling out of control. That is why I mark it "not fatal but conditional."

## Six more: not a squeaker, but a clear pass

The 31.5% operating margin is the most striking item among these seven. In media, Disney's operating margin has sat in the 10%–15% range for years, and Paramount and WBD's streaming businesses have only just turned profitable. **Netflix earns the entire industry's profit with a single company's P&L.** Not because its content is cheaper, but because its user base is three times everyone else's, spreading fixed costs thinnest.

Read the 25.2% net margin alongside operating cash flow/net profit of 86%, and the profit is real. Some companies post high net margins but poor cash flow, propped up by receivables; Netflix users prepay monthly, so cash comes in faster than profit. Q3 net profit was 3.664 billion and operating cash flow 3.162 billion, the gap mainly cash outflow on content spend—**the only place it spends before it earns, and the only weakness in this business.**

Net debt/operating profit of 0.44x makes it almost a net cash company. With 15.3 billion of debt and 8.2 billion of cash against a business generating 12 billion of free cash flow a year, leverage is negligible. Revenue growth of 13% is the weakest of the seven—it comes from price increases and advertising, not membership growth, and will likely fall to around 10% in 2027, at which point it hugs the threshold.

## What this set of results means: the problem is not quality, it is duration

The purpose of the negative screen is to rule out companies whose business itself does not work. Netflix passes six of the seven criteria, and all six are clear passes, which shows that its business quality is not a risk.**Its current share price problem is a valuation problem, not a quality problem—these two things must be kept separate.**

Where does the valuation problem come from? From the market repricing the "growth ceiling." Q3 revenue growth of 11.8% was the lowest in three years, advertising revenue was cut from RMB 3 billion to RMB 2.7 billion, and Q4 operating margin guidance is 26.0% (dragged down 1.2 percentage points by severance costs). Put these three pieces of news together, and the market concluded that "growth is over."

But looking at the seven hard metrics, that conclusion goes too far. A company with a 31.5% operating margin, FCF of RMB 12 billion, net debt of 0.44x, and still doing large buybacks is a high-quality asset even if growth slows to 8%—it is just no longer a growth stock.**Buying it at a growth-stock valuation is wrong; buying it at a mature-media valuation is a bargain—today's 18x is the latter.**
