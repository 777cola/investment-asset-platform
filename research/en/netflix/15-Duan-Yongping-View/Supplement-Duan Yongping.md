# Netflix（NFLX）— Duan Yongping Supplement

- Research date：2026/10/23
- Price：65.85 USD
- Market cap：About USD 270 billion

## Notes

This module frames an analysis around the investment principles Duan Yongping has stated publicly; it is not his own view, and the Q&A format is a writing device. Financial figures are from Netflix's 2026Q3 earnings report and full-year guidance; the share price of USD 65.85 and 52-week high of USD 109.35 are on the post-10-for-1-split basis effective 2025/11/17. The US viewing shares of 7.8% (Netflix) and 14% (YouTube) are third-party audience-measurement figures (October 2026).

## Q: Is this a good business?

Good. Good in three ways. First, cash comes in before it goes out—no receivables. Second, content costs are fixed and spread thinner as users grow—that is how Netflix gets to a 31.5% operating margin. Third, it can raise prices. It raised prices once in 2026 and churn fell YoY, which shows users are not comparing prices but comparing "what do I watch at night without it."

But to be clear about what is "not good": content spending cannot stop. Apple can halt R&D for a year and still be Apple; if Netflix halts content spending for a year, subscribers fall the next year. So a large chunk of its free cash flow is maintenance capex and cannot all be counted as shareholder money.

In one sentence: a good business, but a good business that "must keep running," not one that "collects rent lying down." That distinction sets the valuation ceiling—18 to 22 times is reasonable; above 30 times is a bet it can win without running.

## Q: Will it still be around in ten years?

It survives. The subscription model will not be displaced: every "free plus advertising" platform of the past decade eventually launched a paid tier, and no paid platform has successfully gone fully free. Payment is the positive feedback loop that funds content quality, and that mechanism is unchanged.

But surviving and thriving are two different things. In ten years it could be a company with USD 100 billion in annual revenue, a 34% operating margin and USD 20 billion of annual buybacks—or one growing 3% and squeezed into a corner by YouTube. What separates the two is not the "model" but "whether the content can keep moving people."

I cannot answer that judgment definitively. So my approach is: buy on the assumption that "it survives," and control position size on the assumption that "it deteriorates." That is the point of scaling in.

## Q: Can I figure it out?

Easy to understand. This business does not require a view on semiconductors or monetary policy. It requires judging two things: whether people still watch TV, and whether they are willing to pay every month. Both are observable by anyone.

What is not understandable is "content" — you cannot predict what the next hit will be. That does not affect the judgment, because Netflix does not survive on a single hit; it runs a probability game of "50 shows a year, two or three will break out." The larger the scale, the more stable the odds.

What is genuinely hard to understand is the "ad tech stack" and "sports rights pricing." The former drives valuation elasticity, the latter drives margin. If you cannot see either clearly, strip them out of the valuation and value the company on "subscriptions + buybacks" alone — that gives the price corresponding to 18x P/E, which is the current price.

## Q: At what price to buy?

Buy a little at USD 65.85, add below USD 55 and build to a full position near USD 45. Each of the three levels maps to a different kind of "certainty."

USD 65.85 equates to 18x 2026E P/E (21x excluding one-off gains) and a 4.4% free cash flow yield.At that price the market already values Netflix as a mature media company and pays nothing for growth.If you believe it can still grow 10%, that is cheap.

USD 55 equates to 15x, the absolute floor since 2023. At that level the market is already pricing in 8% growth, and a margin of safety emerges. USD 45 is the bear case, where the payoff becomes 10% downside against 75% upside.

In one sentence:"Cheap" is not about the absolute price; it is about whether the worst case is priced in.At USD 65.85, only half of the worst case was priced in.

## Q: What is the biggest fear?

The biggest fear is not Disney — it is users "not watching and not feeling deprived."

Today, cancelling Netflix feels like "something is missing." If it ever becomes "cancelling feels like nothing," the business is finished. The shift will not be sudden; it will creep in — first they stop following shows, then a month passes without opening the app, and finally the auto-renewal slips their mind, and the moment they notice it, they cancel on the spot.

How do you spot it in the data? Watch two metrics: monthly active viewing hours, and the share of users who resubscribe within 30 days of cancelling. The first falls, and the second falls too — that is the danger signal. A 7.8% US viewing share against YouTube's 14% is already part of that signal.

The second fear is "spending to buy growth for growth's sake" — sports rights get more expensive, content investment keeps growing, and the margin eventually drops from 31.5% back to 28%. At that point revenue is still growing but profit is not, and the valuation gets hit on both earnings and multiple. These two "biggest fears" are why I set "operating margin falling below 30%" as the liquidation line.
