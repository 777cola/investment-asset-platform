# Netflix（NFLX）— Duan Yongping Supplement

- Research date：2026/10/23
- Price：65.85 USD
- Market cap：About USD 270 billion

## Notes

This module is an analysis framed around the investment principles Duan Yongping has stated publicly; it is not his own view, and the Q&A format is a writing device. Financial figures come from Netflix's 2026Q3 earnings report and full-year guidance; the share price of USD 65.85 and 52-week high of USD 109.35 are on the post-10-for-1-split basis effective 2025/11/17. The US viewing shares of 7.8% (Netflix) and 14% (YouTube) are third-party audience-measurement figures (October 2026).

## Q: Is this a good business?

**Good.** Good in three ways. First, cash comes in before it goes out—no receivables. Second, content costs are fixed and spread thinner as users grow—that is how Netflix gets to a 31.5% operating margin. Third, it can raise prices. It raised prices once in 2026 and churn fell year on year, which shows users are not comparing prices but comparing "what do I watch at night without it."

But to be clear about what is "not good": **content spending cannot stop.** Apple can halt R&D for a year and still be Apple; if Netflix halts content spending for a year, subscribers fall the next year. So a large chunk of its free cash flow is maintenance capex and cannot all be counted as shareholder money.

In one sentence: **a good business, but a good business that "must keep running," not one that "collects rent lying down."** That distinction sets the valuation ceiling—18 to 22 times is reasonable; above 30 times is a bet it can win without running.

## Q: Will it still be around in ten years?

**It survives.** The subscription model will not be displaced—every "free + advertising" platform over the past decade eventually launched a paid tier, and conversely no paid platform has successfully converted to purely free. Payment is the positive feedback loop for content quality, and that mechanism has not changed.

But "surviving" and "thriving" are two different things. **In ten years it could be a company with USD 100 billion in annual revenue, a 34% operating margin, and USD 20 billion in annual buybacks—or it could be a company growing 3% and squeezed into a corner by YouTube.** The difference between the two is not the "model" but "whether the content can keep moving people."

I cannot give a definitive answer to that judgment. So my approach is: **buy on the assumption that "it survives," and control position size on the assumption that "it deteriorates."** That is the point of scaling in.

## Q: Can I figure it out?

**Understandable.** This business does not require understanding semiconductors or monetary policy. It requires judging two things: whether people still watch TV, and whether they are willing to pay every month. Anyone can observe both.

What is not understandable is "content" — **you cannot predict what the next hit will be.** But this does not affect the judgment, because Netflix does not survive on a single hit; it runs a probability game of "50 shows a year, two or three will break out." The larger the scale, the more stable this probability game.

What is genuinely hard to understand is the "ad tech stack" and "sports rights pricing." **The former determines valuation elasticity, the latter determines margin.** If you cannot see these two clearly, strip them out of the valuation and value the company on "subscriptions + buybacks" alone — that gives the price corresponding to 18x PE, which is the current price.

## Q: At what price to buy?

**USD 65.85 is a level to buy a little; below USD 55, buy more; near USD 45, buy to full position.** Three price levels map to three kinds of "certainty."

USD 65.85 corresponds to 18x 2026E PE (21x excluding one-off gains) and a 4.4% free cash flow yield.**That price means the market already values it as a mature media company and pays no premium for growth.**If you believe it can still grow 10%, this is cheap.

USD 55 corresponds to 15x, the absolute bottom since 2023. At that level, the market is already pricing in "8% growth," and a margin of safety appears. USD 45 corresponds to the bear case, where the payoff turns into "10% downside, 75% upside."

In one sentence:**"Cheap" is not about the absolute price; it is about whether the worst case has been priced in.**At USD 65.85, the worst case was only half priced in.

## Q: What is the biggest fear?

**The biggest fear is not Disney — it is "not watching it and not feeling deprived."**

Today, when users cancel Netflix, they feel "something is missing." If that ever becomes "canceling feels like nothing," the business is finished. This shift will not happen suddenly; it will happen slowly — **first they stop following shows, then a month passes without opening the app, and finally the auto-renewal is forgotten, and when they notice it, they cancel on the spot.**

How do you see it in the data? Watch two metrics: **monthly active viewing hours, and the share of users who resubscribe within 30 days of canceling.** The first falls, and the second falls too — that is the danger signal. A 7.8% US viewing share against YouTube's 14% is already part of that signal.

The second fear is "spending to buy growth for growth's sake" — sports rights get more expensive, content investment keeps growing, and the margin eventually drops from 31.5% back to 28%. **At that point revenue is still growing but profit is not, and the valuation gets hit on both earnings and multiple.** These two "biggest fears" are why I set "operating margin falling below 30%" as the liquidation line.
