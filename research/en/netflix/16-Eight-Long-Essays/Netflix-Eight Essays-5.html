Part Five: Industry and Competition — Three Battlegrounds, Two Already Decided — Netflix

It lost the duration war to YouTube and won the subscription war against Disney. The advertising war has only just begun.
Research date 2026/10/23  ·  Price $65.85 (-1.79%)  ·  Market cap About USD 270 billion  ·  PE(2026E) ~18x (including one-off gains, ~21x excluding them)

First define the battlefield, then discuss competition

Treating "streaming competition" as a single phenomenon yields muddled conclusions. Split it into three battlegrounds and the picture clears: time spent, subscription revenue, advertising revenue. The outcomes differ completely across the three, and none carries over to the others.

Netflix lost the time-spent war (YouTube 14% vs 7.8%), won the subscription war (5%–8% price hikes with no subscriber loss), and has only just begun the advertising war (RMB 2.7 billion, an entry-level industry level). That combination makes Netflix a "high-margin premium paid service," not a "mass entertainment platform."

Duration war: a structural failure, not worth further investment

YouTube's edge lies not in content but in its supply structure: user uploads, no production costs borne by the platform, zero marginal cost. Its content library is therefore unbounded; Netflix's is not.

On "total watch time," Netflix is bound to lose, so it chose another path: earn more money from fewer hours. A 7.8% share of watch time yields USD 12.874 billion in quarterly revenue; YouTube's 14% yields ad revenue, with monetisation efficiency per hour at less than half of Netflix's.

The choice is right, but it comes at a cost: giving up watch time means giving up "the scale of ad inventory." Ads sell on impressions; without enough hours, there isn't enough inventory. That is the structural reason ad revenue is only USD 2.7 billion—not that it can't be sold, but that there isn't enough inventory to sell.

Subscription battle: a win, but not an easy one

Pricing power is the decisive factor in the subscription war. Netflix raised prices 5%–8% in 2026 and churn still fell YoY; Disney+ has also raised prices but with clearly less room—its content depends on a handful of IPs such as Marvel and Star Wars, users leave once they have finished watching, and retention rests on "waiting for the next one."

Amazon's Prime Video does not compete on this battlefield. It is a perk of Prime membership rather than a standalone profit centre for video, so it neither engages in price wars nor pursues subscription revenue.It poses no threat, but it offers no benchmark either.

The real change is the February 2026 M&A deal: Paramount acquired WBD for about USD 110 billion.The industry has shifted from "everyone builds a platform" to "those who can't survive sell to those who can."Integration will take at least two years, during which Paramount will have no spare capacity to poach Netflix's users—a breathing window for Netflix.

Ad war: exclusive inventory, lagging technology, and a ceiling set by technology

Global TV advertising is roughly USD 170 billion a year and is migrating from linear TV to streaming. Netflix's inventory is exclusive—advertisers are not buying "a platform" but "that cohort of high-income, high-attention users on Netflix," for which there is no substitute.

The technology, however, lags. Netflix Ads Suite will not expand programmatic buying until Q3 2026, when it connects MercadoLibre and AdForm.Against Google's and Meta's ad systems, which have run for fifteen years, Netflix trails across targeting, attribution and bidding.

The cut from USD 3 billion to USD 2.7 billion is therefore, in my view, a delivery-capability problem, not a demand problem.The March 2027 expansion into 15 new countries is the test: if ad revenue grows more than 15% quarter on quarter in the two quarters after expansion, the technology catch-up has succeeded; below 8%, this leg will not grow.

Cycle position: in a consolidation, the winner is the one with the deepest cash

The industry is consolidating. In February 2026 Paramount acquired WBD for about USD 110 billion, a deal that underscores how "mid-sized players cannot survive": they lack the scale to spread costs thin enough and, standalone, cannot compete with the leaders.

The winner in a consolidation phase is not the highest bidder but the one with the deepest cash. Netflix sat out this deal, repurchasing USD 4.8 billion of its own stock over the same period—a contrast that is itself the judgment. Choosing to buy itself rather than others signals management sees a higher return on its own assets.

Consolidation carries another side effect: content supply will shrink near term. When two companies merge, production departments overlap and projects get cut—good news for Netflix, with one fewer rival bidding for content. 2027–2028 may be its two years of slowest content-cost growth.

Conclusion

Three battlegrounds, three conclusions: time spent is lost — structural, and cannot be won back; subscriptions are won — on pricing power; advertising has barely begun — it depends on closing the technology gap. The valuation implication of that mix: 15–18x for subscriptions plus option value on advertising, for 18–22x combined. At 18x, the stock sits right at the low end of that range.
