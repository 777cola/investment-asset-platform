Chapter Six: Management — The Best Spenders, and the Most Restless Operators — Netflix

Capital allocation scores 8 and organisational execution 5, for a combined 6

The division of labour between two people, and the character of a company

Netflix's management cuts two ways. On capital allocation it ranks among the best in the industry: no M&A, no dividend, large buybacks, low debt. Walking away from the USD 82.7 billion WBD acquisition in February 2026 was the most telling call of all.

On organisational execution it ranks among the most turbulent: three restructurings in a year, 6% of staff cut, and the content chief and film head departing at the same time. Taken together, the two faces earn a score of 6.

Capital allocation: all three key decisions were right

First, the 2023 password-sharing crackdown. Converting "one family, one account" into "everyone pays for their own" adds paying accounts with content costs unchanged. That was pure margin expansion — the most efficient operational move in Netflix's history.

Second, walking away from WBD in February 2026. At an USD 82.7 billion offer, closing would have loaded Netflix with over USD 80 billion of debt and a portfolio of declining cable channels. Abandoning the deal sent the stock up instead, and the market's read was clear: the company does not need M&A to solve its problems.

Third, the 10-for-1 split in November 2025. The move is neutral in itself, but it usually signals that a company wants to broaden its shareholder base and stabilise the share price. Post-split the stock trades at USD 65.85, far below the USD 658.5 "psychological threshold" that prevailed before the split, drawing more retail participation.

Organisational execution: three moves a year, with the cost landing two years later

On October 13, 2026, the company cut roughly 6% of staff — nearly 1,000 people — its largest reduction since 2022. The same round removed Chief Content Officer Bela Bajaria and saw film head Dan Lin depart. Content acquisition and film production are the two main arteries, and both changed hands within a year.

Why does that matter? Because content carries an 18-month lag. A show takes 12–18 months from greenlight to launch, so a change in content leadership hits the 2027 slate — and a thinner slate will not show up in churn until 2028.

By then, the stock will already have de-rated. This is the hardest kind of risk for investors to handle: not invisible, but visible and impossible to disprove in the short term. I therefore score organisational execution at just 5, with one hard watch line: operating margin must hold at 32% in 2027.

Governance structure: the pros and cons of dual CEOs

Sarandos runs content; Peters runs product and technology; both report jointly to the board. The upside is a clear division of labour and faster decisions; the downside is that when the two disagree, there is no clear arbitration mechanism.

Outside observers cannot tell who drove the October 2026 organisational reshuffle. That kind of unclear accountability is not a problem in good times, but it becomes one in bad times. Add Reed Hastings remaining in place as executive chairman, and founder influence compresses the professional managers' room to manoeuvre.

The succession question has no answer in 2026: both co-CEOs are over 50 and the chairman is 65. This is the only factor in this report that cannot be quantified but must be discounted—and the direct reason I cap the position at 6% rather than 10%.

Where will the next round of spending go?

Three items are queued up: buybacks, sports rights, M&A. On current signals the priority is buybacks > sports > M&A.

The remaining buyback authorization is RMB 22.3 billion, which at a pace of RMB 19 billion per year lasts more than a year; a renewal in 2027 is highly likely.Sports is a "trade profit for retention" deal — investment will rise but will not spiral, because what it buys is broadcast rights, not ownership, giving it high flexibility.

M&A has the lowest probability. After walking away from WBD at USD 82.7 billion, the odds of another large deal in the near term are very low.This is one of the reasons I like this company: knowing what it should not do is harder than knowing what it should do.

Conclusion

Management 6/10: Capital allocation 8 points (no M&A, large buybacks, low leverage), organisational execution 5 points (three moves in one year, unclear succession). The implication: financial downside is contained, strategic downside is not. You can build a position, but should not go heavy — a heavy position requires no questions on the "people" dimension.
