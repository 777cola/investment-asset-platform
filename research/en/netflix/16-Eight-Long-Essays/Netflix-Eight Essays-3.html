Part Three: Anatomy of the Moat — Six Streams, Two Fates — Netflix

Some rivers are widening; others are being dug through.

Brand and cost are wide moats; switching costs and duration are shallow fords

A moat is not a single verdict but six separately scorable dimensions. Netflix scores strongly on brand (8), scale cost (9) and data algorithms (7), and weakly on switching costs (5), licences (3) and share of viewing time (5).

That structure points to one conclusion: Netflix can defend "paid television" but not "all screen time." The former is the moat; the latter is the ceiling.

Kuanhe: brand and cost — the moat widens with use

The test of a brand moat is not name recognition but default choice. When users turn on the TV, is it the first thing they look for? Yes. That is the value of the brand: it eliminates the decision cost of "what to watch tonight."

The cost moat is harder to replicate. Nearly 1 billion viewers dilute fixed content costs, driving unit content cost to the lowest in the industry. A new entrant replicating this cost structure would need a decade and hundreds of billions of dollars. This is not a problem money can solve—it is a problem of time and scale.

The two moats reinforce each other: the brand brings users, users dilute costs, low costs support more content, and more content strengthens the brand. This flywheel is Netflix's most valuable asset and the source of its 31.5% operating margin.

Shallows: switching costs and licences were never deep

Switching costs score only 5, because cancelling a subscription costs nothing. All you lose is "the show you're halfway through," and you can always come back to it. The real friction comes from household decisions and habit, not money.

Compare Apple: switching phones means migrating data, rebuying apps, reconfiguring everything — several hundred dollars plus a full day. Netflix's switching cost is ten minutes and a bit of reluctance. So it must keep producing good content — not to build a wall, but to keep pushing back the act of "cancelling."

Licences and market access score 3, with almost no barriers. Streaming needs no licence; the only entry barrier is money. Over the past decade more than twenty companies have entered, most of them died — but they died not because they "couldn't get in" but because they "couldn't make money." The industry has no access barriers, only scale barriers.

The one that got hollowed out: share of time

This is Netflix's only moat that is narrowing. US viewing share stands at 7.8% for Netflix versus 14% for YouTube, and the gap is widening.

Why is it losing ground? Not because the content is bad, but because the supply structure differs. YouTube's content is user-uploaded, carries zero marginal cost and offers unlimited supply; Netflix pays for every additional hour of content, so its supply is bounded. On total watch time, a paid platform can never beat a UGC platform.

With this moat breached, Netflix cannot win on "more content"—only on "more expensive content": premium originals, live sports and exclusive series. That path carries high margins but a low ceiling. The 7 points for moat and 5 points for watch time are docked precisely for this ceiling.

Time dimension: which river will change in five years

Moats are not static. Over five years, brand and cost advantages will hold, the data-algorithm moat will widen, switching costs will narrow, and share of time will keep shrinking.

Why will the data-algorithm moat widen? Because advertising demands finer user profiles and attribution capabilities, which in turn improve recommendation accuracy and reduce churn. If advertising succeeds, the algorithm compounds; if it fails, the algorithm is just a cost.

Why will switching costs narrow? Because content is growing more abundant and more fragmented, and the odds that users "watch everything they want on one platform" are falling. Once users subscribe to two or three platforms at once, Netflix goes from "the default" to "one of them" — and pricing power will then decline markedly. This is why I assign only 6%–8% growth after 2028.

Conclusion

Three of the six moats are wide (brand, cost, data) and three are shallow (switching costs, licences, time spent). This is the classic "high profit, low growth" structure: profit derives from cost and brand, while time spent and access cap growth. That supports 18–22x, and the current 18x sits at the low end of the fair range.
