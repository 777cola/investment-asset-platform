## Value distribution across the streaming media industry chain

| Segment | Representative companies/entities | Value or gross margin | Bargaining power |
|---|---|---|---|
| Upstream IP and sports rights | Disney, Warner, the NFL, boxing leagues | IP licensing is near pure profit; sports rights rise 8%–10% a year | Strong (supply monopoly) |
| Upstream: Production and post-production | Five major studios, local production companies in various countries | Project-based, gross margin around 10%–20% | Weak to moderate (substitutable) |
| Midstream platforms and distribution | Netflix, Disney+, Prime Video, YouTube | Netflix operating margin 31.5% (company-reported) | Strong (users and data) |
| Midstream Technology and Advertising | Open Connect (self-built CDN), Netflix Ads Suite, Google/Meta ad systems | Ad tech take rate of about 15%–20% (industry norm) | China (Netflix is a newcomer) |
| Downstream users and advertisers | More than 1 billion viewers and brand advertisers across 190+ countries | ARPU varies widely by region | Weak (no bargaining power per user) |
| Downstream bundled channels | Telecom operators, Flipkart and other retail platforms | Distribution commission | China (owns the touchpoints) |

The annual increase in sports rights fees reflects an industry trend reported publicly and has not been verified deal by deal.

## Upstream: content is getting more expensive, and rightly so

Upstream splits into two categories. One is substitutable—series, films, local productions, with many production companies, project-based work and weak bargaining power, where 10%–20% gross margins are the norm. The other is non-substitutable—**sports rights and super IPs**, where the sellers are leagues and a handful of studios, and supply is naturally monopolistic.

Netflix's strategy is "originals first, licensing as a supplement." In 2026 it added a content licensing deal with Disney and a partnership with France's TF1—these broaden "breadth," not buy "exclusivity." **Making content in-house means controllable costs and owning the IP; the downside is the need for scale, and scale it already has.**

But sports is the exception. For rights like the NFL and boxing, Netflix cannot buy "ownership," only "broadcast rights," paid annually and renegotiated at expiry. **This effectively hands pricing power over part of its profit to the leagues.** Q4 2026 operating margin guidance is 26.0%, and sports investment is one reason. This is the only gap in the value chain where "the midstream is squeezed by the upstream."

## Midstream: why platforms are the most profitable, and how long they can hold that position

The platform makes money because it controls three things at once: **user relationships (who pays), data (who is watching what), and distribution (how content reaches the viewer).** These three things let it squeeze suppliers on one side and raise prices on the other.

Netflix has taken the middle layer to its limit: its own CDN (Open Connect) internalizes distribution costs, its recommendation algorithm achieves the industry's highest content-matching efficiency, and its ad tech stack (Netflix Ads Suite) added programmatic buying in Q3 2026, integrating MercadoLibre and AdForm. **The result is a 31.5% operating margin — the highest in the media industry's history.**

But this position is being squeezed from both ends. Upstream, sports rights are getting more expensive; downstream, YouTube is grabbing viewing time (14% vs. 7.8% share in the US). **The golden age of middle-layer platforms was 2020–2024, when content was cheap, competition was limited and there was ample room to raise prices; after 2025 they enter a "defend-the-fort" phase.** That phase looks like what we see now: margins still rising, but growth slowing.

## Downstream: users have no bargaining power, but their attention does

Individual users have no bargaining power over the platform—prices are set, take it or leave it. But users' "attention" does have bargaining power, because it can flow elsewhere. **This is why Netflix's real rival is YouTube, not Disney+.**

There is another easily overlooked link downstream: bundling channels. In India, Flipkart bundling drove net paid additions to a record high, showing that in low-income markets, "selling subscriptions through e-commerce/telcos" is more effective than "acquiring users yourself." **Such channels earn distribution commissions, and their bargaining power is rising.**

On the advertiser side, Netflix's position is awkward: its inventory is exclusive (high-income users), but its technology lags (programmatic is just starting). **So it can sell at high prices, but not in high volumes**—USD 2.7 billion in scale is only entry-level in this industry.

## Cycle position: from land grab to rent collection, now in between

The industry has gone through three stages: 2013–2019 burning cash to grab territory, 2020–2023 scale effects paying off, and 2024 to now price increases + advertising + buybacks. **It is now in the middle of the third stage.**

Three grounds for this judgment. First, M&A consolidation has begun — in February 2026 Paramount acquired WBD for about USD 110 billion, and the industry shifted from "everyone builds a platform" to "those who can't survive sell to those who can." Second, content cost growth is slowing — Netflix FCF growth of 8.4% is below revenue growth of 11.8%, showing content spending is not out of control. Third, shareholder returns have become mainstream — Netflix bought back 4.8 billion in a single quarter, unthinkable in 2020.

What this position means: **the industry will no longer have a big explosion, but it will not collapse either.** The surviving players will enter a state of "stable rent collection," and the valuation center will fall from the 30–45x of growth stocks to the 15–22x of mature companies. Netflix is now at 18x, right in the middle of this new center. **In other words, its valuation de-rating is largely complete; what remains is earnings delivery.**
