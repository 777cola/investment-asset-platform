Streaming media value chain: profit pools sit in the middle, but the middle is being squeezed by the upstream

The streaming value chain has three layers. Upstream is content and rights (studios, sports leagues, local production companies), midstream is platforms and distribution (Netflix, Disney+, Prime Video, YouTube) and downstream is users and advertisers (nearly 1 billion viewers, brand advertisers, carrier bundle channels).

Value distribution is clear-cut: midstream platforms capture the most profit, with Netflix's operating margin at 31.5% versus gross margins of typically just 10%–20% at upstream production companies. One thing has changed over the past three years, however: bargaining power in sports rights is shifting upstream, and prices for exclusive assets such as the NFL and boxing are rising faster than platforms can raise prices. That is the structural reason Q4 operating margin guidance is only 26.0%.

Upstream: content is getting more expensive, and rightly so

Upstream splits into two categories. The first is substitutable—series, films and local productions, where production companies are plentiful, work is project-based and bargaining power is weak, so 10%–20% gross margins are the norm. The second is non-substitutable—sports rights and super IPs—where sellers are leagues and a handful of studios, and supply is naturally monopolistic.

Netflix's strategy is "originals first, licensing as a supplement." In 2026 it added a content licensing deal with Disney and a partnership with France's TF1—moves that broaden "breadth" rather than buy "exclusivity." Making content in-house means controllable costs and owning the IP; the downside is the need for scale, and scale it already has.

Sports is the exception. For rights such as the NFL and boxing, Netflix cannot buy "ownership," only "broadcast rights," paid annually and renegotiated at expiry. This effectively hands pricing power over part of its profit to the leagues. Q4 2026 operating margin guidance is 26.0%, and sports investment is one reason. This is the only gap in the value chain where "the midstream is squeezed by the upstream."

Midstream: why platforms are the most profitable, and how long they can hold that position

The platform monetises control of three things at once: user relationships (who pays), data (who watches what) and distribution (how content reaches the viewer). That combination lets it squeeze suppliers on one side and raise prices on the other.

Netflix has pushed the middle layer to its limit. Its own CDN (Open Connect) internalises distribution costs, its recommendation algorithm delivers the industry's highest content-matching efficiency, and its ad tech stack (Netflix Ads Suite) added programmatic buying in Q3 2026, integrating MercadoLibre and AdForm. The result is a 31.5% operating margin — the highest in the media industry's history.

That position is now being squeezed from both ends. Upstream, sports rights are getting more expensive; downstream, YouTube is grabbing viewing time (14% vs. 7.8% share in the US). The golden age of middle-layer platforms ran from 2020 to 2024, when content was cheap, competition was limited and there was ample room to raise prices; after 2025 they enter a "defend-the-fort" phase. That phase looks like what we see now: margins still rising, but growth slowing.

Downstream, users have no bargaining power, but their attention does

Individual users have no bargaining power over the platform: prices are set, take it or leave it. Attention is another matter—it can flow elsewhere. That is why Netflix's real rival is YouTube, not Disney+.

One downstream link is easily overlooked: bundling channels. In India, Flipkart bundling drove net paid additions to a record high, showing that in low-income markets, "selling subscriptions through e-commerce/telcos" beats "acquiring users yourself." Such channels earn distribution commissions, and their bargaining power is rising.

On the advertiser side, Netflix's position is awkward: its inventory is exclusive (high-income users), but its technology lags (programmatic is just starting). So it can sell at high prices, but not in high volumes—USD 2.7 billion in scale is only entry-level in this industry.

Cycle position: from land grab to rent collection, now in between

The industry has passed through three stages: 2013–2019, burning cash to grab territory; 2020–2023, when scale effects paid off; and 2024 to now, defined by price increases, advertising and buybacks. It is now in the middle of the third stage.

Three grounds support this judgment. First, M&A consolidation has begun — in February 2026 Paramount acquired WBD for about USD 110 billion, and the industry shifted from "everyone builds a platform" to "those who can't survive sell to those who can." Second, content cost growth is slowing — Netflix FCF growth of 8.4% trails revenue growth of 11.8%, showing content spending is not out of control. Third, shareholder returns have become mainstream — Netflix bought back 4.8 billion in a single quarter, unthinkable in 2020.

What this position means: the industry will no longer have a big explosion, but it will not collapse either. The surviving players will enter a state of "stable rent collection," and the valuation center will fall from the 30–45x of growth stocks to the 15–22x of mature companies. Netflix is now at 18x, right in the middle of this new center. In other words, its valuation de-rating is largely complete; what remains is earnings delivery.

Conclusion

Industry-chain view: Midstream platforms remain the most profitable link in the chain, and Netflix occupies the strongest position within it—but that position has now hit a ceiling, squeezed by rising upstream content costs and downstream loss of viewing hours. Valuation: 15–22x is the reasonable range, with 18x at the lower-middle. The investment implication: "earn from earnings, not from re-rating"—unless advertising scales up and reopens valuation upside.
