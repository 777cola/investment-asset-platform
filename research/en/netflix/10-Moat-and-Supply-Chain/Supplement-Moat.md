## Position in the supply chain: the largest buyer, and the most dangerous seller

Netflix occupies a peculiar position in the value chain: **it is both the largest single customer on the content-buying side and a direct competitor on the content-selling side.** On one hand, it spends tens of billions of dollars a year buying content, and every studio wants to do business with it; on the other, it produces its own content and competes directly with the studios.

This dual identity gives it bargaining power, but also exposes it to the risk of being boycotted. In 2026 it signed a content licensing agreement with Disney and partnered with France's TF1—**moves that show it is willing to spend on "breadth" rather than pursue "exclusivity."** The strategy is sound: exclusivity keeps getting more expensive, while breadth is more cost-effective.

Looking upstream, its soft spot is sports. The sellers of sports rights are leagues with monopolistic supply, so Netflix can only buy broadcast rights year by year, never ownership. **That amounts to surrendering pricing power over part of its profit.** The Q4 operating margin guidance of 26.0% reflects, among other things, sports investment.

## Moat breakdown: why switching costs score only 5

This is the most overrated factor. Many people believe Netflix has a "content library lock-in," but it does not—**the cost of canceling a subscription is zero.** All you lose is "a show you're halfway through," and you can come back and finish it anytime.

The real switching cost comes from "habit" and "household decision-making." For a family to switch platforms, they have to convince everyone, find new content, and readjust to a new interface.**This cost is not money—it's hassle, so it only deters some people.** That is what a score of 5 means: there is friction, but it is not strong enough.

Compare Apple: switching phones means migrating data, rebuying apps, and reconfiguring everything—the switching cost is "several hundred dollars + a whole day." **Netflix's switching cost is "ten minutes + a bit of reluctance."** That is why it must keep producing good content—not to raise switching costs, but to keep pushing back the act of "canceling."

## The most dangerous competitor: YouTube, and why it is not Disney

Disney has IP, but it does not have watch time—Disney+ users "subscribe to finish a series, then leave," and retention depends on "waiting for the next one." **The pricing headroom for this model is only 2%–3%, versus 5%–8% for Netflix.**

Amazon has distribution, but it does not make money from video—Prime Video is a giveaway for Prime members and does not profit from video on a standalone basis. **That means it will not fight a price war, but it also means it will not win.**

Paramount spent about USD 110 billion to buy WBD, funded with debt. Integration will take at least two years, and during those two years it will have no spare capacity to poach Netflix's users.

Only YouTube has all three at once: **zero-marginal-cost content supply, a 14% US viewing share, and a closed loop that converts watch time directly into ad revenue.** Netflix pays for every additional hour of content; YouTube does not. Netflix cannot win this war. What it can do is make each hour of content monetize at twice the efficiency of its rivals—and in 2026 it did. **But that means its ceiling is a "premium paid service," not a "mass entertainment platform."**

## Competitive comparison table: four different ways to operate

The difference among the four companies is not scale but what they make money from. That difference determines how much of a threat each poses to Netflix: **those monetizing subscriptions compete for users, those monetizing advertising compete for viewing time, and those monetizing bundles compete for neither.**

Netflix's position: **it wins on the subscription battlefield, loses on the viewing-time battlefield, and is a novice on the advertising battlefield.** The weighted outcome across the three battlefields is an 18x PE—neither the 40x of a winner nor the 10x of a loser.
