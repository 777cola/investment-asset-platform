## Position in the supply chain: the largest buyer, and the most dangerous seller

Netflix occupies a peculiar position in the value chain: it is both the largest single buyer of content and a direct competitor in content production. It spends tens of billions of dollars a year on content, and every studio wants to do business with it; yet it also produces its own content and competes head-on with those same studios.

That dual identity confers bargaining power but also exposes Netflix to boycott risk. In 2026 it signed a content licensing agreement with Disney and partnered with France's TF1—moves that signal a willingness to pay for breadth rather than chase exclusivity. The logic holds: exclusivity keeps getting more expensive, while breadth is more cost-effective.

Upstream, sports is the soft spot. Sports rights are sold by leagues with monopolistic supply, so Netflix can only buy broadcast rights year by year, never ownership. That amounts to ceding pricing power over part of its profit. The Q4 operating margin guidance of 26.0% reflects, among other things, sports investment.

## Moat breakdown: why switching costs score only 5

This is the most overrated factor. Many investors believe Netflix has a "content library lock-in," but it does not—the cost of cancelling a subscription is zero. All you lose is "a show you're halfway through," and you can come back and finish it anytime.

The real switching cost comes from "habit" and "household decision-making." For a family to switch platforms, they have to convince everyone, find new content and readjust to a new interface. This cost is not money—it's hassle, so it only deters some people. That is what a score of 5 means: there is friction, but it is not strong enough.

Compare Apple: switching phones means migrating data, rebuying apps and reconfiguring everything—the switching cost is "several hundred dollars + a whole day." Netflix's switching cost is "ten minutes + a bit of reluctance." That is why it must keep producing good content—not to raise switching costs, but to keep pushing back the act of "cancelling."

## The most dangerous competitor is YouTube, not Disney

Disney has the IP but not the watch time: Disney+ users "subscribe to finish a series, then leave," and retention hinges on "waiting for the next one." Pricing headroom for this model is only 2%–3%, versus 5%–8% for Netflix.

Amazon has distribution but makes no money from video—Prime Video is a giveaway for Prime members and does not profit from video on a standalone basis. It will not fight a price war, but it also will not win.

Paramount spent about USD 110 billion to buy WBD, funded with debt. Integration will take at least two years, and throughout that period it will have no spare capacity to poach Netflix's users.

Only YouTube has all three at once: zero-marginal-cost content supply, a 14% US viewing share, and a closed loop that converts watch time directly into ad revenue. Netflix pays for every additional hour of content; YouTube does not. Netflix cannot win this war. What it can do is make each hour of content monetise at twice the efficiency of its rivals—and in 2026 it did. But that caps its ceiling at a "premium paid service," not a "mass entertainment platform."

## Competitive comparison: four operating models

What separates the four companies is not scale but their revenue model, and that determines how much of a threat each poses to Netflix: subscription monetisers compete for users, advertising monetisers compete for viewing time, and bundle monetisers compete for neither.

Netflix's position: it wins on the subscription battlefield, loses on viewing time and is a novice in advertising. Weighted across the three, the outcome is an 18x P/E—neither the 40x of a winner nor the 10x of a loser.
