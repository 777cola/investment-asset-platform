The key takeaway from the earnings report: Netflix no longer needs high growth to prove itself

Li Lu reads financial reports by asking where a company sits in its industry's evolution. Netflix's Q3 report sends a clear signal: it has moved from the land-grab phase into the rent-collection phase.

Three pieces of evidence: revenue growth slowed to 11.8% (a three-year low), operating margin rose to 34.6% (a record high), and buybacks reached USD 4.8 billion (a record high). When a company posts its lowest growth, highest margin and largest buyback at the same time, its strategic focus has shifted from growth to returns. This is not decline; it is maturity.

The three stages of industry evolution — and Netflix has reached the third

Streaming has passed through three phases. Phase one (2013–2019) was "burn cash for land": whoever held more content won. Phase two (2020–2023) was "scale effects materialise": margins at companies with large user bases began to climb. Phase three (2024 to now) is "price hikes + advertising + buybacks", as competition shifts from "who grows faster" to "who earns more."

Netflix's three Q3 numbers map exactly onto phase three: growth of 11.8% (no longer chasing 20%), a margin of 34.6% (fully harvesting scale effects) and USD 4.8 billion of buybacks (returning cash to shareholders). These are the standard moves of a mature company, and the inevitable outcome of an industry entering its late stage.

But note: mature does not mean safe. Competition in phase three is fought on "content quality + ad efficiency," and Netflix holds an absolute lead in neither—Disney's IP is stronger on content, and Google's systems are more mature on advertising. Its strength is "doing both reasonably well," which is enough in a mature phase but not enough when technology shifts.

Ad revenue cut exposes the identity problem of "tech company vs content company"

Advertising guidance was cut from USD 3 billion to USD 2.7 billion, with the company citing "ad product features rolling out slower than planned." The subtext: Netflix's execution on ad technology lags its execution on content.

That is normal. No company can lead on two entirely different capabilities at once—content runs on taste and relationships, advertising on data engineering and bidding algorithms. Netflix is world-class at content and a novice at advertising. USD 2.7 billion in revenue equals just 0.4% of Google's ad revenue.

How would Li Lu judge this? He would say: do not value it as an "advertising company," and do not value it as a "content company." It is a "subscription company plus a bit of advertising"; advertising success determines valuation upside, not survival. That judgment matches my base case—in which advertising grows only to about USD 3.5 billion, short of USD 4 billion.

A ten-year view: what kind of company is it most likely to become?

On a ten-year view, Netflix most likely looks like this: 1.2–1.5 billion viewers globally, USD 90–100 billion in annual revenue, a 33%–35% operating margin, advertising at 15%–20% of revenue, and more than USD 20 billion of buybacks a year. That model yields an annualised return of 8%–10% (revenue 6%–7% + margin 1% + buybacks 3% − valuation compression).

That is a "bond substitute" return, not a "ten-bagger" return. The reason to buy it is certainty, not imagination. For most people, buying a certain 8%–10% asset at 18x P/E beats buying one that might return 15% or might return 0 at 30x P/E.

Li Lu would close with one question: if it really looks like this in ten years, are you willing to buy at USD 65.85? My answer is yes — USD 65.85 corresponds to 18x P/E, and a certain 8%–10% return is reasonable, even low, at that multiple. That is the ultimate basis for "buy, in tranches."

Conclusion

What matters about this earnings report on a ten-year view is simple: it proves Netflix has already completed the shift from a "growth story" to a "cash flow asset." The market still frames the company the old way, yet prices it as the new one—that is the mismatch. The mismatch will correct, but it takes time—roughly two to three years. Hence the call is "Buy, in tranches" rather than "Buy, full position."
