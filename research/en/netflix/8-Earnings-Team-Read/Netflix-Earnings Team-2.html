Masterclass Close Read (Buffett): Converting Profit Back into Cash — Netflix

Of the RMB 3.664 billion net profit, how much can actually reach shareholders' pockets?
Research date 2026/10/23  ·  Price $65.85 (-1.79%)  ·  Market cap About USD 270 billion  ·  PE(2026E) ~18x (including one-off gains, ~21x excluding them)
← Back to the hub

One line: the RMB 12 billion free cash flow is real, but buybacks are spending faster than it earns

Buffett does one thing when reading financial statements: convert accounting profit back into "cash shareholders can take out." Netflix Q3 net income was USD 3.664 billion, operating cash flow USD 3.162 billion, free cash flow USD 2.884 billion. All three numbers fall in sequence, and the decline is content spending.

The conclusion after that conversion: the company can generate USD 12 billion of free cash flow a year (full-year guidance), which is 4.4% of its USD 270 billion market cap. That yield is not high, but paired with 6%–7% annual share cancellation, total return can reach about 10%—and that 10% does not require the stock price to rise.

First step: align net profit with cash flow

Net profit RMB 3.664 billion, operating cash flow RMB 3.162 billion — a gap of RMB 502 million. A normal company should have cash flow above profit (depreciation and amortization cost no cash), but Netflix is the reverse. The reason is content accounting: spending is capitalized and amortized by viewership; the income statement deducts amortization, while cash flow reflects cash out the door.

This is not fraud, but it implies a risk: if content cash spending substantially exceeds amortization in a given year, profit is overstated. How to check? Look at the annual ratio. 2026 FCF RMB 12 billion ÷ normalized net profit RMB 12.9 billion = 93%, healthy. Historically this ratio has held between 85% and 100% over the long run.

So my conclusion: the RMB 500 million gap in Q3 is quarterly noise; the annual 93% is the signal. That is also why I repeatedly use FCF rather than net profit as the valuation anchor in this report — cash flow is harder to dress up through accounting.

Second step: calculate "the cash shareholders can take out"

Not all of the RMB 12 billion in free cash flow is distributable. Maintenance content spending must be deducted — the portion where "if you don't spend it, you lose customers." This figure is not publicly disclosed and can only be assessed qualitatively: top-tier drama series, sports rights and localized content all fall into the category of "stop for a year and subscribers leave."

A rough reference: Netflix's content amortization roughly matches its cash spending each year, indicating no obvious "investment backlog." If amortization ever falls materially below cash spending, it is borrowing from the future; conversely, amortization above cash spending means building inventory. The two currently match, which is a good sign.

So of the RMB 12 billion in FCF, I estimate at least RMB 8–9 billion is genuinely distributable (rough calculation, net of the excess portion of maintenance investment). Against a market cap of RMB 270 billion, that is a distributable yield of 3%–3.3%; adding 6%–7% from buyback-driven share cancellation, total annualized return is 9%–10%. That number is not spectacular, but it is what you get under the assumption that nothing happens.

Third action: watch how management spends the money

Q3 buybacks totaled USD 4.8 billion at an average price of USD 75.12 (rough calculation), 14% above the current price. Buying back stock at 18x PE is a move Buffett endorses—he has always held that when the share price is below intrinsic value, buybacks are better capital allocation than acquisitions.

But the scale went too far. Buybacks were 1.66x FCF, with the gap covered by cash and debt. Buffett's stance on "borrowing to buy back stock" is clear: permissible, but it cannot become the norm. His preferred cadence is "annual buybacks equal to free cash flow," so that net debt does not rise.

Another positive move was abandoning the USD 82.7 billion WBD acquisition in February 2026. Had the deal gone through, Netflix would have taken on over USD 80 billion in debt to buy a bundle of declining cable channels. Walking away shows management knows where its boundaries lie. I rate this highly—most media companies have stumbled on exactly this.

Conclusion

Stripped back to cash, Netflix is a company that "generates USD 12 billion of free cash flow a year, buys back USD 19 billion of its own stock, and carries USD 7.1 billion of net debt." That structure is a good deal at 18x PE and a bad deal above 25x. Buffett's bid would land somewhere between USD 50 and USD 58 (14–16x normalized PE), 12%–24% below the current price—that is the difference between "fair" and "cheap."
