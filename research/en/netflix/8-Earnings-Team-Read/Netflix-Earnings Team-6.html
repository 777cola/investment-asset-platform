The market watches growth; the earnings report talks margins

On the day Netflix reported 2026Q3 results, the stock neither crashed nor rose. A week later it fell to a two-year low of USD 65.85, down about 29% year to date and 39.8% from its post-split high in November 2025. The market's verdict is that growth is over. I think that verdict is aimed at the wrong target.

The report did contain three pieces of bad news: revenue growth of 11.8% was the slowest in three years, advertising revenue was cut from USD 3 billion to USD 2.7 billion, and full-year revenue guidance landed at the low end of the original range. But on the same day, the company delivered a 34.6% operating margin, net income up 44% year on year, and a record USD 4.8 billion of single-quarter buybacks. A company whose growth is over would not do all three at once.

The number that got ignored: 31.5%

Everyone is discussing the 11.8% growth rate; no one is discussing the 31.5% operating margin guidance. What that number means: Netflix's 2026 operating margin is 2 percentage points above 2025's 29.5%, and this is already the third consecutive year of expansion.

No other company in the media industry has achieved this. Disney's operating margin has sat at 10%–15% for years, and the streaming businesses of Paramount and WBD have only just turned profitable. Netflix turns one company's profit into the entire industry's profit—the reason is no mystery: its subscriber base is three times everyone else's, so content, a fixed cost, is spread the thinnest.

More critically, this 2-percentage-point expansion was achieved on the back of price increases. Netflix raised prices in 2026, and churn fell year over year. Raising prices without losing customers is the hardest evidence of a moat—more reliable than market share or content volume.

Ad cut revised down, but the market overread it

Cut from USD 3 billion to USD 2.7 billion, a 10% downgrade, and the market read it as proof the second curve had failed. But USD 2.7 billion is only 5.3% of full-year revenue. The miss hurts the valuation, not the profit.

And the company was clear about why it cut: some ad product features rolled out slower than planned. Translated, that means "the tech isn't finished," not "advertisers stopped buying." One corroboration: alongside the cut, the company firmed up its 2027 expansion from "planned" to "15 countries live by March 2027." If demand had truly collapsed, the right move would be to contract, not expand.

Skepticism is still warranted, of course. Advertising is the one battlefield where Netflix faces stronger opponents — Google's and Meta's systems have run for fifteen years, while Netflix's ad tech stack has only just hooked up programmatic buying (MercadoLibre, AdForm). USD 2.7 billion is entry level in this industry. Treat it as a side dish, not the main course.

What should really be feared is organizational lag

On October 13, the two co-CEOs sent an internal letter announcing layoffs of about 6%, or close to 1,000 people, the largest since 2022. In the same round of changes, Chief Content Officer Bela Bajaria was replaced and film division head Dan Lin departed.

This is not routine optimization; it is a structural adjustment. Content acquisition and film production are Netflix's two main arteries, and both changed leadership within a year. The danger lies in the lag: a series takes 12–18 months from greenlight to launch, so a change of content chief means the 2027 slate will be affected, and a thinner slate will only show up in churn in 2028.

By then, the stock price will have already fallen.This is the hardest kind of risk for investors to handle: not invisible, but visible and impossible to disprove in the short term. So I give management only 6 points, and I have written one observation line into the report as a hard test—whether the 2027 operating margin can hold at 32%. If it holds, the cuts removed redundancy; if it does not, the cuts removed muscle.

The price of this deal: 18x, buying certainty

USD 65.85 corresponds to 18x 2026E PE, or about 21x excluding the one-off gain in Q1. Netflix's PE has ranged between 18x and 45x since 2023; it now sits at the bottom of that range.

Another way to look at it: free cash flow of USD 12 billion is 4.4% of the USD 270 billion market cap; annualized Q3 buybacks of about USD 19 billion are 7% of market cap.Together, even with the valuation multiple unchanged, per-share value rises 6%–7% a year.If the multiple returns to 22x, that is an extra gift.

Conclusion

In one sentence: the market has priced "growth slowing from 15% to 12%" as "the growth story is over," while the earnings report says "the profit story is just beginning." That mismatch will not correct immediately, but 18x PE, a 4.4% FCF yield and a 6%–7% annual cancellation rate already offer enough time value. Buy, in tranches, and let it prove itself.
