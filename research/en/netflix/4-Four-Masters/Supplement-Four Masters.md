## Duan Yongping: The essence of this business is whether people are willing to pay every month

Duan Yongping would first file it under "subscription businesses with pricing power." The test is not market share, but **whether it can raise prices repeatedly without users leaving**. Netflix raised prices again in 2026, and on its earnings call the company said "user response was broadly consistent with previous price increases," with churn even falling year over year. That evidence is harder than any share data—it shows users are not comparing prices, they are comparing "what do I watch at night without it."

But he would immediately press a second question: does this business have to keep spending money? The answer is yes. Stop investing in content for a year and subscribers fall the next year. This differs from Apple: Apple can pause R&D for a year and still be Apple. **So what Netflix is worth depends on whether it can turn content spending into reusable assets**—an IP library (properties like Stranger Things that can be licensed repeatedly), sports rights (NFL, boxing, which lock in specific audiences), and localized content (India's Lock Upp and the like, low cost and high stickiness).

His conclusion would roughly be: **understandable, a good business, but a good business that "must keep running," not one that "collects money lying down."** A company like this cannot command too high a valuation multiple; 18–22x is reasonable, and above 30x is a bet that it never stops.

## Buffett: a 4.4% cash flow yield, plus a share-cancelling machine

Buffett would run two numbers first. First, free cash flow yield: 2026 guidance of USD 12 billion against a market cap of USD 270 billion is **4.4%**. Second, the pace of share cancellation: Q3 buybacks of USD 4.8 billion and 63.9 million shares, annualized at roughly USD 19 billion, is 7% of the USD 270 billion market cap.

Put those two numbers together and you get the structure behind his line about "making money even if nothing happens": **the company earns USD 12 billion of cash a year and spends USD 19 billion buying back its own stock, so even with the valuation multiple going nowhere, per-share value rises 6%–7% a year.** If the multiple re-rates from 18x back to 22x, that is an extra gift, not a requirement.

He would hesitate in two places. First, debt: USD 15.3 billion of debt, USD 8.2 billion of cash, USD 7.1 billion net debt — not much, but not net cash like Apple. Second, buybacks exceeding FCF: Q3 buybacks of USD 4.8 billion against quarterly FCF of only USD 2.884 billion, with the gap covered by balance-sheet cash and debt. **He likes buybacks, but not buybacks funded with borrowed money.** That point would push his bid a little lower — he would only feel there is a "genuine margin of safety" around USD 55.

## Munger: invert the question — under what circumstances does it fall from 66 to 45?

Munger would not ask "how high can it go"; he would ask "under what circumstances does it halve". Three triggers, ranked by probability:

- **Revenue growth falls below 8% in 2027**, and the market de-rates the valuation to 14–15x. This is not a low-probability event—Q3 was already 11.8%, and advertising guidance was cut again.
- **Advertising fails to become a second growth curve within two years**, with RMB 2.7 billion stalled in place. This is already happening, but because advertising is only 5.3% of revenue, it weighs on the valuation, not on profit.
- **Content costs resume rising**, with sports live rights (NFL, boxing) and top-tier series pushing the operating margin back down to 28%. This is the most dangerous trigger because it hits both profit and the narrative at once.

His verdict would be: **the first two are already half priced in; the third is not.** The market is worried about growth now, not margins. If a report ever shows "revenue +10%, operating margin down 2 percentage points", that is when the real de-rating hits—because it would mean Netflix has started buying growth to protect growth.

## Li Lu: will it still charge subscription fees in ten years?

Li Lu takes the longest view. He starts with an industry fact: **global TV advertising runs about USD 170 billion a year and is migrating systematically to streaming.** This migration is not a cycle but a structural shift, with a decade still to run. Netflix is the only player that combines three things at once—global distribution (more than 190 countries), in-house content capability (non-English content accounts for over a third of viewing), and an ad-tech stack (Netflix Ads Suite already connected to programmatic).

But he would point to a structural constraint: **Netflix's rival in the watch-time battle is not Disney but YouTube.** US viewing share is 7.8% versus 14%, and YouTube's marginal content cost is zero. Netflix pays for every additional hour of content, which means it can only ever defend premium dayparts with "prestige" and cannot win the watch-time war with "infinite supply."

This constraint means it should not command growth-stock valuation. So Li Lu's conclusion is not "don't buy" but "buy at the right price": **a company that can capture 15%–20% of the USD 170 billion TV ad pool over ten years (USD 25–34 billion), paired with a 30% operating margin, is worth about 20x PE.** At 18x today, it trades below that. His point of disagreement is timing—he thinks this repricing takes three years, not three months.

## Odds assessment: downside is set by valuation, upside is set by buybacks

Three scenarios: 45 / 79 / 107, versus 65.85, implying −32% / +20% / +62%. On the surface the payoff looks ordinary (a neutral 1:0.6 odds ratio), but the shape of the probability distribution works in our favor: **the neutral scenario requires only that "nothing changes"** — 12% growth, a 31.5% margin, continued buybacks — and all three were already delivered in 2026.

The bear case requires two things to happen at once: growth falling to 8% and the margin dropping back to 30%. These two rarely arrive together — if growth falls, management's first response is to step up buybacks and raise prices, so the margin need not fall; if the margin falls, it is usually because content spend rises, and revenue holds up instead. So USD 45 is a conservative floor; I put the true probability at 25%.

One buffer that is often overlooked: **authorization remaining USD 22.3 billion, equal to 8.2% of market cap.** The lower the share price, the more shares the same money buys back, and the better per-share value looks. This is the only structure in which "a falling share price improves the fundamentals." All four masters understand this layer, which is why none of them rates the stock "avoid."

The bull case requires advertising to break USD 4 billion, sports to lift time spent, and the margin to reach 33%. Advertising was already half-disproven in 2026 (USD 3 billion to USD 2.7 billion), so I put the probability at 20%. **Neutral 55%, bear 25%, bull 20%, expected value = 0.55×79 + 0.25×45 + 0.2×107 = USD 76.1, +16% versus the current price.** That expected value is not spectacular, but it is calculated on the premise that "no good news is needed" — which is the reason to buy in tranches.
