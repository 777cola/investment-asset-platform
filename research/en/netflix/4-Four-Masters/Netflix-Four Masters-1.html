Four Masters' Consensus: A Good Business at a Fair Price — What's Missing Is Patience

Duan Yongping goes to the essence of the business: "a subscription business with pricing power, but content must be continuously invested in." Buffett runs the cash flow numbers — a 4.4% FCF yield plus a 6%–7% buyback rate is a structure he likes. Munger flips the question to "what would make it fall another 30%", and the answer is growth dropping below 8% or advertising becoming completely unworkable. Li Lu takes a ten-year view: the global TV advertising pool of USD 170 billion still has ten years of migration to streaming, but Netflix cannot win the watch-time war.

All four converge on the same answer: you can buy, just don't buy it all at once. Three-scenario valuation — bear case USD 45, base case USD 79, bull case USD 107, against the current price of USD 65.85, that is −32% / +20% / +62%. The payoff ratio isn't pretty; the win rate in the base case only requires that "nothing changes".

Duan Yongping: the essence of this business is whether people are willing to pay every month

Duan Yongping would file it first under "subscription businesses with pricing power." The test is not market share but whether it can raise prices repeatedly without users leaving. Netflix raised prices again in 2026 and said on its earnings call that "user response was broadly consistent with previous price increases," with churn even falling YoY. That evidence is harder than any share data—it shows users are not comparing prices, they are comparing "what do I watch at night without it."

He would immediately press a second question: does this business have to keep spending? Yes. Stop investing in content for a year and subscribers fall the next. This differs from Apple: Apple can pause R&D for a year and still be Apple. So what Netflix is worth depends on whether it can turn content spending into reusable assets—an IP library (properties like Stranger Things that can be licensed repeatedly), sports rights (NFL, boxing, which lock in specific audiences), and localised content (India's Lock Upp and the like, low cost and high stickiness).

His conclusion would be roughly: understandable, a good business, but a good business that "must keep running," not one that "collects money lying down." A company like this cannot command too high a valuation multiple; 18–22x is reasonable, and above 30x is a bet that it never stops.

Buffett: 4.4% free cash flow yield, plus a share-cancelling machine

Buffett would run two numbers first. Free cash flow yield: 2026 guidance of USD 12 billion against a market cap of USD 270 billion is 4.4%. Pace of share cancellation: Q3 buybacks of USD 4.8 billion and 63.9 million shares, annualised at roughly USD 19 billion, equal 7% of the USD 270 billion market cap.

Together those two numbers explain his line about "making money even if nothing happens": the company generates USD 12 billion of cash a year and spends USD 19 billion buying back its own stock, so even with the multiple going nowhere, per-share value rises 6%–7% a year. A re-rating from 18x back to 22x would be an extra gift, not a requirement.

He would hesitate in two places. First, debt: USD 15.3 billion of debt against USD 8.2 billion of cash leaves USD 7.1 billion of net debt — modest, but not net cash like Apple. Second, buybacks exceeding FCF: Q3 buybacks of USD 4.8 billion against quarterly FCF of only USD 2.884 billion, with the gap covered by balance-sheet cash and debt. He likes buybacks, but not buybacks funded with borrowed money. That would push his bid a little lower — he would only feel there is a "genuine margin of safety" around USD 55.

Munger's inversion: what would have to happen for the stock to fall from 66 to 45?

Munger would not ask "how high can it go"; he would ask "under what circumstances does it halve". Three triggers, ranked by probability:

  • Revenue growth falls below 8% in 2027, and the market de-rates the stock to 14–15x. This is not a low-probability event—Q3 was already 11.8%, and advertising guidance was cut again.
  • Advertising fails to become a second growth curve within two years, with RMB 2.7 billion stalled in place. This is already happening, but because advertising is only 5.3% of revenue, it weighs on the valuation, not on profit.
  • Content costs resume rising, with sports live rights (NFL, boxing) and top-tier series pushing the operating margin back down to 28%. This is the most dangerous trigger because it hits both profit and the narrative at once.

His verdict would be: the first two are already half priced in; the third is not. The market is worried about growth now, not margins. If a report ever shows "revenue +10%, operating margin down 2 percentage points", that is when the real de-rating hits—because it would mean Netflix has started buying growth to protect growth.

Li Lu: will it still charge subscription fees in ten years?

Li Lu takes the longest view. He starts with an industry fact: global TV advertising runs about USD 170 billion a year and is migrating systematically to streaming. This is not a cycle but a structural shift with a decade still to run. Netflix is the only player that combines three things at once—global distribution (more than 190 countries), in-house content capability (non-English content accounts for over a third of viewing), and an ad-tech stack (Netflix Ads Suite already connected to programmatic).

He would, however, point to a structural constraint: Netflix's rival in the watch-time battle is not Disney but YouTube. US viewing share is 7.8% versus 14%, and YouTube's marginal content cost is zero. Netflix pays for every additional hour of content, so it can defend premium dayparts only with "prestige" and cannot win the watch-time war with "infinite supply."

That constraint argues against a growth-stock valuation. Hence Li Lu's conclusion is not "don't buy" but "buy at the right price": a company that can capture 15%–20% of the USD 170 billion TV ad pool over ten years (USD 25–34 billion), paired with a 30% operating margin, is worth about 20x P/E. At 18x today, it trades below that. His point of disagreement is timing—he thinks this repricing takes three years, not three months.

Valuation sets the downside; buybacks set the upside

Three scenarios — 45 / 79 / 107 against 65.85 — imply −32% / +20% / +62%. On the surface the payoff looks ordinary, a neutral 1:0.6 odds ratio, but the shape of the distribution works in our favour: the neutral scenario requires only that "nothing changes" — 12% growth, a 31.5% margin, continued buybacks — and all three were already delivered in 2026.

The bear case needs two things to happen at once: growth falling to 8% and the margin dropping back to 30%. These rarely arrive together. If growth falls, management's first response is to step up buybacks and raise prices, so the margin need not fall; if the margin falls, it is usually because content spend rises, and revenue holds up instead. USD 45 is therefore a conservative floor; we put the true probability at 25%.

One buffer is often overlooked: authorisation remaining USD 22.3 billion, equal to 8.2% of market cap. The lower the share price, the more shares the same money buys back, and the better per-share value looks. This is the only structure in which "a falling share price improves the fundamentals." All four masters understand this layer, which is why none of them rates the stock "avoid."

The bull case requires advertising to break USD 4 billion, sports to lift time spent, and the margin to reach 33%. Advertising was already half-disproven in 2026 (USD 3 billion to USD 2.7 billion), so we put the probability at 20%. Neutral 55%, bear 25%, bull 20%, expected value = 0.55×79 + 0.25×45 + 0.2×107 = USD 76.1, +16% versus the current price. That expected value is not spectacular, but it rests on the premise that "no good news is needed" — which is the reason to buy in tranches.

Conclusion

The four masters agree on the fundamentals: a good business at a reasonable price, though not cheap enough to go all in. They differ only on time horizon — Duan Yongping looks three to five years out, Buffett at cash-flow returns, Munger fixates on the worst case, Li Lu ten years out. Every horizon yields the same action: open a first position of one-third at the current price, add a second tranche below USD 55, complete the position near USD 45. If 2027 guidance cuts growth to single digits, all four turn to "wait" at once — the only collective veto condition.
