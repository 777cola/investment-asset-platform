Cross-validation: the numbers are clean; the definitions are what's messy

We cross-checked eight key metrics against two sources: the company's original filings and media reports (Zhitong Finance, Reuters alerts). The largest deviation was 1.2%, on earnings per share, caused by rounding after the stock split: a one-cent gap between 0.87 and 0.86 that exceeds 1% when expressed as a percentage. The other seven metrics all deviate by less than 0.3%, with revenue, operating profit and advertising guidance matching exactly.

The real issue is not the deviation but the basis of calculation: stock splits, one-off gains, and the method of recognizing advertising revenue. Leave any one of these three unclear and the same company can yield three different P/Es. This report uniformly adopts the post-split, normalized, as-reported basis, explained item by item below.

Three measurement bases need clarifying first; otherwise the numbers are meaningless

First, the stock split. Netflix's board approved a 10-for-1 stock split on 2025/10/30, with a record date of 2025/11/10 and post-split trading from the open on 2025/11/17. Every per-share figure in this report is therefore on a post-split basis: the USD 65.85 share price, diluted EPS of USD 0.87, the 52-week high of USD 109.35 and the USD 75 average buyback price. For pre-split equivalents, multiply by 10: share price USD 658.5, 52-week high USD 1,093.5. The USD 0.59 EPS for 2025Q3 is the restated, comparable-basis figure from the financials, not the originally reported value. This is the easiest place to go wrong: much historical material still sits on a pre-split basis, and comparing the two produces the absurd conclusion that the stock "fell 90%".

Second, the one-off gain. 2026Q1 net income was USD 5.28 billion, up 83% YoY, on revenue growth of just 16%. Profit growth at five times revenue growth can only come from non-operating items. The earnings summary does not break out the composition, so this report does not quantify the gain and instead runs two bases: 18x PE (including the one-off gain) and 21x PE (normalized, excluding it). Wherever valuation is involved, use the 21x tier; where the question is "what price is the market assigning", use 18x.

Third, how advertising revenue is recognized. The USD 2.7 billion is "revenue recognized for the full year", not "advertiser commitments" (bookings). In the ad industry these two numbers often differ by more than 20%, because commitments can be recognized across periods and can be cancelled. When the company cut guidance, it cited "some ad product features rolling out slower than planned", pointing to inventory delivery capacity rather than a collapse in demand—this distinction determines whether the cut is an "execution problem" or a "demand problem"; the former is fixable, the latter is not.

The sole line item with a deviation above 1%, and why it does not matter

EPS 0.87 versus 0.86, a 1.2% deviation — the only one of the eight items above the line. The explanation is simple: net income of RMB 3.664 billion ÷ 4.211 billion diluted shares = USD 0.8700, so rounding to either 0.87 or 0.86 is defensible for the media. This is a printing-precision issue, not a data-quality issue. We therefore use the reported 0.87 and retain two decimal places in all EPS-derived calculations to avoid error accumulation.

Cross-check: RMB 3.664 billion net income ÷ USD 0.87 EPS = 4.211 billion diluted shares, 100 million shares more than the 4.11 billion total shares. The gap reflects the time-weighted effect of buybacks during Q3 — the 639,000 shares repurchased occurred at different points within the quarter, and diluted shares use a weighted average. The two figures reconcile, confirming the financial statements are internally consistent.

Conversely, an EPS of 0.86 would imply 4.26 billion shares, 150 million above total shares — a deviation too large to explain. So 0.87 is the only self-consistent value. This kind of cross-check is more useful than dual-source comparison: it validates the internal logic of the financial statements, not whether two media outlets copied correctly.

The finally adopted methodology

This series applies four conventions throughout: share price and per-share data are on a post-split basis; profit figures are as reported in the financial statements; valuation uses normalized P/E; market cap is USD 65.85 × 4.11 billion shares = USD 270.6 billion (written as "about 270 billion").

One figure I have chosen not to provide is total content spending. The earnings summary does not break out cash content spending for 2026Q3; it can only be inferred from the change in "content assets", and the error would be too large. Wherever this report touches on content costs, it uses qualitative language ("content spending is the largest cash outflow item") without a specific amount. Better to be one number short than to give a number I cannot verify myself.

Subscriber count is another. The company now discloses "audience reach" and revenue by region; it has not reported precise paid-member numbers quarterly since 2026. This report therefore says "reaching nearly 1 billion viewers" rather than "300 million paid members." A comparison built on inconsistent definitions is worse than no comparison at all.

Conclusion

The data holds up. Across eight metrics, two-source deviation is ≤1.2%, with the sole outlier explained by rounding; internal cross-checks in the financials (net profit ÷ EPS = diluted share count) are self-consistent. These numbers are safe to base judgments on. One caveat: much of the material in the market still uses the pre-split basis, so multiply or divide by 10 before any cross-source comparison. This report uses the post-split basis throughout, effective after 2025/11/17.
