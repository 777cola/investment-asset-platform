## Why the cap is 6%, not 10%

The single-name cap is usually set at 10%, but Netflix gets a 30% haircut. There is only one reason: **management scores 6 out of 10.** Three organizational moves in a year, with the content chief and film head both departing at the same time — the cost of these things only becomes visible 18 months later. **Risks you cannot see clearly must be covered by position size, not by judgment.**

Another reason is correlation. Netflix and the tech stocks in the portfolio (especially ad-driven Meta and Alphabet) move in the same direction on the "ad budget cycle," so holding both at once amplifies the same risk. **With 6% Netflix plus the existing tech positions, actual ad-cycle exposure may already exceed 15%.**

And a third reason: its payoff structure is "downside 32%, upside 20% (base case)." **That payoff profile does not suit a heavy position; it suits a medium position plus adding on dips.** A heavy position requires upside far exceeding downside, and that is not the case here.

## How is the add-position price level calculated?

USD 55 corresponds to 15x normalized PE (55 ÷ 3.66). **15x is the absolute bottom of Netflix's PE range since 2023**, historically touched only briefly during the 2022 net subscriber decline. At this level, the market has already priced in "growth slowing to 8%."

USD 45 corresponds to the bear case (EPS 3.0 × 15x). **This price implies three things happening at once: "advertising fails outright + margins roll over + 8% growth."** I put the probability at 25%, but if it gets there, the payoff becomes "10% downside, 75% upside" — worth going all in.

No mechanical adding between the two levels. **If it falls to 55 because earnings blow up (say, revenue below 13.3 billion), that's not an opportunity, that's evidence.** The precondition for adding is "fundamentals intact," not "price reached."

## Set a thesis stop, not a price stop

This stock should not be stopped out on price. **The reason is its volatility is too high — down 29% year to date and 39.8% from its high, any 15% price stop would be triggered repeatedly.** The purpose of a stop-loss is to control losses, not to control volatility.

The right approach is a thesis stop: **of the five assumptions written at purchase, if any one is disproven, trim the position.** For example, single-digit revenue growth guidance for 2027 would mean the assumption that "price increases plus advertising sustain double-digit growth" is broken, so cut to 2%; a 2027 operating margin falling below 30% would mean "costs are controllable" is broken, so exit entirely.

The cost of this method is slow reaction — earnings come four times a year and events a few times a year, so stops are always lagging. **But it avoids the risk of being washed out by noise, and the latter does greater damage to long-term holding.**

## Correlation with other holdings: calculate the three exposures separately

**Advertising-cycle exposure.** Moves with Meta and Alphabet—when advertiser budgets are strong, all three do well; when budgets are weak, all three do poorly. Advertising is only 5.3% of Netflix's business, so exposure is small, but the direction is the same.

**Content and IP-cycle exposure.** More often inverse than aligned with Disney—they compete for the same users' subscription fees. Holding Disney alongside means running a hedge, but neither side makes money.

**Rate exposure.** Negatively correlated. Netflix's valuation rests on discounted cash flow, so rising rates compress the multiple; its debt is fixed-rate, locking in interest costs, so rising rates barely affect profit but hit valuation hard. **If the portfolio already holds a lot of long-duration assets (e.g., high-multiple software stocks), adding Netflix increases rate sensitivity.**
