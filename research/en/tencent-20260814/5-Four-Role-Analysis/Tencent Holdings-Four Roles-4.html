Risk Assessment (Li Lu's perspective) — Tencent Holdings

Five main risks and one tail risk — which is most likely to actually impair capital
Research date 2026/08/14  ·  Price HK$440.00 (-0.23%)  ·  Market cap Approximately HKD 4.01 trillion  ·  P/E (TTM) Approximately 15.0x

Core view

The risk is not "WeChat being replaced" — that is highly unlikely. The real risks are three: RMB 52.784 billion of capex with no return, depreciation lives proving too optimistic, and advertising growth stalling with the macro. All three point to the same word: cash flow.

Judgement: each risk is bearable on its own; the probability of two occurring at once is about 25%. That is the mathematical reason to "buy in tranches" rather than "buy fully invested".

Risk 1: Where are the returns on RMB 52.7 billion of capex?

This is the number-one risk because it hits both the income statement and the cash flow statement: RMB 52.784 billion in a single quarter and RMB 84.72 billion in the first half, which management calls "a one-off startup investment in new AI-native businesses." "One-off" is the three-word phrase in this report that most needs verification.

The test is simple. If capex falls back below RMB 30 billion per quarter in 2027 while AI-related revenue (cloud plus WorkBuddy and others) grows above 30%, it was an investment; if capex is still above RMB 50 billion in 2027 and AI revenue is still not disclosed separately, it was an expense. Same money, two characterisations, a 30% valuation gap.

Risk 2: Depreciation is a slow-burning fuse

RMB 50 billion a quarter of compute assets will ultimately flow through to depreciation. On a 5-year weighted life, RMB 190 billion of annual capex implies roughly RMB 38 billion of incremental depreciation in 2027; if auditors require GPUs to be depreciated over 3 years, that figure rises by a further RMB 13 billion. At a 25% tax rate, the hit to net profit ranges from RMB 14 billion to RMB 27 billion, or 4.4% to 8.5% of 2027 Non-IFRS net profit.

The market is already debating this. A group of tech companies depreciates compute assets over longer lives, and once hardware obsolescence runs faster than expected, profits will be systematically revised down in the years ahead. Tencent's depreciation policy is not currently in question, but its capex is now so large that the depreciation assumption has itself become an independent variable.

Risk 3: Advertising is the only segment accelerating, and the most fragile

Marketing services grew 22% and generated almost all of the incremental profit this quarter, but it rests on three variables: macro consumption, Reels inventory and advertiser budgets. Only one of the three, inventory, sits within Tencent's control.

If ad growth slows to 8%, on a quarterly base of RMB 43.565 billion, that implies roughly RMB 24 billion less revenue and about RMB 10 billion less net profit a year. That is not fatal in itself, but it would lift a 13x P/E to 14.5x—and in a year of negative free cash flow, the market has very little tolerance for a "growth story."

Risk 4: Games' high base and licence approvals

Domestic games' +17% rests on four titles surging at once, which makes the 2027 comparable base very ugly. Tencent's response is its pipeline: Shikong Jinhua, launched in July, passed 40 million pre-registrations and topped the iOS free chart after release, but whether new titles can absorb the revenue of older ones will only be clear in the first half of 2027.

Licensing is a standing risk, not a sudden one. Tencent's licence approval cadence has been stable over the past three years; I assign this "low probability, medium impact".

Risk 5: Regulation and major shareholders — slow-moving variables that do not disappear

Regulation is a structural issue for Tencent: game licences, payment compliance, platform liability and minor protection. Tighter rules on any one of these compress the valuation rather than the profit. They show up as a lower valuation centre, not as an earnings blow-up.

Major shareholder Prosus/MIH holds 22.8%, down 1.21 percentage points YoY; a slow long-term selldown is a common valuation drag on Hong Kong-listed Chinese stocks. Fundamentals are unchanged, but any re-rating is slowed.

Tail risks: three things happening at once

Bear case: the global AI trade keeps cooling, Tencent's AI revenue stays buried inside the segment, and advertising stalls with the macro.If all three land together, 2027 Non-IFRS net profit could be just RMB 260 billion; at 11x that implies HKD 330, 25% below the current price.

I assign this scenario a 10%–15% probability. It needs no black swan—only the continued deterioration of three trends already underway, which is precisely why it is worth writing down.

Sizing a position starts with the risk question: how much can you afford to lose?

Li Lu starts with how much you can lose: tail scenario HKD 330 (-25%), probability 10%–15%; pessimistic HKD 370 (-16%), probability 30%. Combined, that is a 40%–45% probability of losing 16%–25% first.

A position you can hold through that drawdown is 8%; if you cannot, it is 4%. And whether you can hold through it turns not on courage but on portfolio structure—if the portfolio already holds a pile of China internet names, Tencent's marginal risk is very high.

His advice, then: first look at how much "China internet beta" the portfolio carries, then decide where this 4% comes from. If already overweight, swap out something else rather than adding to total exposure.

Conclusion

Risk conclusion: no single risk invalidates this business, but three can compress its valuation. Capex returns, depreciation and an advertising slowdown all tie back to cash, so monitoring cash flow is sufficient.

Positioning implications: tail scenario -25% at 10%–15% probability; neutral +22% at 50%. That distribution argues for building the position in tranches — neither going all in at once nor staying out entirely.
