Four Masters — Tencent Holdings

Core view

All four would buy, but for four almost entirely different reasons: Duan Yongping buys "an entrance that can't be replaced," Buffett buys "57.8% gross margin + 13x PE," Munger buys "a good company down 30%" but wants room to spare, and Li Lu buys "the infrastructure of Chinese digital consumption."

The disagreement is not whether to buy but how much. Munger and Li Lu would both insist on scaling in, because they cannot pin down the payback period on RMB 52.784 billion of capex; Duan Yongping would say HKD 440 is still not cheap enough, though he concedes it is a level at which one can start. The joint conclusion: buy, half position initially, add to full on a break below 400.

Duan Yongping: the business will still be around in ten years, and the price is cheap enough

Duan Yongping's first question is always: "will this company still be here in ten years?" Tencent's answer is firmer than most. WeChat has 1.439 billion MAU, with contacts, groups, payments and mini-programs all growing off the same relationship chain. Replacing WeChat is not a technology problem but a social one — more than a billion people would have to switch messaging tools at once, and history offers no successful precedent.

His second question is "is this a good business?" He looks at two things: gross margin and capex. A gross margin of 57.8%, still rising YoY, signals pricing power; capex of RMB 52.784 billion, 25.8% of revenue, signals the business is also getting heavier. The logic behind his words is roughly this: a good business has gotten heavier, but as long as it is still worth this price, it is still a good business.

The third question is price. At HKD 440 and 13.2x 2026E Non-IFRS, against valuations above 30x in 2021, he would say "this price is a level to start buying at," not "go all in." Duan Yongping's approach has always been to buy a little first and add only once things are clearer, because he knows he can be wrong too.

Buffett: 57.8% gross margin at 13x P/E is a rare quote

The first lesson Buffett drew from See's Candies was pricing power: a business with pricing power sees gross margin rise on its own. In a year when Tencent doubled capex, raised R&D by 35% and lifted administrative expenses by 22%, gross margin still rose from 56.8% to 57.8% — a health check on pricing power, and one it passed.

The second lesson is capital allocation. In 2025 it bought back HKD 80 billion of stock and paid a dividend of HKD 5.30 per share; in the first half of 2026 it bought back a further HKD 24.4 billion, while explicitly refusing equity financing. How a company spends money is more credible than what it says. Buybacks clustered roughly in the HKD 400–500 range imply management sees that range as below intrinsic value — whether that judgment is right is another matter, but at least they sit on the same side as shareholders.

The third lesson is price. A market cap of HKD 4.01 trillion against 2026E Non-IFRS profit of about RMB 280 billion puts it at just over 13x. Buffett would pay 20x for a business like this, provided he "understands" it. He does not understand Tencent's AI piece, so he would apply a discount — and 13x is precisely the discount the market has already applied on his behalf.

Munger: invert the question — does the RMB 52.7 billion capex become a bottomless pit?

Munger's approach is to invert: under what circumstances would this investment lose me a lot of money? The answer is not WeChat being displaced (extremely low probability), but this RMB 52.784 billion turning into a sunk cost. Single-quarter capex was RMB 52.784 billion, +176% YoY and +65% QoQ, while capex cash payments reached RMB 59.3 billion — already above operating cash flow of RMB 52.710 billion.

He would press on three details: what will these GPUs be worth in three years? If Hy4 disappoints, how large is the impairment? Is the depreciation life being stretched too long? Industry participants are already raising this — depreciating compute assets over longer lives means that if hardware iterates faster than expected, profits will be systematically marked down in the coming years.

His conclusion would not be "don't buy," but "you can buy, but leave room for error." That room means not buying in full at HKD 440 in one go: acknowledge that you cannot precisely calculate the payback cycle, and use staged purchases to hedge judgment error. This ties to the lesson from his Alibaba purchase — a good company plus a good price can still be the wrong timing.

Li Lu: Tencent as a proxy for Chinese digital consumption

Li Lu's framework operates at the civilizational level: an economy of 1.4 billion people completing its digitalisation needs a default gateway, and Tencent is that gateway. Social, payments, content, mini programs and WeCom are facets of one thing: driving down the daily transaction costs of Chinese people. Over the long run, cash flow at such companies tracks nominal GDP; there is no need to bet on any single hit product.

He also anchors on the valuation centre. His judgment runs roughly as follows: policy and public-opinion constraints will persist over the long term, the era of 30x PE is over, and the reasonable centre is 15–18x. His expected return is therefore not "doubling" but "12%–15% annualised"—buy at 13x, sell at 15x, add 2%–3% from buybacks and dividends, and that is the number.

His view on AI investment is milder than Munger's: this is not an optional investment but a toll that must be paid. If Chinese internet companies do not hold a place at the model layer, in five years they will become pure traffic pipes—and that is when the moat truly disappears.

Valuation: why the base case is only HKD 535

The denominator is Non-IFRS, since IFRS net profit attributable to shareholders is too distorted by fair value moves in the investment portfolio: this quarter Non-IFRS net profit of RMB 68.42 billion grew 9%, versus just 0.7% for the IFRS figure of RMB 56.02 billion, a gap of RMB 12.4 billion. A rough 2027 Non-IFRS net profit of RMB 320 billion implies about 14% growth off roughly RMB 280 billion in 2026. 14x sits in the lower-middle of Tencent's 11–23x range since 2021, consistent with a market that treats AI spending as investment but not yet as a growth engine.

RMB 320 billion × 14x = RMB 4.48 trillion, or HKD 4.87 trillion; divided by 9.103 billion shares that gives HKD 535. Bear case: RMB 280 billion × 11x = HKD 370; bull case: RMB 350 billion × 17x = HKD 700.

Some will ask whether net cash and the USD 72 billion investment portfolio count. They do, but only in the bear case—as the floor when the business is worth nothing, not as a reason to rise. I do not add them in the base case, because the timing of monetising these assets is management's call, not mine.

Odds: 22% upside, 16% downside — win rate is the variable

Neutral +22%, bear case -16% — a payoff ratio of 1.4:1, hardly spectacular. But payoff ratio is not everything; probability of being right is what drives expected value. The bear case needs ad growth to fall from 22% to 12% while AI spending shows no ceiling; I put the probability of both at 30%. The neutral case needs ads to hold at 18% and capex to fall back in 2027, probability 50%. The bull case needs AI revenue broken out separately in the financials, probability 20%.

Expected value = 0.3×(-16%) + 0.5×(+22%) + 0.2×(+59%) = +18%. Positive expectancy, but not the kind you buy blindly. A +18% expected value argues for building half a position first and filling it out on a break below — not going all in at once, because the -16% bear case is no tail scenario; it is a path I have seriously modeled.

One further asymmetry: the 16% downside is cushioned by RMB 58.19 billion in net cash and a USD 72 billion investment portfolio, while the 59% upside rests on a re-rating to 17x. The former is countable assets; the latter is sentiment. The shape of this investment is therefore "hard floor below, sentiment above" — suited to trading time for upside, not to amplifying with leverage.

Disagreement log: where the three analysts diverge

Disagreement one: is the capex one-off? Management calls it "a one-off startup investment in the new AI-native business"; Liu Chiping likens training compute to a fixed cost rather than an equal annual increment of spending. Li Lu accepts this; Munger does not — he has seen too many "one-offs" become perennial spending.

Disagreement two: is 13x the floor? Buffett thinks a business with 57.8% gross margin is cheap enough at 13x; Duan Yongping thinks it can get cheaper (his likely level is HKD 380–400). Neither is in a hurry; the difference is that one buys half now, the other waits for a break below 400.

Disagreement three: is AI a moat or an expense? Li Lu sees it as a toll that must be paid; Munger sees it as a potential bottomless pit. That disagreement will not be resolved in 2026; it can only wait until AI revenue first appears separately in the 2027 annual report — or never appears at all.

One-line position conclusion

If you remember only one line, make it this: everyone would buy this stock, but no one would hold it at full position. Duan Yongping finds it expensive, Buffett finds the AI hard to understand, Munger finds the depreciation heavy and Li Lu finds the growth slow—four men, four reasons not to go full position, and not one reason not to buy.

In odds terms, that "everyone bullish, everyone cautious" combination is the best kind: it means the share price will not surge on consensus, but neither will it collapse on disagreement. It suits a 4%–8% position held over two to three years in exchange for 12%–15% annualised.

Conclusion

The four disagree not on whether to buy but on how much. Duan Yongping would argue this price justifies a small initial position; Buffett would call a monopoly business with 57.8% gross margin trading at 13x a rare opportunity; Munger would insist on scaling in and keeping dry powder; Li Lu would cap expected return at 12%–15% annualised. Consensus: buy, half position initially, add to full on a break below HKD 400.

The disagreement is recorded here for later review: I believe AI capex peaks and rolls over in 2027 (60% probability); Munger believes it may stay flat at RMB 50 billion/quarter long term (40% probability). If Q1 2027 capex is still above RMB 50 billion while marketing services growth falls below 15%, this buy call must be conceded as wrong.
