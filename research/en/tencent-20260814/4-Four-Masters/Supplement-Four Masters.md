## Duan Yongping: This business will still be around in ten years, and the price is cheap enough

Duan Yongping's first question is always "will this company still be here in ten years." Tencent's answer is firmer than most: WeChat has 1.439 billion MAU, with contacts, groups, payments and mini-programs growing on the same relationship chain. **Replacing WeChat is not a technology problem but a social one** — more than a billion people would have to switch messaging tools at once, and history offers no successful precedent.

The second question is "is this a good business." He looks at two things: gross margin and capex. Gross margin of 57.8%, still rising year on year, signals pricing power; capex of RMB 52.784 billion, 25.8% of revenue, signals the business is also getting heavier. The logic behind his words is roughly: **a good business has gotten heavier, but as long as it is still worth this price, it is still a good business.**

The third question is price. At HKD 440 and 13.2x 2026E Non-IFRS, against valuations above 30x in 2021, he would say "this price is a level to start buying at," not "go all in." Duan Yongping's approach has always been to buy a little first and add only once things are clearer, because he knows he can be wrong too.

## Buffett: A business with 57.8% gross margin at 13x PE is a rare quote

The first lesson Buffett learned from buying See's Candies was pricing power: **a business with pricing power sees its gross margin rise on its own**. In a year when Tencent doubled capex, raised R&D by 35% and lifted administrative expenses by 22%, gross margin rose from 56.8% to 57.8% — a health check on pricing power, and one it passed.

The second lesson is capital allocation. In 2025 it bought back HKD 80 billion of stock and paid a dividend of HKD 5.30 per share; in the first half of 2026 it bought back a further HKD 24.4 billion, while explicitly refusing equity financing. **How a company spends money is more credible than what it says.** Buybacks clustered roughly in the HKD 400–500 range imply management sees that range as below intrinsic value — whether that judgment is right is another matter, but at least they sit on the same side as shareholders.

The third lesson is price. A market cap of HKD 4.01 trillion against 2026E Non-IFRS profit of about RMB 280 billion puts it at just over 13x. Buffett would pay 20x for a business like this, provided he "understands" it. He does not understand Tencent's AI piece, so he would apply a discount — and 13x is precisely the discount the market has already applied on his behalf.

## Munger: Invert the question — will the RMB 52.7 billion capex become a bottomless pit?

Munger's approach is to invert: **under what circumstances would this investment lose me a lot of money?** The answer is not WeChat being displaced (extremely low probability), but this RMB 52.784 billion turning into a sunk cost. Single-quarter capex was RMB 52.784 billion, +176% YoY and +65% QoQ; capex cash payments reached RMB 59.3 billion, already exceeding operating cash flow of RMB 52.710 billion.

He would press on three details: What will these GPUs be worth in three years? If Hy4 disappoints, how large is the impairment? Is the depreciation life being stretched too long? People in the industry are already raising this — depreciating compute assets over longer lives means that if hardware iterates faster than expected, profits will be systematically marked down in the coming years.

His conclusion would not be "don't buy," but "**you can buy, but leave room for error**." That room means not buying in full at HKD 440 in one go — acknowledge that you cannot precisely calculate the payback cycle, and use staged purchases to hedge judgment error. This ties to the lesson from his Alibaba purchase: a good company plus a good price can still be the wrong timing.

## Li Lu: Hold Tencent as an index on Chinese digital consumption

Li Lu's framework operates at the civilizational level: an economy of 1.4 billion people completing its digitalization needs a default gateway, and Tencent is that gateway. Social, payments, content, mini programs, WeCom—all are different facets of the same thing: **driving down the daily transaction costs of Chinese people**. The cash flow of such companies tracks nominal GDP over the long run; there is no need to bet on any single hit product.

He also looks at the valuation center. His judgment is roughly this: policy and public-opinion constraints will persist over the long term, the era of 30x PE is over, and the reasonable center is 15–18x. **So his expected return is not "doubling" but "12%–15% annualized"**—buy at 13x, sell at 15x, plus 2%–3% from buybacks and dividends, and that is the number.

His view on AI investment is milder than Munger's: this is not an optional investment but a toll that must be paid. If Chinese internet companies do not hold a place at the model layer, in five years they will become pure traffic pipes—and that is when the moat truly disappears.

## Valuation: why the base case is only HKD 535

The denominator uses Non-IFRS, because IFRS net profit attributable to shareholders is too distorted by fair value changes in the investment portfolio (this quarter RMB 56.02 billion grew only 0.7%, while Non-IFRS RMB 68.42 billion grew 9%, a gap of RMB 12.4 billion). A rough 2027 Non-IFRS net profit of RMB 320 billion: about RMB 280 billion in 2026, growing at 14%. **14x sits in the lower-middle of Tencent's 11–23x range since 2021**, corresponding to a state where "AI spending is recognized as investment but not yet as a growth engine."

RMB 320 billion × 14x = RMB 4.48 trillion, or HKD 4.87 trillion, divided by 9.103 billion shares = HKD 535. Bear case RMB 280 billion × 11x = HKD 370; bull case RMB 350 billion × 17x = HKD 700.

Some will ask: do net cash and the USD 72 billion investment portfolio count? They do, but only in the bear case—that is "the floor when the business is worth nothing," not "a reason to rise." In the base case I do not add them, because the timing of monetizing these assets is decided by management, not by me.

## Odds: 22% upside, 16% downside — win rate is the variable

Neutral +22%, bear case -16%, a payoff ratio of 1.4:1 — not spectacular. **But the payoff ratio is not everything; the probability of being right is what determines expected value.** The bear case requires ad growth to fall from 22% to 12% while AI spending shows no ceiling; I put the probability of both happening at 30%. The neutral case requires ads to hold at 18% and capex to fall back in 2027, probability 50%. The bull case requires AI revenue to be broken out separately in the financials, probability 20%.

Expected value = 0.3×(-16%) + 0.5×(+22%) + 0.2×(+59%) = +18%. **Positive expectancy, but not the kind you buy blindly.** A +18% expected value calls for building half a position first and filling it out on a break below — not going all in at once, because the -16% bear case is not a tail scenario; it is a path I have seriously modeled.

One more asymmetry is worth noting: the 16% downside is cushioned by RMB 58.19 billion in net cash and a USD 72 billion investment portfolio, while the 59% upside rests on a re-rating to 17x. The former is countable assets; the latter is sentiment. So the shape of this investment is "hard floor below, sentiment above" — suited to trading time for upside, not to amplifying with leverage.

## Disagreement log: where the three analysts' judgments diverge

**Disagreement one: whether capex is one-off.** Management calls it "a one-off startup investment in the new AI-native business"; Liu Chiping likens compute for model training to a fixed cost rather than an equal annual increment of spending. Li Lu accepts this; Munger does not — he has seen too many "one-offs" turn into perennial spending.

**Disagreement two: whether 13x is the floor.** Buffett thinks buying a business with 57.8% gross margin at 13x is cheap enough; Duan Yongping thinks it can get cheaper (his likely level is HKD 380–400). Neither is in a hurry; the difference is that one buys half now, the other waits for a break below 400.

**Disagreement three: whether AI is a moat or an expense.** Li Lu sees it as a toll that must be paid; Munger sees it as a potential bottomless pit. This disagreement will not be resolved in 2026; it can only wait until AI revenue first appears separately in the 2027 annual report — or never appears at all.

## One-line position conclusion

If you can remember only one line: **this is a stock everyone would buy, but no one would hold at full position.** Duan Yongping finds it expensive, Buffett finds the AI hard to understand, Munger finds the depreciation heavy, Li Lu finds the growth slow—each of the four has one reason not to go full position, but none has a single reason not to buy.

This combination of "everyone bullish, everyone cautious" is the best kind in odds terms: **it means the share price will not surge on consensus, but neither will it collapse on disagreement.** Suited to a 4%–8% position held over two to three years in exchange for 12%–15% annualized.
