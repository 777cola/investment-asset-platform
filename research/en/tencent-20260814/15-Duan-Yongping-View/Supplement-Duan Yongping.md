# Tencent Holdings（00700）— Duan Yongping Supplement

- Research date：2026/08/14
- Price：440.0 HKD
- Market cap：Approximately HKD 4.01 trillion

## Notes

This is a Q&A-style exercise applying Duan Yongping's publicly stated investment framework (business essence, circle of competence, benfen, rough estimation, stop-doing list). It is not his own statement on Tencent. Data: Q2 2026 revenue RMB 204.785 billion, net profit RMB 56.022 billion, gross margin 57.8%, WeChat MAU 1.439 billion, capex RMB 52.784 billion; H1 2026 buyback of 50.03 million shares for HKD 24.4 billion, average price HKD 487.7; WorkBuddy PC visits 20.97 million in June (Analysys data).

## Question 1: Is this a good business?

Good. Gross margin 57.8%, net profit of RMB 56 billion in a single quarter, 1.439 billion users — if that isn't good, what is? And it doesn't live off one product: advertising, games, payments — three legs. If one leg gives out, two remain.

But look closely: it is spending heavily right now. Capital expenditure of RMB 52.784 billion in one quarter, a quarter of revenue. Whether that money turns into assets or into expenses, nobody can say today. A good business does not mean the money is being spent wisely — those are two separate things.

## Question 2: Will it still be around in ten years?

It will. Think about it: will Chinese people still be using WeChat in ten years? I think so. To replace WeChat, you would have to migrate your contacts, groups, payments, mini programs, and your employer's WeCom all at once. Who would do that? Something with no reason to happen will not happen.

But "still there" and "good" are two different things. In ten years it may still be around, but growing only 5%, which makes it a utility, and a 12x PE would be fair. So I don't much care whether it is still there in ten years; I care whether it can still raise prices in ten years. Only pricing power makes a good business; merely surviving makes infrastructure.

## Question three: Can you understand it?

Eight out of ten. Advertising, gaming, payments, cloud — I understand these four. How revenue is generated, what the costs are, why they make money — I can explain all of it.

What I cannot understand is what that RMB 52.784 billion turned into. It bought cards, built data centers, trained models — and then what? WorkBuddy gets 20 million visits a month, which sounds good, but how much money do 20 million visits convert into? I don't know. What I don't understand, I don't value. So in my calculations I only apply the 13x multiple to the core business and treat the AI part as free.

## Question 4: At what price to buy?

You can start at this price. At 440, 13x, the company's own average buyback price in the first half was 487.7, more expensive than what I paid. The company itself is buying with real money at 490, and I'm buying at 440 — the math works.

But I don't recommend buying it all at once. Why? Because I don't know whether that capex will still be RMB 50 billion next quarter. If it is, I'll have to redo the math. So I usually buy a little first, watch for two quarters, and add once I understand it. I'll add below 400, and I'll be even happier at 350 — provided advertising is still growing.

## Question 5: What worries you most?

My biggest fear is that it throws all the money it earns into a place I don't understand, and gets addicted to doing so. RMB 200 billion a year, and three years of that is RMB 600 billion. If three years from now the AI side still cannot report revenue on a standalone basis, that RMB 600 billion turns into depreciation and impairment, and whether profit can withstand that is anyone's guess.

My second fear is that to save money it sharply raises ad load. That is squeezing out the last copper coin: short-term profit looks good, but users leave over the long run. Tencent hasn't done this in the past decade, and I hope it keeps not doing it.

What I don't fear is the share price falling. A fall is better; I can buy more. What I fear is the business deteriorating — that is the real loss.

## Question 6: If you were CEO, what would you do?

I would do the same thing, but say it more clearly. The problem now is that management says "this is a one-off investment" and the market does not believe it, because there is no evidence.If I were CEO, I would break out AI revenue separately in the earnings report—even if it were only RMB 5 billion, that would still be better than saying nothing. With transparency, the discount disappears.

Second, I would clarify the buyback parameters: how much can be bought back this year, and whether that can continue next year. What the market fears most is not ugly numbers, but not knowing how ugly the numbers will get.Ultimately, running a business is like being a person: stay grounded, and do not let people think you are hiding something.

## Question 7: Dividends or buybacks — which do you value more?

Buybacks are better, provided the price is cheap. A dividend hands you the cash; a buyback has the company use its cash to buy its own stock—if the company buys cheaper than you could yourself, the buyback is making money on your behalf. Tencent's average buyback price in the first half was 487.7, and the stock is now at 440, meaning it bought 10% too high. That is not its fault; no one can buy the exact bottom.

But it bought back RMB 24.4 billion this year and paid another RMB 41.6 billion in dividends, RMB 66 billion in total, while free cash flow was negative.That is a problem: borrowing to pay dividends and selling assets to fund buybacks is not sustainable.I would rather it bought back less this year, held on to the cash, and bought again when prices are cheaper.

The dividend does not worry me. HKD 5.30 per share, a 1.2% yield—neither high nor low. If it ever raised the payout ratio to 30%, that would be treating shareholders as shareholders in earnest.

## Question 8: If you could buy only one Chinese internet stock, which would it be?

Tencent. Not because it is the best — ByteDance is not listed on the HKEX, so it cannot be bought; Alibaba is stronger in cloud, but it has more things to explain than Tencent; NetEase has high gross margins but no growth; Meituan is still fighting.

The reason to buy Tencent is that it "requires the least explanation."1.4 billion people use it, advertising is rising, gross margins are expanding, management is buying back stock — all of this is visible. The invisible part (AI) I am not paying for; if it succeeds, I count that as a bonus.

That is enough. In investing, what you earn comes from "the part you understand," not "the part you guessed right."
