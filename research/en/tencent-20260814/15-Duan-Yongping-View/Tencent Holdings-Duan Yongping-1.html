Core thesis

Ask Tencent the questions Duan Yongping would ask: Is this a good business? Yes. Will it still be here in ten years? Yes. Do I understand it? About eight parts out of ten. At what price do I buy? Roughly here. What do I fear most? That it pours all its profits into something I don't understand.

Conclusion: buy, but don't go all in. His standard line is "rough estimate," and the rough-estimate answer is "cheap, but not cheap enough to bet the house on."

Question 1: Is this a good business?

Good. Gross margin 57.8%, net profit of RMB 56 billion in a single quarter, 1.439 billion users — if that isn't good, what is? And it doesn't live off one product: advertising, games and payments give it three legs. If one gives out, two remain.

Look closer, though: it is spending heavily right now. Capex of RMB 52.784 billion in one quarter, a quarter of revenue. Whether that money turns into assets or into expenses, nobody can say today. A good business does not mean the money is being spent wisely — those are two separate things.

Question 2: Will it still be around in ten years?

It will. Ask yourself: will Chinese people still be using WeChat in ten years? I think so. Replacing WeChat would mean migrating your contacts, groups, payments, mini programs and your employer's WeCom all at once. Who would do that? Something with no reason to happen will not happen.

But "still there" and "good" are two different things. In ten years it may still be around, but growing only 5%, which makes it a utility, and a 12x PE would be fair. So I don't much care whether it is still there in ten years; I care whether it can still raise prices in ten years. Only pricing power makes a good business; merely surviving makes infrastructure.

Question three: Can you understand it?

Eight out of ten. Advertising, gaming, payments, cloud — I understand all four: how revenue is generated, what the costs are, why they make money. I can explain the lot.

What I cannot understand is what that RMB 52.784 billion turned into. It bought cards, built data centres, trained models — and then what? WorkBuddy draws 20 million visits a month, which sounds good, but how much money do 20 million visits convert into? I don't know. What I don't understand, I don't value. So in my numbers I apply the 13x multiple to the core business only and treat AI as free.

Question 4: At what price to buy?

You can start at this price. At 440, or 13x, the company's own average buyback price in the first half was 487.7 — more than I paid. The company is buying with real money at 490 and I'm buying at 440; the math works.

But I wouldn't buy it all at once. Why? Because I don't know whether that capex will still be RMB 50 billion next quarter. If it is, I'll have to redo the math. So I usually buy a little first, watch for two quarters, and add once I understand it. I'll add below 400, and I'll be even happier at 350 — provided advertising is still growing.

Question 5: What worries you most?

My biggest fear is that it pours every yuan it earns into a business I don't understand, and gets addicted to doing so. RMB 200 billion a year, and three years of that is RMB 600 billion. If the AI side still cannot report revenue on a standalone basis three years from now, that RMB 600 billion turns into depreciation and impairment, and whether profit can withstand that is anyone's guess.

My second fear is that it sharply raises ad load to save money. That is squeezing out the last copper coin: short-term profit looks good, but users leave over the long run. Tencent hasn't done this in the past decade, and I hope it keeps not doing it.

What I don't fear is the share price falling. A fall is better; I can buy more. What I fear is the business deteriorating — that is the real loss.

Question 6: If you were CEO, what would you do?

I would do the same, but put it more plainly. The problem is that management calls this "a one-off investment" and the market does not believe it, because there is no evidence.If I were CEO, I would break out AI revenue separately in the earnings report—even at only RMB 5 billion, that beats saying nothing. Transparency removes the discount.

Second, I would clarify the buyback parameters: how much can be bought back this year, and whether that pace can continue next year. What the market fears most is not ugly numbers, but not knowing how ugly they will get.Ultimately, running a business is like being a person: stay grounded, and do not let people think you are hiding something.

Question 7: Dividends or buybacks — which do you value more?

Buybacks beat dividends, provided the price is right. A dividend hands you the cash; a buyback has the company spend its own cash buying back stock—if it buys cheaper than you could yourself, the buyback is making money on your behalf. Tencent's average buyback price in the first half was 487.7 against a stock now at 440, so it bought 10% too high. That is not its fault; no one buys the exact bottom.

But it bought back RMB 24.4 billion this year and paid another RMB 41.6 billion in dividends—RMB 66 billion in total—while free cash flow was negative.That is a problem: borrowing to pay dividends and selling assets to fund buybacks is not sustainable.I would rather it bought back less this year, held the cash, and bought again when prices are cheaper.

The dividend does not worry me. HKD 5.30 per share, a 1.2% yield—neither high nor low. Lifting the payout ratio to 30% would be treating shareholders as shareholders in earnest.

Question 8: If you could own just one Chinese internet stock, which would it be?

Tencent — not because it is the best. ByteDance is not listed on the HKEX, so it cannot be bought; Alibaba is stronger in cloud but has more to explain than Tencent; NetEase has high gross margins but no growth; Meituan is still fighting.

The reason to buy Tencent is that it requires the least explanation.1.4 billion people use it, advertising is rising, gross margins are expanding, management is buying back stock — all of it visible. I am not paying for the invisible part (AI); if it succeeds, I count that as a bonus.

That is enough. In investing, what you earn comes from the part you understand, not the part you guessed right.

Conclusion

The Duan Yongping-style conclusion: I understand this business, I am willing to buy at this price, but I will not bet my entire net worth. Buy a little first, watch for two quarters, add once I understand it — that is more reliable than any valuation model.

In one sentence: At 440 you are buying a company 1.4 billion people use every day, with a 57.8% gross margin, that is itself buying back stock at 487.7 — you don't need to be too clever, just don't get scared off while it is spending money.
