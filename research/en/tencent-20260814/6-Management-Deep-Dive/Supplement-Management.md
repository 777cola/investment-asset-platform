## Capital allocation: buybacks, dividends and capacity expansion advance on three fronts at once

2025: HKD 80 billion of buybacks, final dividend of HKD 5.30 per share (RMB 41.6 billion paid in total, settled in the first half of 2026). First half of 2026: 50.03 million shares repurchased for HKD 24.4 billion, average price HKD 487.7; 21 consecutive trading days of buybacks before the results announcement, totalling about HKD 4.1 billion. **Buying steadily at HKD 440–490 is the most direct statement this company can make about its own value.**

Capacity expansion over the same period: first-half capex of RMB 84.72 billion, up 82% year on year, with RMB 52.784 billion in Q2 alone. Three things at once — buybacks, dividends and heavy capex — rest on a single premise: cash on the balance sheet. Net cash at the end of June was RMB 58.191 billion, down RMB 88.7 billion from the end of March, and that premise is thinning.

The track record is solid too: an in-specie distribution of JD.com in 2022 and of Meituan in 2023, handing investment gains directly to shareholders rather than keeping them on the balance sheet as "strategic investments". **That approach is a minority practice among Chinese internet companies.**

## Disclosure: the bigger the commitment, the less the transparency

The most alarming thing about this earnings report is not the numbers but the disclosure choices: AI revenue is not broken out (management only said on the call that "cloud growth accelerated from high-teens to low-twenties"), Weixin Channels advertising revenue is not broken out, and segment profit for cloud is not broken out (fintech and business services are combined, with a gross margin of 52%).

**These three items happen to be the three most critical bull arguments.** Inputs are visible (RMB 52.784 billion), outputs are not; this is not fraud, but a way to keep the power of interpretation in its own hands. Compare with Tencent before 2021—back then even "fintech and business services" was broken out in detail—current disclosure is clearly more conservative.

Management also provided an operating profit metric "excluding new AI products" (RMB 86.1 billion vs RMB 75.64 billion). This metric is useful for analysis, but it is a non-IFRS measure defined by the company itself, **and must be used alongside the standard metric**, otherwise it creates the illusion that "profit grew 19%".

## Track record: no blow-ups, but two lessons learned

Tencent has never had hard failures such as financial fraud, controlling-shareholder fund appropriation, or abnormal audit opinions. Its two major historical external shocks were not integrity issues: the 2021 antitrust and gaming regulation, and the 2022–2023 delisting risk for China concept stocks. **Both hurt valuation, not the financial statements.**

What truly warrants recording is the "flexibility of wording." Martin Lau split this round of capital expenditure into "that related to core businesses" and "one-off startup investment in new AI-native businesses," and said compute for model training is closer to a fixed cost and does not require equal incremental additions every year. **This statement holds logically but has no historical precedent.** It is "verifiable optimism," not "misleading," but note that its premise is that after Hy4, no new clusters of equivalent scale will be needed.

## Incentive mechanism: no layoffs, but no further hiring either

Net headcount rose by 5,672 in 2025 and attrition fell to 15.9%; Martin Lau stated plainly that there is no major layoff plan. **In a year when AI replaces white-collar work, "no layoffs" is a cost choice** — what is saved is not wages but organizational trust.

R&D spend was RMB 27.28 billion, up 35% year on year; general and administrative expenses were RMB 38.808 billion, up 22%. All of the incremental spending went to AI. Where these two pools of money went shows incentives tilting toward technology: compute, models and AI product teams received more resources than mature businesses. That also explains why revenue growth in mature businesses is slowing — resources were deliberately redirected.

## Succession: not a problem within three years, but a problem within five

Pony Ma, born in 1971, is 55 in 2026 and still drives strategy (the three-layer AI architecture in this earnings report was his call). Martin Lau and Ren Yuxin are both "veterans" who joined in the 2000s and are around 50, so the succession pipeline is clear. **No near-term handover risk.**

The five-year view hinges on something else: who holds product judgment in the AI era. Yao Shunyu (formerly OpenAI) joined in 2025 and delivered Hy3 in July 2026 — the first time Tencent has handed the mission-critical "model capability" role to an outside hire parachuted in.** That is both a plus (willingness to use outsiders) and a risk (whether the organization can accommodate him long term).**

## Compensation is not the point; equity ownership is

Pony Ma's annual salary is about HKD 48.53 million, not high for the founder of a company with a HKD 4 trillion market cap. What matters is that he holds 805 million shares, an 8.82% stake, and at a dividend of HKD 5.30 per share, that yields about HKD 4.24 billion a year in dividends. **His income structure is "holdings first, salary second," which aligns his decision horizon with shareholders.**

Counterexamples abound: at companies run by professional managers, pay is tied to short-term profit, which easily leads to moves like cutting R&D or buying back stock to prop up the price. Tencent does not have this problem—ninety percent of Pony Ma's wealth comes from the share price and dividends, and he cares more than any fund manager about what the company will be worth in three years.

## A counterpoint: buybacks and capex are in a race

In the first half of 2026, buybacks consumed HKD 24.4 billion (about RMB 22.5 billion), while capex consumed RMB 84.72 billion. **Capex was 3.8x buybacks.** In 2024 the ratio was reversed—buyback spending far exceeded capex at that time.

This is not a change of heart by management but a shift in priorities: AI compute is "invest or be left behind" spending, while buybacks are "nice to have" optimization. When cash is limited, the former always comes first. For shareholders, this means **buybacks will contribute less to total returns over the next two years, while dividends will contribute more**—the payout ratio may rise passively, because buybacks require cash to be retained.
