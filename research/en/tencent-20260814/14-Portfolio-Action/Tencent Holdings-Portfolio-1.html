Core view

Position size is a function not of "degree of bullishness" but of "odds × win rate × cost of being wrong." For Tencent here: odds 1.4:1, neutral win rate 50%, pessimistic -16%, tail -25%. That combination supports an initial position of 4%, a target of 8% and a cap of 10%.

Judgment: buyable, but it must not become the largest holding in the portfolio. The direction of its cash flow remains uncertain, and that question will be answered within four quarters.

Why 4%, not 8% or 2%

The problem with 8%: if Q3 results confirm capex is the new normal, the stock could fall to HKD 370 (-16%), and an 8% position would cost the portfolio 1.3pp — tolerable, but it leaves no ammunition to add at the wrong moment. The problem with 2%: on a 1.4:1 payoff, a 2% position makes no money — it is just going through the motions.

4% is the compromise: a drop to 400 gives you ammunition to build to 8%; a rise to 535 gives you a position to take profits on (halving to 4% still leaves a base position). In essence, it trades position size for observation time.

Three entry levels for adding to the position, each with a distinct rationale

440 (now): Buy on "core business intact + valuation floor", the logic being mean reversion. 400: Buy on "the market treats AI as a pure liability", the logic being rising odds — 12x P/E, 11.8x ex-cash, with the pessimistic scenario largely priced in. 350: Buy on "extreme pessimism", the logic being asset support — RMB 58.19 billion net cash plus a USD 72 billion portfolio, about 15% of market cap; at 350 you are buying the core business at 10.5x with the AI option thrown in free.

Position sizes at these three levels are 4%, 8% and 10%. The premise for adding is that the thesis has not been falsified — if ad growth falls below 15%, do not add even at 350; that is where a value trap begins.

Rebalancing: where does this 4% of money come from

This is not "adding to positions" but "switching positions." Tencent's 4% should come from two places: first, trimming a position that has already reached its target price; second, cutting the overweight cash allocation.

It cannot come from "adding more leverage." For a portfolio at 13x, with negative free cash flow and capex still climbing, leverage would turn a normal 16% drawdown into a 32% forced-liquidation risk.

The benefit of switching is that it forces a comparison: if the stock being sold has an expected return above 15% over the next three years, it should not be switched. That action itself matters more than the position number.

Trim and stop-loss: follow the thesis, not the price

This stock does not warrant a price stop-loss. Tencent de-rating from 13x to 11x is an opportunity, not a risk; a mechanical stop-loss would force you to sell your best asset at the bottom. What you need is a thesis stop-loss.

Three thesis stop-loss triggers: ad growth below 15% for two consecutive quarters; capex above RMB 50 billion for three consecutive quarters alongside cloud growth below 15%; Non-IFRS net profit turning negative YoY. If any one is triggered, halve the position; if two are triggered, exit entirely. Add one hard stop at HKD 330 (-25%) — not because it will necessarily get there, but because if it does, the market knows something I don't.

If already holding: how to adjust now

Three scenarios.Already holding with a cost above HKD 600: do not average down. First check whether the thesis has broken — if advertising is still growing and capex is rolling over, you can wait; if both have broken, taking the loss and switching stocks beats averaging down.Cost of HKD 400–500: keep holding, neither add nor trim, wait for the Q3 results.

Already overweight (above 10%): trim to 8%. The reason is not a bearish view but position discipline — with a single stock above 10%, any earnings swing will distort your judgment.

The common principle across all three: whether to add depends on whether the thesis has deteriorated; whether to trim depends on whether the position has breached its limit; neither depends on your cost basis. Cost basis is a mental account, not an investment basis.

Same capital as the China internet ADRs.

Tencent's correlation with the Hang Seng Tech Index has long held above 0.85, versus around 0.7 with Alibaba and around 0.6 with Kuaishou. Buying Tencent ≈ buying the beta of a basket of Chinese internet, just with slightly better alpha.

Position sizing must therefore be calculated on a combined basis: if you already hold Alibaba, Meituan and Kuaishou, Tencent's 8% cannot be viewed in isolation — it must be controlled within "total China internet exposure". I recommend total China internet exposure of no more than 20% of the equity portfolio, with Tencent at no more than half of that.

Correlation with global AI hardware is negative — rising compute prices benefit Nvidia and hurt Tencent — which makes it a natural hedge: if the portfolio holds Nvidia, a rise in Tencent's capex is itself a hedge.

Conclusion

Portfolio management conclusion: initial position 4%, target 8%, cap 10%, hard stop HKD 330. Add on price (400, 350); trim on thesis (advertising, capex, net profit). Never mix the two.

On correlation, group it with Alibaba, Meituan and Kuaishou as "China internet exposure", capped at 20% combined. Even if the call is wrong, the loss then stays within 2% of the portfolio and does not impair principal.
