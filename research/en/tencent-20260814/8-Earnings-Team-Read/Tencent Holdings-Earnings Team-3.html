Master Class Close Read (Munger) — Tencent Holdings

Core view

Munger does not ask whether AI can succeed; he asks how bad it gets if it fails. The answer: RMB 52.784 billion of equipment depreciated over 5 years consumes RMB 10 billion of cost a year, and if demand does not arrive, impairment comes on top.

That is not fatal, but it caps the valuation—a company that must pay RMB 10 billion a year for a pile of metal should not command a "light-asset platform" multiple.

Why did scale effects fail this quarter?

Scale drives profitability in a platform business: more users dilute server costs. This quarter inverted that—users did not grow (1.439 billion, +2%), yet server costs jumped 176%. Because compute is not the cost of "serving existing users," it is the cost of "betting on future users."

Munger would say these are two entirely different categories of spending, and lumping them into one statement leads to misjudgment. Traditional server expansion grows linearly with revenue; AI compute is stepwise, front-loaded and depreciates quickly. Valuing it as "normal capital expenditure" leads to systematic overvaluation.

The incentive mismatch: who is accountable for the RMB 52.7 billion

The people signing off on this RMB 52.784 billion almost certainly carry KPIs tied to "model capability rankings," "token call volume" and "WorkBuddy user count" — not "return on capital." This is the incentive misalignment Munger warned about most: the people spending the money are not the people bearing the depreciation.

Management is already correcting course, splitting capex into "core business-related" and "new AI-native business," with the latter framed as one-off startup investment; Liu Chiping argues that compute for model training is closer to a fixed cost. The essence of these statements is to separate "investment" from "expense" in the narrative, so the market applies a different multiple to each.

Munger's reaction would be: the narrative can be separated, the accounting cannot. Depreciation still flows through the income statement.

The hardest cost to cut: compute

In an internet company's cost base, headcount can be cut, marketing spend can be halted, content procurement can be reduced. Compute is different: once a cluster is built, depreciation starts running, and the bill continues even if it sits idle. This is the first time Tencent has carried a large cost that is "irreversible."

On 2026 capex of RMB 190 billion and 5-year depreciation, 2027 incremental depreciation is about RMB 38 billion, equal to 12% of that year's forecast Non-IFRS net profit. If AI revenue cannot cover this depreciation, profit will be held down — not because the business is deteriorating, but because the asset base is too heavy.

Think in reverse: under what circumstances would this money be worth it?

Three scenarios: (1) office AI such as WorkBuddy charges real fees, with paying users' gross margin approaching the cloud segment's overall level (per management) — in which case compute is capacity; (2) WeChat mini-programs shorten the path from demand to transaction, lifting both advertising and e-commerce commissions; (3) external MaaS token sales monetise idle compute.

If just one of the three works, RMB 52.784 billion is not expensive. A Munger-style judgment: not all three need to work, but at least one must be quantifiable. All three remain early-stage today, so only half a position is warranted.

Conclusion

Through a Munger lens: this is not a company that has "gone bad," it is a company that has "gone heavy." Going heavy costs RMB 38 billion a year in depreciation, turns free cash flow negative and curbs buyback capacity. What it buys is a ticket into the AI era.

His recommended action: buy, but cap the position at a level where "even if this entire investment goes to zero, the portfolio is unaffected." By his standard that is 4%–6%, not 15%.
