Core view

Li Lu does not judge Tencent on quarterly earnings; he judges it on how Chinese people will live a decade from now. The answer certainly includes WeChat: the communications, payments and government-services gateway for 1.439 billion people. The value of that gateway tracks nominal GDP, not quarterly advertising budgets.

What he cares about is something else: whether the owner of that gateway is willing to pay the entry ticket for the next era. That ticket costs RMB 52.784 billion.

Pricing logic for infrastructure-type companies

Infrastructure-type companies share four traits: slow growth, steady cash flow, a deep moat and a valuation ceiling. Tencent's revenue growth has slowed to 11% and Non-IFRS net profit growth to 9%, the pace of a "mature infrastructure" business.

The fair valuation for such a company is 12–18x, not 25–30x. Li Lu would accept this reality and then set expected return at "net profit growth + dividend and buyback": 9% + 2.3% ≈ 11.3%. Add the one-off contribution from the re-rating from 13x back to 15x, and the three-year annualised return is roughly 15%. That is a number he would accept.

Policy discount: why the multiple cannot return to 30x

Li Lu's assessment of Chinese companies has always carried an "institutional discount": however excellent the business, it must bear a discount for regulatory uncertainty. Tencent spans social, payments, gaming, content and cloud, and every one of those operations sits within regulators' line of sight.

The discount shows up not in falling profit but in a lower valuation centre: 30x before 2021, 20x after 2022, 13x in 2026. Each step down reflects a "rule change", not an "earnings decline". Hence his view: "don't expect a return to 30x".

Global compute cycle: Tencent is a buyer, not a seller

In this global AI capex cycle, Nvidia is the seller and the cloud providers are the buyers. Tencent is a buyer — it spent RMB 52.784 billion on chips, betting the application layer can earn it back. Buyers suffer most when the cycle turns up: chips get more expensive, delivery slows, depreciation accelerates.

Li Lu's framework would place this within the "progress of civilization": compute is the new electricity, and over the long run it will certainly be oversupplied and certainly be cheap. What is truly scarce is "who has the use cases to turn compute into revenue." Tencent has use cases reaching 1.439 billion people — that is its advantage as a buyer.

Three scenarios for a decade from now

①"China's Meta": ad load maxed out, Channels e-commerce proven, revenue up 8%–10% a year, 15–18x valuation, market cap doubles. 40% probability. ②"China's utility": growth slows to 5%, 10–12x valuation, returns delivered through dividends and buybacks, 8% annualised. 45% probability. ③"The gateway disrupted by AI": agents bypass apps, WeChat becomes a pipe, 8x valuation. 15% probability.

Weighting the three scenarios gives an expected 10%–12% annualised return. This is not a "life-changing" investment; it is an investment that "beats inflation plus the index." Li Lu would define it honestly in exactly those terms.

Conclusion

Through Li Lu's lens, Tencent is an index on Chinese digital consumption rather than a growth stock. A reasonable expectation is 10%–15% annualised on a central multiple of 15x; a return to 30x is unlikely.

His risk warning comes down to one line: this index's constituents include a policy variable, and policy variables cannot be predicted, so the only response is a discount. The 13x is the discount the market has already applied.
