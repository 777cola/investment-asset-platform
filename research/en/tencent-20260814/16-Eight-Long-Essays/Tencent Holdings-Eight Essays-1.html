01 Cognitive reset: three misconceptions about Tencent — Tencent Holdings

After a 35% drop, first establish what has actually fallen
Research date 2026/08/14  ·  Price HK$440.00 (-0.23%)  ·  Market cap Approximately HKD 4.01 trillion  ·  P/E (TTM) Approximately 15.0x

Core view

Three popular misconceptions about Tencent circulate in the market: first, that a 30% decline means the fundamentals have deteriorated; second, that Tencent is still a growth stock; third, that AI investment is a free option. All three are wrong, and wrong in different directions—the first two misjudge the company's stage, the third misreads the accounting.

This piece takes apart these three misconceptions and replaces them with a usable framework: a mature infrastructure company paying a visible admission fee for the next era.

Misconception 1: a 30% fall means the fundamentals have deteriorated

From HKD 677.5 to HKD 440 — a 35% decline. Had the fundamentals deteriorated, the financials would show it: falling revenue, falling gross margin, user churn, share loss. None of that appears.

2026Q2: revenue RMB 204.79 billion, +11% YoY (above the consensus of RMB 202.8 billion); gross margin 57.8%, +1 percentage point YoY; marketing services +22%; domestic games +17%; WeChat MAU 1.44 billion, +2% YoY; Video Accounts watch time +20%.An across-the-board strong set of operating results.

So what fell? The multiple. The same earnings commanded 23x last year and 15x this year. The multiple fell 35% while earnings rose 10% — net the two out and you get the roughly 30% share-price decline.What fell was the valuation, not the business.

Misconception 2: Tencent is still a growth stock

Tencent generated RMB 751.8 billion of revenue in 2025 and RMB 401.243 billion in H1 2026, up 10% YoY, with non-IFRS net profit of RMB 136.32 billion in H1, also up 10% YoY. 10% growth is the pace of a mature company, not a growth stock.

A growth stock is defined by sustained growth above 20% with a distant ceiling. Tencent's ceiling is already visible: WeChat MAU of 1.439 billion against a Chinese population of 1.4 billion means penetration has peaked; gaming is constrained by licence approvals and product cycles; payments track consumption. That leaves three growth sources: pricing (ad load), mix (share of high-margin businesses) and new businesses (cloud and AI).

Accepting that it is a mature company is what makes its value calculable: mature companies are valued on cash flow, dividends and buybacks, and on valuation range. Through a growth-stock lens it will always look cheap but unable to move; through a mature-company lens, 13x is clearly undervalued.

Misconception 3: AI investment is a free option

Many treat AI as a "free option": the spending sits in expenses, the revenue sits outside the valuation, so "any pullback is free money." That view overlooks one thing—the option premium is deducted from cash flow, and deducted for real.

Capex this quarter was RMB 52.784 billion, up 176% YoY; cash paid was RMB 59.3 billion; free cash flow was negative RMB 13.8 billion. Net cash fell from RMB 146.860 billion at the end of March to RMB 58.191 billion, down RMB 88.7 billion in a single quarter.This is not "free"—it was bought with real money.

Put differently, AI is an option on which the down payment has already been made. That down payment is RMB 52.784 billion a quarter, with several more quarters of payments to come. Its value hinges on two things—whether products like WorkBuddy can charge fees, and whether the cloud can sell the compute. Until those two questions have answers, treating it as an option is fine, but not as a free option.

An infrastructure company buying its ticket to the game

Take three data points together: 10% growth, the hallmark of a mature company; a 57.8% gross margin, the hallmark of a monopoly; and capex at 25.8% of revenue, the hallmark of an asset-heavy business. Together they describe a company that guards a toll bridge and is spending heavily to build a second one alongside it.

The tolls are still coming in, and faster than before (advertising +22%). The money to build the bridge comes out of those tolls, so cash flow is negative this quarter. Whether the second bridge gets built, and whether anyone uses it once it is, is unknown for now.

That framing leads to a direct judgment: the value of the existing bridge (13x P/E) is safe, the value of the new bridge (AI) is free, but the risk of building it (capex becoming routine) deserves a discount. Put the three together and the conclusion is "you can buy, but don't go all in."

From 677.5 to 440: the collapse of three valuation logics

Phase one (October 2025, HKD 677.5, about 23x): The narrative was "AI empowers everything," and the market treated Tencent as one of "China's biggest AI beneficiaries," awarding it a growth-stock multiple. Phase two (June 2026, HKD 411.8, about 14x): The narrative was "AI is just a cost." With capex rising and no revenue disclosure, the market reclassified AI from a plus to a minus.

Phase three (now, HKD 440, 13.2x 2026E): The narrative is "AI is a ticket you have to pay for." The market no longer pays an AI premium, but no longer docks it hard either—advertising is accelerating and gross margin is improving.

Across the three phases, the valuation has gone from 23x to 13x, back to the low end of its range since 2022. Where it goes next depends on the fourth narrative: whether AI revenue shows up in the financials. The timing for that is 2027.

Conclusion

The cognitive reset, in sum: Tencent has not deteriorated, it has become heavier; it is not a growth stock but infrastructure; AI is not a free option but a ticket on which the down payment has already been paid. Invert those three perceptions and the meaning of a 13x P/E becomes clear—not "absurdly cheap" but "reasonably low, with the risk already priced in."
