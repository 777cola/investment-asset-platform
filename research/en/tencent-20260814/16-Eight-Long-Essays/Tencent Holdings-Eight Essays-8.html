Core view

A decision comes down to four questions: What is this business worth? How much am I willing to pay? Under what conditions would I change my mind? What if I'm wrong? The first two determine whether to buy; the last two determine whether you can hold on.

Tencent's answers: worth HKD 535 (base case), willing to pay 440 (now), three conditions for changing my mind, and a stop-loss at 330 if wrong.

Question 1: How much is this business worth

We use non-IFRS net profit rather than IFRS, which fair value changes in the investment portfolio distort too heavily (a RMB 12.4 billion gap this quarter). Our 2027E of RMB 320 billion assumes 2026E of RMB 280 billion growing at 14%.

14x sits in the lower-middle of Tencent's 11–23x range since 2021, consistent with "AI investment recognised as capex but not yet as a growth engine." 320 × 14 = RMB 4.48 trillion = HKD 4.87 trillion ÷ 9.103 billion shares = HKD 535.

Cross-check: sum-of-the-parts gives about HKD 520, consistent in magnitude. So 535 is not an isolated number.

Question 2: How much am I willing to pay?

My rule is "at least 20% upside to target price." 535 ÷ 1.2 = HKD 446. At 440 the stock just clears that bar — not deeply undervalued, but only barely qualifying.

The hurdle looks harsh, but it addresses a real problem: good companies are often "good but not cheap enough," and buying them makes no money. The 20% threshold separates "good company" from "good investment."

Apply a stricter 30% upside and the threshold becomes HKD 412 — which means waiting, and the 411.8 print on June 26 offered exactly one such opportunity. So this stock is not for chasing, it is for waiting.

An easily overlooked preliminary question: why now

Every buy decision carries an implicit question: why not last month, why not next month. Tencent's answer: because three conditions now hold at once—valuation at the low end of its historical range (13x), the core business accelerating (advertising +22%), and bad news just fully out (the RMB 13.8 billion FCF hit has already been digested by the market for a round).

Last month (HKD 411.8 in June) the valuation was cheaper, but advertising acceleration had not yet been verified; next month, if capex falls back, the price may already reflect it. The value of "now" lies in the maximum mismatch between information and price.

This is also why I do not recommend "waiting for a better price"—during the wait, the information that advertising is accelerating will become consensus, and the price will move first.

Question 3: Under what conditions would we change our mind?

Three conditions, each verifiable against a clear threshold: ① Marketing services growth below 15% for two consecutive quarters (the advertising foundation is collapsing); ② Capex above RMB 50 billion for three consecutive quarters with cloud growth below 15% (spending with no return); ③ Non-IFRS net profit turning negative YoY (the core business starts shrinking).

Why these three? They map to my three core assumptions: advertising holds up, AI is an investment, the core business is intact. If an assumption is overturned, exit, regardless of price.

No fourth condition of "the stock falls below X": price is an outcome, not a cause. Using price as a condition means selling at the cheapest point.

Question 4: What if we get it wrong?

Two layers of protection. The first is position sizing: an initial 4% position means even a halving costs the portfolio 2%. The second is a hard stop: HKD 330 (-25%) triggers an unconditional halving of the position — not because it will necessarily get there, but because at that level the market knows something I don't.

There is also an asymmetric protection: net cash of RMB 58.19 billion plus a USD 72 billion investment portfolio, roughly RMB 570 billion, or 15% of market cap. That means at HKD 330 you are effectively buying the core business at 9x, with the remaining assets thrown in for free.

Add up all three layers and the worst case is a 2%–3% portfolio loss. That number is bearable, so this investment is actionable.

Review mechanism: what to look back at after three months

Watch three things: (1) Q3 capex — RMB 40 billion or RMB 55 billion; (2) marketing services growth — 20% or 15%; (3) operating cash flow — RMB 70 billion or RMB 50 billion. These three numbers decide whether this call is half right or half wrong.

Also watch one softer item: whether Xiaowei moves from gray release to scale. It is the only leading indicator for judging whether the entry point can be bypassed, and it arrives earlier than any financial number.

A post-mortem is not about proving yourself right; it is about identifying which assumption broke first. Whichever broke first is what the next analysis must focus on.

One-sentence executive version

If you remember only one line: Buy half at 440, fill up at 400, stop at 480, cut half at 535, admit you are wrong at 330, walk away if advertising falls below 15%.

These six numbers tie together valuation, odds, discipline and risk control. No need to watch the tape the rest of the time — watch the earnings.

Why I do not set a "target price ceiling"

Some will ask: what if it rises to HKD 600? You do not sell out entirely. 535 is the "halve" level, not the "liquidate" level. The valuation denominator is growing—Non-IFRS could reach RMB 360 billion in 2028, at which point 535 is only 13.5x.

Setting a "ceiling" makes you exit the best companies too early. The lesson from Tencent over the past fifteen years is that selling too soon is more common than getting stuck holding. So the discipline should be "halve on the way up, keep a core position," not "liquidate once the target price is hit."

Conclusion

Decision framework, concluded: value at 535, willing to pay 440, three falsification conditions, two layers of stop-loss protection. Together these four answers make an executable buy decision rather than a judgment that it "feels cheap."
