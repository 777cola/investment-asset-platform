## Upstream: compute shifts from a cost line to a bottleneck

The biggest change in this chain in 2026 sits upstream. AI training and inference demand has exploded, leaving GPUs, HBM and advanced process nodes all tight. Chipmakers' gross margins have climbed to around 70%, while buyers of cards must pay upfront and wait for delivery. Tencent's Q2 capex was RMB 52.784 billion against cash paid of RMB 59.3 billion; the RMB 6.5 billion excess is the "queue fee."

A more expensive upstream directly redistributes profit along the chain: for the first time in the past decade, internet profit is flowing from midstream platforms back upstream to hardware. In Tencent's financials this shows up as revenue +11% and gross profit +13%, but free cash flow of -RMB 13.8 billion.

## Midstream: the platform layer remains the most profitable position in the chain

Tencent's gross margin is 57.8%, 40 percentage points above server manufacturers and 45 percentage points above ad agencies. The reason is simple: the platform holds the power to decide "who gets seen." What an advertiser pays for a single impression depends on the size of the platform's user base and the accuracy of its algorithm, not on how expensive the servers are.

The midstream is itself stratified: Tencent and ByteDance form the first tier (owned traffic + owned content), Kuaishou and Bilibili the second (traffic, but low monetisation efficiency), and Meituan is a "local-life platform" that, beyond traffic, must also support riders. All are called platforms, yet their asset lightness differs tenfold.

## Downstream: advertisers are getting poorer, and smarter

Downstream sits advertisers, merchants and developers, and what they share in 2026 is tighter budgets and a sharper focus on ROI. That favours platforms with a closed loop that can drive transactions directly, and penalises those that only sell impressions.

Tencent's advertising grew 22% this quarter, capturing precisely this shift: WeChat Mini Shops, Mini Programs and Channels complete the "see-click-order" path, and advertisers pay a premium for it. Pure brand budgets (splash screens, pre-roll) are migrating to short video and e-commerce.

Another downstream change: AI has cut creative production costs by 80% (company figure), letting advertisers rotate creative more frequently and accelerating the platform's inventory turnover. This is the first real AI benefit to the advertising business, and it already shows in gross margin.

## Tencent's position in the value chain: rent collector and rent payer

Tencent plays two roles at once. Rent collector: it takes money from advertisers (RMB 43.57 billion/quarter), from gamers (RMB 65.9 billion/quarter) and fees from merchants (included in RMB 60.29 billion). Rent payer: it buys compute from chipmakers (RMB 52.78 billion/quarter), racks from IDCs and content from copyright holders.

In 2026 the ratio between these two roles shifts: the rent-paying portion rises from 10% of revenue (RMB 19.11 billion in 2025Q2) to 25.8%. This is not a deteriorating business model; it is buying land for the next stretch of rent collection. But the land is paid for out of current cash flow, and shareholders bear the gap in the interim.

## Cycle position: upstream at the top, midstream at the bottom

Upstream compute: late-cycle peak. Chip prices, lead times and cloud capex all sit at highs, and history says that when everyone is scrambling for cards, surplus follows two years later. Midstream platforms: valuation trough. The Hang Seng Tech Index is down more than 25% year to date, Tencent 35%, and 13x P/E marks the bottom of a ten-year range.

The dislocation is striking: upstream gross margins come from midstream heavy spending; midstream low valuations come from upstream high costs. If compute turns surplus and prices fall in 2027, profit flows back to the midstream — by then Tencent's depreciation will already be booked, procurement costs will drop, and gross margin and cash flow will improve together. This is the most underappreciated thread in the investment case.

## Content and IP: Tencent's second upstream

Beyond compute, Tencent owns a second upstream layer: content — in-house studios (TiMi, Lightspeed, Aurora), stakes in Supercell, Riot and Epic, and film, TV and music rights. Tencent has pricing power at this layer: it both buys and produces content, and can steer traffic to content owners through its distribution platforms.

The evidence this quarter: domestic games RMB 47.3 billion, +17%, with four titles live simultaneously, three of them self-developed. The content upstream needs no external sourcing and keeps gross profit in-house. A constrained compute upstream versus a self-sufficient content upstream — two entirely different moats.

## Why doesn't Tencent make chips?

This is the question we hear most often across the supply chain. The answer lies in gross margins: chips at 70%, platform at 57.8%. Chips look more profitable, but chips are a business of "heavy R&D + strong cyclicality + customers who are competitors." For Tencent to build its own cards would mean competing simultaneously with its own suppliers (NVIDIA, Huawei) and its customers (cloud-using manufacturers).

Its choice is "buy cards + leverage use cases," leaving uncertainty to upstream and keeping certainty (1.439 billion users) for itself. That choice hurts when compute is scarce (costs rise) and pays off when compute is in surplus (costs fall). Long term, this is the right choice—because compute will ultimately be in surplus.

## Counter-scenario: if compute stays expensive forever

Assume compute prices stay flat for three years and delivery lead times remain tight. Tencent's capex would then hold above RMB 50 billion per quarter, depreciation would climb by RMB 38 billion a year, gross margin would compress to 55%, and free cash flow would hover near 0 over the long term. Under this scenario, Tencent's fair valuation is 10–11x, implying HKD 300–330.

This is a stress test, not a forecast. The takeaway: compute price is the single most important variable for this stock, ahead of advertising growth or game grossings. So each quarter I look first at capex and second at cloud revenue growth—one sets cost, the other sets revenue.
