## Upstream: compute shifts from a cost line to a bottleneck

The biggest change in this chain in 2026 is upstream. AI training and inference demand has exploded, and GPUs, HBM and advanced process nodes are all tight. Chipmakers' gross margins have climbed to around 70%, while buyers of cards must pay upfront and wait for delivery. Tencent's Q2 capex was RMB 52.784 billion, with cash paid of RMB 59.3 billion; the RMB 6.5 billion excess is the "queue fee."

The direct consequence of a more expensive upstream is a redistribution of profit along the chain: for the first time in the past decade, internet profit is flowing from midstream platforms back upstream to hardware. In Tencent's financials this shows up as revenue +11% and gross profit +13%, but free cash flow of -RMB 13.8 billion.

## Midstream: the platform layer remains the most profitable position in the entire chain

Tencent's gross margin is 57.8%, 40 percentage points above server manufacturers and 45 percentage points above ad agencies. The reason is simple: the platform holds the power to decide "who gets seen." How much an advertiser will pay for a single impression depends on how many users the platform has and how accurate its algorithm is, not on how expensive the servers are.

The midstream is itself stratified: Tencent and ByteDance form the first tier (owned traffic + owned content), Kuaishou and Bilibili the second tier (traffic but low monetization efficiency), and Meituan is a "local-life platform" (beyond traffic, it must also support riders). All are called platforms, yet their asset lightness differs tenfold.

## Downstream: advertisers are getting poorer, and smarter

Downstream are advertisers, merchants and developers. What they share in 2026 is tighter budgets and a sharper focus on ROI. This favors platforms with a closed loop (able to drive transactions directly) and penalizes platforms that only sell impressions.

Tencent's advertising grew 22% this quarter, precisely capturing this shift: WeChat Mini Shops, Mini Programs and Channels complete the "see-click-order" path, and advertisers will pay a premium for it. Pure brand advertising (splash screens, pre-roll) budgets are migrating to short video and e-commerce.

Another downstream change: AI has cut creative production costs by 80% (company figure), letting advertisers rotate creative more frequently and accelerating the platform's inventory turnover. This is the first real AI benefit to the advertising business, and it already shows in gross margin.

## Tencent's position in the chain: rent collector + rent payer

Tencent plays two roles at once. Rent collector: it takes money from advertisers (RMB 43.57 billion/quarter), from gamers (RMB 65.9 billion/quarter), and fees from merchants (included in RMB 60.29 billion). Rent payer: it buys compute from chipmakers (RMB 52.78 billion/quarter), racks from IDCs, and content from copyright holders.

In 2026 the ratio between these two roles shifts: the rent-paying portion rises from 10% of revenue (RMB 19.11 billion in 2025Q2) to 25.8%. This is not a deteriorating business model; it is buying land for the next stretch of rent collection. But the land is paid for out of current cash flow, and shareholders bear the gap in the interim.

## Cycle position: upstream at the top, midstream at the bottom

Upstream compute: late-cycle peak. Chip prices, lead times and cloud capex all sit at highs. History says when everyone is scrambling for cards, surplus follows two years later. Midstream platforms: valuation trough. The Hang Seng Tech Index has fallen more than 25% year to date; Tencent is down 35%, and 13x PE marks the bottom of a ten-year range.

The dislocation is striking: upstream gross margins come from midstream heavy spending; midstream low valuations come from upstream high costs. If compute turns surplus and prices fall in 2027, profit flows back to the midstream — by then Tencent's depreciation will already be booked, procurement costs will drop, and gross margin and cash flow will improve together. This is the most underappreciated thread in the investment case.

## Content and IP: Tencent's second upstream

Beyond compute, Tencent has another upstream layer: content — in-house studios (TiMi, Lightspeed, Aurora), invested studios (Supercell, Riot, Epic), and film, TV and music rights. Tencent has pricing power at this upstream layer — it both buys and produces content, and can channel traffic to content owners through its distribution platforms.

The evidence this quarter: domestic games RMB 47.3 billion, +17%, with four titles online simultaneously, three of them self-developed. This shows the content upstream layer needs no external sourcing, and gross profit stays in-house. A constrained compute upstream versus a self-sufficient content upstream — two entirely different moats.

## Why doesn't Tencent make chips?

This is the most frequently asked question across the industry chain. The answer lies in gross margins: chips at 70%, platform at 57.8%. Chips look more profitable, but chips are a business of "heavy R&D + strong cyclicality + customers who are competitors." For Tencent to build its own cards would mean competing simultaneously with its own suppliers (NVIDIA, Huawei) and its customers (cloud-using manufacturers).

Its choice is "buy cards + leverage use cases," leaving uncertainty to upstream and keeping certainty (1.439 billion users) for itself. This choice hurts when compute is scarce (costs rise) and pays off when compute is in surplus (costs fall). Long term, this is the right choice—because compute will ultimately be in surplus.

## Counter-scenario: if compute stays expensive forever

Assume compute prices do not fall for three years and delivery lead times stay tight. Tencent's capex would stay above RMB 50 billion per quarter, depreciation would rise by RMB 38 billion a year, gross margin would be compressed to 55%, and free cash flow would hover near 0 long term. Under this scenario, Tencent's fair valuation is 10–11x, corresponding to HKD 300–330.

This is not a forecast; it is a stress test. It tells me: compute price is the single most important variable for this stock, more important than advertising growth or game grossings. So on every quarterly report, I look first at capex and second at cloud revenue growth—one determines cost, the other determines revenue.
