Core view

Buffett reads financial statements in a fixed order: first, whether the cash can be collected; then, whether returns on capital are high; finally, whether the moat will be eroded. This report fails at the first hurdle.

ROIC is a rough calculation: NOPAT = operating profit × (1 - 23%), invested capital = equity + long-term debt - cash; not verified line by line.
Gate one: cash flow. He made no exception.

TTM operating cash flow was RMB 168.3 billion against capex of RMB 210.7 billion, a gap of -RMB 42.4 billion. Buffett's verdict on companies like this is well known: "Profit is an opinion, cash is a fact." By that standard, Amazon's 2026 "fact" is negative.

In fairness, operating cash flow itself grew 28.7% YoY, so the business is generating cash and capex is what consumed it. Buffett has never bought a company with negative operating cash flow, but he has bought capital-intensive businesses with predictable returns — BNSF Railway, Berkshire Energy. The difference is certainty of returns: railways hold right-of-way contracts; Amazon has a USD 578 billion order book.An order book is not contracted revenue, but it is far firmer ground than a "vision."

Gate two and gate three: is a 15% ROIC good or bad

ROIC is roughly 15% on our back-of-envelope calculation; we assume a 6%–7% cost of capital (RMB 173.1 billion of long-term debt, c.RMB 6.8 billion of annualised interest expense, a blended cost of about 4%, and a WACC of about 8% once cost of equity is included). 15% against 8% leaves 7 points of excess return — the hurdle is cleared.

The trend is less flattering: invested capital, the denominator, is expanding at RMB 77.2 billion a quarter, while NOPAT, the numerator, will not catch up for two to three years. ROIC therefore falls before it rises in 2027, and the trough sits in the middle of that stretch. The third hurdle, moat, is not in dispute — switching clouds costs customers years, and replicating the logistics network costs hundreds of billions.

Conclusion

Close-reading verdict: two of three tests passed, the first failed. That is not a "fail" so much as "outside his comfort zone." How would he handle it? Most likely a small position held for the long term rather than a heavy one, because the company's cash-flow profile breaks one of his most basic disciplines. The takeaway for ordinary investors is the same: size the position small enough that "even if 2027 depreciation knocks earnings back, it does not affect your life."
