Core thesis

Amazon's moat is not a single asset but a set of interlocking gears: the logistics network drives cost leadership, cost leadership enables lower prices, lower prices bring more traffic, traffic sells higher-priced advertising, and cash flow from advertising and retail funds AWS's capex, while AWS's scale further lowers technology costs across the whole company. What is hardest to replicate is this interlocking itself—not any single point.

Five-dimension score 41/50. The weakest link is not chips but power and permits: data centers can be built, but grid connections queue for three to five years, and on this Amazon stands in the same queue as every cloud provider.

Position in the value chain: buyer, seller and infrastructure provider at once

Most companies occupy a single position: they either buy chips to deliver services (cloud providers) or sell goods for a margin (retailers). Amazon occupies three at once—it buys chips (as one of the world's largest buyers), sells compute (AWS), sells goods (retail) and sells advertising (traffic monetisation).Vertical integration is a moat when supply is tight: others cannot secure capacity, but Amazon can; it becomes a burden when demand weakens, because self-built capacity cannot sit idle.

In 2026, the position is the former. On the earnings call, Jassy stressed that "owning or long-term leasing most of our data center capacity, combined with our use of custom silicon, gives us a structural cost advantage over cloud providers that rely more on colocation or new types of cloud arrangements." That statement is aimed squarely at Microsoft and CoreWeave—when compute is scarce, owned capacity is cheaper than rented and guarantees delivery.

The limits of this advantage are equally clear: once compute is in surplus, self-built heavy assets become a depreciation burden, while asset-light rivals can brake immediately. That is why its moat score is 41 rather than 46—its moat is widest at the top of the cycle and narrowest at the bottom.

Breaking it down item by item: the true strength of the four strong moats

Cost advantage 9/10. This is the hardest advantage to replicate, and the data validates it every year: shipping cost +11% against unit volume +12%, and shipping cost up only +8% after stripping out fuel and line-haul inflation. North America retail operating margin expanded from 4.3% to 7.7% without raising prices. A rival would need hundreds of billions of dollars and decades to replicate this network, and would still have to clear land-use and environmental review in every US state.

Switching costs 9/10. Once AWS customers move their core systems onto the platform, changing clouds means "rewriting everything plus downtime risk," and the decision cycle is measured in years. A backlog of RMB 578 billion, locked through 2027–2028, is the quantified proof of this moat. But note: switching costs protect the installed base, not the incremental base. For a new customer's first workload, AWS has no advantage at all.

Network effects 9/10. Third-party sellers contribute 61% of unit volume; they come because buyers are here, and buyers come because sellers are numerous — a classic two-sided network. Advertising is the network's second monetisation: RMB 19.64 billion in revenue, 12% growth, very high gross margin and no additional capex required.

Brand 8/10. The deduction reflects not a weak brand but one that does not work in the low-price segment: Temu and Shein have taken a slice of price-sensitive buyers on price, and those buyers were originally Amazon's base.

The most dangerous competitor is Microsoft, followed by Google

Microsoft is the real rival, and the reason lies in enterprise customers. AWS is strong with internet-native companies and small and medium businesses; Microsoft is strong in the installed IT budgets of Fortune 500 companies. As AI spending shifts from "innovation projects" to "IT infrastructure," decision rights return to the CIO—and the CIO's default option is Microsoft, because email, Office and Azure AD all live there. Azure grew 48% this quarter, 9 percentage points faster than AWS; that is no accident.

Google is the second threat, different in nature: Google Cloud grew 103% to RMB 30.7 billion, powered by the vertical integration of in-house TPU plus Gemini. Its threat is not taking over existing customers but setting a new price anchor—if TPU's cost per unit of compute is lower than Trainium's, AWS's pricing power will come under pressure.

The third layer of threat is asset structure. Microsoft cut its 2026 capex guidance from RMB 175 billion to RMB 170 billion and is using more third-party compute; Amazon chose to build RMB 220 billion itself. If demand growth slows in 2027, Microsoft's assets are lighter and it can pivot faster; this is not to say Amazon will lose, but that it is betting on a longer demand cycle.

The retail rivals (Walmart, Temu, Shein) do not pose an existential threat: Walmart's grocery share is a defensive advantage, and Temu's price war hits the low-price segment; neither can touch the "fast + broad + membership" combination.

Conclusion

Competitive conclusion: the moat is wide enough, but directional—widest when compute is scarce, narrowest when compute is in surplus. A score of 41/50 puts it in the first tier, but not impregnable. The threat is not retail rivals; it is Azure's growth gap (currently 9 percentage points) and Google Cloud's price anchor (unit cost behind the +103%). Those two indicators determine how long AWS's 38.7% margin can hold.
