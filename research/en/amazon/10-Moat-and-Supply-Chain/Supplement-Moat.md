## Position in the value chain: it is simultaneously a buyer, a seller and infrastructure

Most companies occupy only one position: either they buy chips to deliver services (cloud providers) or they sell goods for a margin (retailers). Amazon occupies three at once—it buys chips (one of the world's largest buyers), sells compute (AWS), sells goods (retail), and sells advertising (traffic monetization).**Vertical integration is a moat when supply is tight: others cannot secure capacity, but it can; it is a burden when demand weakens: self-built capacity cannot sit idle.**

In 2026, the position is the former. On the earnings call, Jassy stressed that "owning or long-term leasing most of our data center capacity, combined with our use of custom silicon, gives us a structural cost advantage over cloud providers that rely more on colocation or new types of cloud arrangements." That statement is aimed squarely at Microsoft and CoreWeave—**when compute is scarce, owned capacity is cheaper than rented and guarantees delivery.**

The limits of this advantage are equally clear: once compute is in surplus, self-built heavy assets become a depreciation burden, while asset-light rivals can brake immediately. That is why its moat score is 41 rather than 46—**its moat is widest at the top of the cycle and narrowest at the bottom.**

## Breaking it down item by item: the true strength of the four strong moats

**Cost advantage 9/10.** This is the hardest one, because the data validates it every year: shipping cost +11%, unit volume +12%, and shipping cost only +8% after stripping out fuel and line-haul inflation. North America retail operating margin went from 4.3% to 7.7%, achieved without raising prices. A rival would need to replicate this network at a cost in the hundreds of billions of dollars and a timeline in decades, and would still have to clear land-use and environmental review in every US state.

**Switching costs 9/10.** Once AWS customers move their core systems onto the platform, changing clouds means "rewriting everything plus downtime risk," and the decision cycle is measured in years. Backlog of RMB 578 billion, locked through 2027–2028, is the quantified proof of this moat. **But note: switching costs protect the installed base, not the incremental base.** For a new customer's first workload, AWS has no advantage at all.

**Network effects 9/10.** Third-party sellers contribute 61% of unit volume; they come because buyers are here, and buyers come because sellers are numerous — a classic two-sided network. Advertising is the network's second monetization: RMB 19.64 billion in revenue, 12% growth, very high gross margin and no additional capex required.

**Brand 8/10.** The deduction is not because the brand is weak, but because the brand does not work in the low-price segment: Temu and Shein have taken a slice of price-sensitive buyers with price, and those buyers were originally Amazon's base.

## The most dangerous competitor: Microsoft, followed by Google

**Microsoft is the real rival, and the reason lies in enterprise customers.** AWS is strong with internet-native companies and small and medium businesses; Microsoft is strong in the installed IT budgets of Fortune 500 companies. When AI spending shifts from "innovation projects" to "IT infrastructure," decision rights return to the CIO, and the CIO's default option is Microsoft—because email, Office and Azure AD all live there. Azure grew 48% this quarter, 9 percentage points faster than AWS; that is no accident.

Google is the second threat, different in nature: Google Cloud grew 103% to RMB 30.7 billion, powered by the vertical integration of in-house TPU plus Gemini. Its threat is not taking over existing customers but **setting a new price anchor**—if TPU's cost per unit of compute is lower than Trainium's, AWS's pricing power will come under pressure.

The third layer of threat is asset structure. Microsoft cut its 2026 capex guidance from RMB 175 billion to RMB 170 billion and is using more third-party compute; Amazon chose to build RMB 220 billion itself. **If demand growth slows in 2027, Microsoft's assets are lighter and it can pivot faster; this is not to say Amazon will lose, but that it is betting on a longer demand cycle.**

The retail rivals (Walmart, Temu, Shein) do not pose an existential threat: Walmart's grocery share is a defensive advantage, and Temu's price war hits the low-price segment; neither can touch the "fast + broad + membership" combination.
