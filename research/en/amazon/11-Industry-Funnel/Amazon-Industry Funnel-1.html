Core view

Screening on the "AI + cash flow" thesis leaves five candidates: Amazon, Microsoft, Alphabet, Meta and Alibaba. The rough screen eliminates three — Meta has no external cloud, Alibaba is split by geography, and JD.com is retail-only, a single segment — leaving a choice between Amazon and Microsoft.

The case for Amazon is not growth — Azure grew 48% versus AWS at 39.3%. It is the valuation gap and business completeness: Amazon trades at 20.8x TTM, the lowest multiple of the three cloud businesses, and pairs a retail traffic pool with advertising monetisation, making it the only company that "generates cash from all three legs." Microsoft is the better company, but paying 25.8x for 48% growth offers worse odds.

Initial screen: first cut companies with only one engine

The first layer of the funnel is not valuation; it is business structure. Returns on this round of AI investment can only be recouped from two places, cloud and advertising, so a candidate must have at least one independently verifiable engine.

Meta is the first to be cut: all of its AI capex serves ad recommendation, it has no external cloud business, and outside investors cannot verify "how much these chips earn." That is not to say it is a bad company, but that you cannot reconcile it against the financials. JD.com is cut: a single segment in the retail chain, no cloud, no high-margin advertising, cheap at 7.5x P/E but with no re-rating engine. Alibaba is cut: its number-one China cloud share is real, but cloud profit has never been disclosed separately, and the China internet discount and geopolitical risk render the valuation anchor ineffective.

The three that remain (Amazon, Microsoft, Alphabet) share one trait: all can be reconciled using the two numbers "cloud revenue + cloud margin." That is the necessary condition for reaching the final selection.

Final pick: Why Amazon, not Microsoft

This is the hardest comparison, because Microsoft wins on many dimensions: Azure grew 48% versus AWS at 39.3%, its enterprise relationships run deeper, and its asset base is lighter (capex was even cut by 5 billion). If the only question is "who is better," the answer is Microsoft.

But buying a stock means buying the gap between price and expectations. Three sets of numbers: One, valuation — Amazon at 20.8x versus Microsoft at 25.8x, a 24% gap; Two, business completeness — Amazon has a retail traffic pool and advertising (RMB 19.64 billion, +12%), while Microsoft has no consumer traffic; Three, expectation gap — the market reads Microsoft's capex cut as "rational" and Amazon's capex increase as "burning money," so sentiment around the two is completely different.

Alphabet is a third case: Google Cloud's +103% is the standout number, but cloud is only a small share of group revenue (RMB 30.7 billion against a group in the hundred-billion range), and its valuation anchor is search advertising, not cloud. Buying Alphabet means buying the present value of search plus an option on cloud; buying Amazon means buying the present value of cloud plus retail efficiency gains — the latter is easier to underwrite.

"Why it" and "when it is not"

Three reasons. One, scale: AWS at USD 46 billion a quarter, USD 184 billion annualised, remains the world's largest, and scale itself is a cost advantage. Two, structure: it is the only company generating three cash flows simultaneously — retail, advertising and cloud — so if one falters, the other two cushion it. Three, price: 20.8x TTM (34x excluding unrealised gains) is the lowest of the three cloud players, with an upside/downside ratio of 1.63 to 1.

When does it stop being the answer? Two cases: AWS growth below 30% for two consecutive quarters while Azure is still above 40%, signalling share loss; free cash flow negative for four consecutive quarters with capex still accelerating, signalling a structural loss rather than cyclical investment. If both conditions appear, switch to Microsoft or exit the sector outright.

Conclusion

Funnel conclusion: six in, three out, two finalists, one selected. Amazon wins on "the most complete business structure + the lowest valuation," not on "the fastest growth." This is an investment in "the best structure at a fair price," not "the fastest growth at a cheap price." If AWS growth falls below 30% in the first half of 2027, this choice needs to be redone.
