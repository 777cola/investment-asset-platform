# Amazon（AMZN）— Duan Yongping Supplement

- Research date：2026/11/06
- Price：259.3 USD
- Market cap：About USD 2.80 trillion

## Notes

This page rewrites the Q&A using Duan Yongping's publicly expressed habits of thought (benfen, equanimity, don't do what you don't understand, use position sizing to control uncertainty); these are not his own words. All data come from Amazon's 2026Q3 earnings report and conference call (2026/10/29–30) and batchdata daily bars (2026/11/06).

## Is this a good business?

Good. Where is the good? All three segments make money, and each makes it from a different group. Retail earns from efficiency, advertising from traffic, AWS from assets. **A business that earns three kinds of money at once, and earns more from each than peers, is not luck.**

The number I care about most: unit volume grew 12%, shipping costs grew only 11%, and after stripping out higher fuel prices and line-haul rate increases, shipping costs grew just 8%. What does that tell you? Scale is turning into efficiency, not bureaucracy. Many companies get dumber as they get bigger; this one gets leaner as it gets bigger, and that is not easy.

## Will it still be around in ten years?

Yes, it will. But "still there" and "still good" are two different things. In ten years Amazon will certainly still be selling goods, and certainly still selling cloud; demand for both will not disappear. The question is whether its margins will still be at today's level.

I judge that AWS will still be around in ten years, because once customers move their systems onto it, moving off is hard — much like switching banks, which is a real hassle. **But I am less certain that in ten years the cloud market will still be a fight among these three.** When technology changes fast, moats get shallower. So I will not take a heavy position because it "will still be around in ten years"; I will buy because it "is cheap now."

## Can you understand it?

I understand it, but there is one part I do not quite understand. Selling goods, selling advertising, selling compute — I can explain all three in one sentence, and I can calculate how much each earns. What I cannot understand is that it spends USD 220 billion a year buying land, building facilities and buying chips, then tells me 2028 will be good.

It is not that I do not believe it — I **cannot verify it**. The order book of USD 578 billion is real, but orders are not revenue; customers can delay. So I put it in the "70% understandable" category — enough to buy, not enough to hold a heavy position. **Do not fill the parts you do not understand with faith; fill them with position size.**

## At what price to buy?

At USD 259, the price is neither cheap nor expensive. A P/E of 20.8x looks undemanding, but note that this includes USD 68.3 billion of unrealized gains on Anthropic; strip that out and it is closer to 34x. Paying 34x for a cloud business growing 40% is defensible, but there is no bargain to be had.

My approach is simple: **buy one-third first, add on a drop to USD 216, trim on a rise to USD 330.** Why not buy the full position at once? Because I cannot see clearly what depreciation will look like in 2027. Why not wait for a decline before buying? Because good companies rarely offer you a cheap price, and those who wait often end up paying more. **Scaling in is not indecision; it is an admission that I cannot get the numbers exactly right.**

## What is the biggest fear?

The biggest fear is not AI demand falling off, but **treating the compute shortage as permanent**. Right now compute sells out the moment it is installed — a nice line, but every "sells out on installation" business in history has ended up as "installed and nobody uses it."

The second fear is accounting. Stretching server depreciation from five years to six immediately makes profit look better by several billion, with not a cent more cash flow. It is not illegal, but it will make you miscalculate. So when I buy this kind of company, I always read the notes first, not the news.

The third fear, frankly, is that it is so good it is too expensive. **Buying a good company at a high price still loses money — just more slowly.**

## If you could look at only one metric, which would it be?

Look at AWS's order book, the backlog. It now stands at USD 578 billion, growing triple digits year over year. This number leads revenue, is harder to fake than profit, and is more honest than the share price.

Once it declines quarter over quarter for two consecutive quarters, customers are deferring or cancelling, and you still have two quarters to get out. Conversely, if it keeps rising, hold with confidence and ignore the share price swings.**An indicator that lets you sleep at night is a good indicator.** As for those who ask you every day "why is it up/down today," I generally pay them little attention—**Mr. Market's mood is not information, it is noise.**
