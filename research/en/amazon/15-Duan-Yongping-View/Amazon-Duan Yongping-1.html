Core view

Run Amazon through Duan Yongping's six questions and the answers converge on one line: about 70% of the business is understandable, the price is not cheap, and the biggest fear is treating the "compute shortage" as a permanent condition. The honest old approach is the right response—buy in tranches, and let position sizing cover the 30% that cannot be understood.

Is this a good business?

Good. But where exactly is the good? All three segments make money, each from a different source: retail earns from efficiency, advertising from traffic, AWS from assets. A business that earns three kinds of money at once, and earns more from each than peers, is not luck.

The number I care about most: unit volume grew 12%, shipping costs grew only 11%, and after stripping out higher fuel prices and line-haul rate increases, shipping costs grew just 8%. What does that tell you? Scale is turning into efficiency, not bureaucracy. Many companies get dumber as they get bigger; this one gets leaner as it gets bigger, and that is not easy.

Will it still be around in ten years?

Yes, it will. But "still there" and "still good" are two different things. In ten years Amazon will certainly still be selling goods, and certainly still selling cloud; demand for both will not disappear. The question is whether its margins will still be at today's level.

I judge that AWS will still be around in ten years: once customers move their systems onto it, moving off is hard — much like switching banks, which is a real hassle. But I am less certain that in ten years the cloud market will still be a fight among these three. When technology changes fast, moats get shallower. So I will not take a heavy position because it "will still be around in ten years"; I will buy because it "is cheap now."

Can you understand it?

I follow the story, but one piece does not quite land. Selling goods, selling advertising, selling compute — I can explain all three in a sentence and calculate what each earns. What I cannot get comfortable with is USD 220 billion a year on land, facilities and chips, paired with a promise that 2028 will be good.

It is not that I disbelieve it — I cannot verify it. The USD 578 billion order book is real, but orders are not revenue; customers can push delivery. So it sits in my "70% understandable" bucket — enough to buy, not enough to carry a heavy position. Do not fill the parts you do not understand with faith; fill them with position size.

At what price to buy?

At USD 259 the stock is neither cheap nor expensive. A P/E of 20.8x looks undemanding, but that includes USD 68.3 billion of unrealized gains on Anthropic; strip those out and the multiple is closer to 34x. Paying 34x for a cloud business growing 40% is defensible, but there is no bargain on offer.

My approach is simple: buy one-third first, add on a drop to USD 216, trim on a rise to USD 330. Why not buy the full position at once? Because I cannot see clearly what depreciation will look like in 2027. Why not wait for a decline? Because good companies rarely offer a cheap entry, and those who wait often end up paying more. Scaling in is not indecision; it is an admission that I cannot get the numbers exactly right.

What is the biggest fear?

The biggest fear is not AI demand rolling over, but treating the compute shortage as permanent. Compute sells out the moment it is installed — a nice line, except that every "sells out on installation" business in history has ended up as "installed and nobody uses it."

The second fear is accounting. Stretching server depreciation from five years to six immediately flatters profit by several billion, with not a cent more cash flow. It is not illegal, but it will make you miscalculate. So when I buy this kind of company, I read the notes first, not the news.

The third fear, frankly, is that it is so good it is too expensive. Buying a good company at a high price still loses money — just more slowly.

If you could look at only one metric, which would it be?

Look at the AWS backlog. At USD 578 billion, it is growing triple digits YoY. This number leads revenue, is harder to fake than profit and is more honest than the share price.

Once it falls QoQ for two consecutive quarters, customers are deferring or cancelling, and you still have two quarters to get out. If it keeps rising, hold with confidence and ignore the share price swings.An indicator that lets you sleep at night is a good indicator. As for those who ask every day "why is it up/down today," I pay them little attention—Mr. Market's mood is not information, it is noise.

Conclusion

One-line takeaway: good business, good company, fair price. Buy a third at USD 259, add below USD 216, trim above USD 330. Where the picture is unclear — 2027 depreciation, the timing of peak capex — don't fill the gap with faith; fill it with position sizing. It is the dumbest approach, and the hardest to get wrong.
