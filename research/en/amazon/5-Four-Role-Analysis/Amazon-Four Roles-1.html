What makes this a good business: who pays, why they pay, and how long they keep paying

Core view

Amazon's business can be summed up in one sentence: sell the most goods at the lowest fulfillment cost, then monetise the resulting traffic, data and compute a second time each. Retail earns efficiency money, advertising earns traffic money and AWS earns asset money. The three serve entirely different customers but share the same infrastructure.

The first rule of a good business: customers willingly pay again

Duan Yongping's first test of a business is crude: do customers come back of their own accord. Amazon's answer is a fulfilment density of 1.5 billion same-day or next-day items in a single quarter, Amazon Now covering more than 450 towns with order volume up tenfold YoY, and Alexa for Shopping with over 400 million users in the past 12 months and interactions up 570% YoY. What these numbers share is "repeat," not "new."

Pricing power is a harder test. Subscription revenue was USD 14 billion, up 11% YoY; the annual fee has risen and churn has not blown out. Fresh groceries and daily essentials grew three times as fast as other categories and account for about half of unit volume — these are the highest-frequency repeat purchases, and once a habit forms, switching costs compound on their own.

The second rule of a good business: scale has to convert into efficiency, or it becomes a burden

Retail is a hard business: the pain sets in when scale grows and costs spiral. The 2026Q3 numbers show Amazon kept control this time: unit volume +12%, shipping costs +11%, and excluding fuel and line-haul inflation, shipping costs rose just +8% — 400 basis points below unit volume. North America operating margin improved from 4.3% to 7.7%, international 3.7%. Per-order fulfillment cost fell for a fourth consecutive year — that sentence matters more than any growth rate.

The mechanism is verifiable: robotic warehouses, regionalized fulfillment (placing inventory closer to customers), package consolidation, same-day delivery density. These are not "strategy"; they are engineering. Duan Yongping would call this exactly "benfen" — doing what ought to be done to a degree others cannot match.

A second customer group comprises millions of third-party sellers.

Looking only at consumers misses half of this business. Third-party sellers accounted for 61% of global paid units and USD 46.49 billion of service revenue (+9%), paying for two things: traffic and fulfilment. This is a "rent collection" business with gross margins far above first-party retail, and Amazon does not need to carry inventory.

Duan Yongping would like this structure because it fits the principle of "getting others to do the work for you." Sellers bear inventory risk, pricing risk and return costs; Amazon provides only the shelf, the traffic and the delivery. Why are sellers willing? Because they cannot get the same density of buyers elsewhere. This is the network effect expressed in cash flow.

The risk sits at this same layer: sellers going multi-platform (TikTok Shop, Temu) would erode this rent base. There is no evidence of large-scale migration yet, but 2027 is worth watching for "whether third-party seller services growth falls below 8%."

Conclusion

The business itself scores 8. Its three segments reinforce each other, customers pay willingly and repeatedly, and scale is converting into efficiency rather than burden. Two deductions. First, the 7.7% retail margin was flattered by the Prime Day timing shift; 4Q will be the clean read. Second, AWS's high profit rests on compute supply falling short of demand — should that premise change, the business shifts from "collecting rent" to "heavy-asset manufacturing."
