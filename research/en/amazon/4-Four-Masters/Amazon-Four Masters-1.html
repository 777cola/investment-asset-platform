Four Masters — Amazon

Core thesis

The four masters would return three verdicts. Duan Yongping asks why customers come and why they stay—Amazon answers beautifully; Buffett looks at the moat and return on capital—a perfect moat, but returns are being diluted; Munger pushes the scenario to extremes and finds that even if AI demand halves, the company merely hurts, it does not die; Li Lu treats it as an infrastructure asset of the compute era, willing to pay a premium but only in a small position.

Consensus: three in favour, one abstention (Buffett frowns at negative free cash flow). Three-scenario valuation of USD 216 / 330 / 438, a payoff ratio of 1.63 to 1. Enough to buy, not enough to go heavy.

Duan Yongping: taking fundamentals to the extreme — the clumsy way

Duan Yongping never starts with growth; he starts with why users come. Amazon's answer is so plain it needs no explanation: cheap, fast, everything. It has done these three things for thirty years, and in 2026Q3 it proved it still is—1.5 billion same-day or next-day orders in a single quarter, with Amazon Now covering more than 450 towns and order volume up tenfold YoY.

The more valuable question is "why don't they leave once they're here". Prime subscription revenue was USD 14 billion, up 11% YoY, and churn stayed low even after the annual fee rose—direct evidence of pricing power. He also likes one detail: unit sales +12%, shipping costs only +11%, and excluding fuel and line-haul inflation shipping costs +8%, 400 basis points below unit sales. Scale is turning into efficiency, not bureaucracy.

On negative free cash flow, his question would not be "has cash flow turned positive" but "can the money spent be recovered". Here he needs one answer: AWS backlog of USD 578 billion, triple-digit growth YoY, with capacity locked through 2027–2028. The order book is evidence that the money can be recovered, not a promise. So he would most likely say: the business is understandable, the price is not cheap, buy in tranches.

Buffett: a perfect moat, but returns on capital at a discount

Buffett would start with the nature of the business. AWS customers move core systems to the cloud, and switching costs amount to "rewrite everything plus downtime risk," so pricing power sits with the supplier — the same logic as an insurer that collects premiums before paying claims: cash in first, service later. The 578 billion backlog is essentially a contractual form of "float."

He would then calculate return on capital, and here he would frown. Operating profit of 28.08 billion looks good, but free cash flow is -42.4 billion; long-term debt rose from 65.6 billion to 173.1 billion, and interest expense rose to 1.69 billion in a single quarter, up 214% YoY. He accepted heavy assets when he bought the BNSF railway, because no one else can build the rail rights of way; but he would not accept a railway that must reinvest 300 billion every year just to sustain growth. That is the difference between the two.

The one detail he would press on is depreciation: servers are depreciated over 5–6 years, and 220 billion of capex turns into tens of billions of depreciation a year in 2027–2028. The assumed useful life determines whether the profit is real. "Is the profit real?" — that is why he would "hold" rather than "reject": he believes in the business, not in the durability of this income statement.

Munger: push the scenario to its extreme and the answer is "painful but not fatal"

Munger would not ask whether AI demand will persist; he would ask what happens if it halves next year. That scenario is modelable: AWS revenue growth slows from 39% to 15%, existing customers' routine workloads stay in place and revenue does not collapse—but the data centers already built and the chips already bought must be depreciated over 5–6 years, and that cost is rigid, pushing AWS operating margin from 38.7% down to around 25%.

At the group level, AWS operating profit falls from RMB 17.78 billion to about RMB 11.5 billion and group operating profit from RMB 28.08 billion to about RMB 21.8 billion, a decline of roughly 20%. Retail (RMB 8.72 billion) and advertising remain.That is the value of a diversified business—it does not raise the ceiling, it lowers the floor.

His second line of questioning: is management clear-eyed? Jassy volunteered two unfavorable facts on the earnings call—AI services carry lower margins than the core cloud business, and rising prices for components such as storage are creating pressure. The CFO also acknowledged that spending will "increase significantly" in 2027.Management willing to deliver bad news in its best quarter is usually more credible than management that only delivers good news.Munger's conclusion would be: you can buy it, but not to the full position—because the ceiling on this business is now set by someone else's capex.

Li Lu: he builds the railroad, sells the rails and collects the tolls — occupying all three roles at once

In Li Lu's framework, civilisation-scale technological shifts ultimately settle into a handful of infrastructure assets: right-of-way in the railway era, the grid in the electricity era, the cloud in the compute era. The cloud is closer to a toll booth than a railway — it has network effects, migration costs and usage-based pricing.

The historical lesson holds too: most railway builders went bankrupt; the money was made selling rails and collecting land rent. Amazon unusually plays all three roles at once — the railway builder (self-built data centers, long-term power contracts), the rail seller (Graviton/Trainium, sold externally by end-2027) and the toll collector (AWS billed by token and instance). If even one of the three holds, this investment does not go to zero.

He would grant tolerance on valuation but restraint on position size. The reason is common sense for Chinese investors: regulation and competition push infrastructure asset returns toward the average over time. Cloud competitors are Microsoft (Azure +48%) and Google Cloud (+103%); once a price war starts, the toll collector is cut first. So he is willing to pay 25x, not 35x.

Odds: 1.63 to 1, normal odds for large-cap companies

Neutral USD 330 (+27%), bear case USD 216 (-17%), odds = (330-259.30)/(259.30-216) = 70.7/43.3 ≈ 1.63 to 1. That is unimpressive. A USD 2.8 trillion market cap rarely offers 3 to 1, because the bad news is already in the price and the good news fully discussed. Anything above 1.5 is actionable — that is the reality of large caps.

The real risk is not the multiple but the denominator. The bear case assumes 2027 EPS of USD 12, roughly flat with TTM 12.46, which already embeds the assumption that depreciation eats growth. If depreciation is heavier than expected (say 2027 capex above USD 300 billion with unchanged useful lives), EPS could be just USD 10.8, and 20x P/E implies USD 216 — breaching the -17% floor. So entry must be staged: build one third first, and reserve the rest of the position for the price after the depreciation data is published.

Conclusion

Four-person consensus: buy, but only with one-third of the capital. Duan Yongping and Li Lu focus on the nature of the business (understandable, infrastructure). Munger focuses on the downside (painful but not fatal). Buffett focuses on earnings quality (depreciation unverified). All four perspectives point to the same action: build a base position near USD 260, with add-on conditions written as "2027Q1 capex and depreciation data + AWS growth still >35%". A break below USD 216 is an add-on zone, not a stop-loss zone—unless the order book starts to decline.
