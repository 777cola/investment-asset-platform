## Duan Yongping: This is the clumsy method of taking the fundamentals to the extreme

Duan Yongping never looks at growth first; he looks at why users come. Amazon's answer is so plain it needs no explanation: cheap, fast, everything. It has done these three things for thirty years, and in the third quarter of 2026 it proved it is still doing them with 1.5 billion same-day or next-day orders—1.5 billion in a single quarter, with Amazon Now covering more than 450 towns and order volume up tenfold year on year.

The question "why don't they leave once they're here" is worth more. Prime subscription revenue was USD 14 billion, up 11% year on year, and churn stayed low even when the annual fee rose—direct evidence of pricing power. He also likes one detail: **unit sales +12%, shipping costs only +11%, and excluding fuel and line-haul inflation shipping costs +8%, 400 basis points below unit sales**. Scale is turning into efficiency, not bureaucracy.

As for negative free cash flow, his question would not be "has cash flow turned positive" but "can the money spent be recovered". Here he needs one answer: AWS backlog of USD 578 billion, triple-digit growth year on year, with capacity locked through 2027–2028. **The order book is evidence that the money can be recovered, not a promise.** So he would most likely say: the business is understandable, the price is not cheap, buy in tranches.

## Buffett: A perfect moat, but returns on capital at a discount

Buffett would start with the nature of the business. AWS customers move core systems to the cloud, and the cost of switching is "rewrite everything plus downtime risk," so pricing power sits with the supplier — the same logic as an insurer that collects premiums before paying claims: cash in first, service later. The 578 billion backlog is essentially a contractual version of "float."

He would then calculate return on capital, and here he would frown. Operating profit of 28.08 billion looks good, but free cash flow is -42.4 billion; long-term debt rose from 65.6 billion to 173.1 billion, and interest expense rose to 1.69 billion in a single quarter, up 214% year on year. **He accepted heavy assets when he bought the BNSF railway, because no one else can build the rail rights of way; but he would not accept a railway that must reinvest 300 billion every year just to sustain growth.** That is the difference between the two.

The one detail he would press on is depreciation: servers are depreciated over 5–6 years, and 220 billion of capex turns into tens of billions of depreciation a year in 2027–2028. The assumed useful life determines whether the profit is real. **"Is the profit real?" — that is why he would "hold" rather than "reject": he believes in the business, not in the durability of this income statement.**

## Munger: Push the scenario to the extreme, and the answer is "painful but not fatal"

Munger would not ask "will AI demand persist"; he would ask "what happens if it halves next year." That scenario can be modeled: AWS revenue growth falls from 39% to 15%, existing customers' routine workloads remain, and revenue does not collapse; but the data centers already built and the chips already bought must be depreciated over 5–6 years, and that cost is rigid, pushing AWS operating margin from 38.7% down to around 25%.

Translated to the group: AWS operating profit falls from RMB 17.78 billion to about RMB 11.5 billion, and group operating profit falls from RMB 28.08 billion to about RMB 21.8 billion, a decline of roughly 20%. Retail (RMB 8.72 billion) and advertising remain.**That is the value of a diversified business—it does not raise the ceiling, it lowers the floor.**

His second line of questioning is "is management clear-eyed." Jassy volunteered two unfavorable facts on the earnings call: AI services carry lower margins than the core cloud business, and rising prices for components such as storage are creating pressure. The CFO also acknowledged that spending will "increase significantly" in 2027.**Management willing to deliver bad news in its best quarter is usually more credible than management that only delivers good news.**Munger's conclusion would be: you can buy it, but not to the full position—because the ceiling on this business is now set by someone else's capital expenditure.

## Li Lu: building the railroad, selling the rails, and collecting the tolls — he occupies all three roles

In Li Lu's framework, civilization-scale technological shifts ultimately settle into a handful of infrastructure assets: in the railway era it was right-of-way, in the electricity era the grid, in the compute era the cloud. The cloud is closer to a toll booth than a railway — it has network effects, migration costs, and usage-based pricing.

The historical lesson also holds: most railway builders went bankrupt; the money was made selling rails and collecting land rent. Amazon unusually plays all three roles at once — **the railway builder** (self-built data centers, long-term power contracts), **the rail seller** (Graviton/Trainium, sold externally by end-2027), and **the toll collector** (AWS billed by token and instance). If even one of the three holds, this investment does not go to zero.

He would grant tolerance on valuation, but restraint on position size. The reason is common sense for Chinese investors: infrastructure asset returns are pushed toward the average over time by regulation and competition. Cloud competitors are Microsoft (Azure +48%) and Google Cloud (+103%); once a price war starts, the toll collector is cut first. **So he is willing to pay 25x, not 35x.**

## Odds: 1.63 to 1, normal odds for large-cap companies

Neutral USD 330 (+27%), bear case USD 216 (-17%), odds = (330-259.30)/(259.30-216) = 70.7/43.3 ≈ **1.63 to 1**. That number is unimpressive. A company with a USD 2.8 trillion market cap rarely offers 3 to 1, because its bad news is already fully priced and its good news fully discussed. **Anything above 1.5 is actionable — that is the reality of large caps.**

The real risk is not the multiple, it is the denominator. The bear case assumes 2027 EPS of USD 12, roughly flat with TTM 12.46 — this already embeds the assumption that depreciation eats growth. If depreciation is heavier than expected (say 2027 capex above USD 300 billion with unchanged useful lives), EPS could be just USD 10.8, and 20x P/E implies USD 216 — breaching the -17% floor. **So entry must be staged: build one third first, and reserve the rest of the position for the price after the depreciation data is published.**
