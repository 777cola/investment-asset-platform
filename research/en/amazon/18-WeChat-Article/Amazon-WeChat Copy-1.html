Release note

This is a ready-to-publish WeChat article: "Spending USD 77.2 billion in a single quarter: Amazon is betting on something that will only become visible in 2028." About 1,500 words. All data come from Amazon's Q3 2026 earnings report (October 29, 2026) and public reports, with no information after the report date.

USD 77.2 billion in a single quarter

On October 29, Amazon delivered its best income statement in thirty years. Operating profit was USD 28.08 billion, up 61% year on year; operating margin was 13.9%, the highest in company history; AWS grew 39.3%, accelerating for the sixth consecutive quarter; backlog stood at USD 578 billion, up triple digits year on year, with compute capacity for 2027 through 2028 already fully booked by customers.

The same earnings report contains another number: in a single quarter, USD 77.26 billion went to buying land, building facilities and purchasing chips. That is up 120% year on year. Free cash flow over the past twelve months was negative USD 42.4 billion.

Put these two numbers together and you have the whole Amazon story today.

First, the good news: this is not financial engineering, it is real capability

Break it into its three segments and the picture is clear.

AWS posted USD 45.98 billion in quarterly revenue and USD 17.78 billion in operating income, a 38.7% margin—it alone generates more than 60% of group profit. North America retail revenue rose just 6%, yet operating income jumped from USD 4.79 billion to USD 8.72 billion, lifting the margin from 4.3% to 7.7%. The international segment came in at 3.7%.

The most valuable line is buried in the details: global unit volume rose 12% while shipping costs rose only 11%; strip out fuel prices and line-haul rate increases and shipping costs rose just 8%, 400 basis points below volume growth. In plain terms—inventory sits closer to customers, more packages are consolidated, and the same spend moves more orders.

Fulfillment cost per unit fell for a fourth consecutive year. That is the hardest thing to achieve in the grueling retail business, and the real reason North America margin recovered from 4.3% to 7.7%. Not pricing, but efficiency.

The cost, then: not enough money, so borrow

Capital expenditure of RMB 77.26 billion consumed all operating cash flow and still fell short. Full-year 2026 guidance is RMB 220 billion, and management says 2027 will "increase significantly" further. UBS raised its 2027 estimate from RMB 250 billion to over RMB 300 billion before the earnings release; Deutsche Bank puts it at RMB 330 billion.

Where does the money come from? Borrowing. Long-term debt rose from RMB 65.6 billion to RMB 173.1 billion within a year, and quarterly interest expense climbed from RMB 538 million to RMB 1.691 billion, up 214% year on year.

One thing needs to be clear: negative free cash flow does not mean the business is deteriorating. Amazon's operating cash flow over the trailing twelve months was RMB 168.3 billion, up 28.7% year on year — the business is generating cash. Capital expenditure is what consumes it. And that spending is almost entirely expansionary — data centers, chips, power — not patchwork maintenance, with capacity already booked by customers two years out.

Spending that can be stopped is not rigid spending. That is the most fundamental difference between this and "burning cash for growth."

Why the share price hasn't risen: earnings outpace the stock

Closed at USD 259.30 on November 6, market cap USD 2.80 trillion, P/E 20.8x. That is only 8.7% off the 52-week high of USD 284.02 and still 30% above the low of USD 198.79.

The message from this level: the market is neither panicking nor offering a discount. Operating profit rose 61% and the P/E is still 20x — not because the market missed it, but because it dares not award a higher multiple while cash flow is negative.

But the 20.8x figure has a trap. In the first three quarters of 2026, Amazon's "other income" was USD 68.359 billion, 46% of pre-tax profit, mainly appreciation on its Anthropic stake. Strip that out and the net margin falls from 19.4% to 10.4%, and the P/E goes from 20.8x to 34x.

So "Amazon is only 20x, what a bargain" folds Anthropic's valuation into Amazon's profit. The reality: at 34x, buying a company with AWS growing 40% is expensive for good reason, but there is no bargain to be had.

The real knife: depreciation, landing in 2027

USD 220 billion of capex does not all become expense in the current period. It becomes an asset, then depreciates over 5 to 6 years. Broker estimates put the average annual incremental depreciation from new compute assets added in 2026 at about USD 40 billion.

"Technology and infrastructure" costs already rose 20.2% this quarter, the fastest-growing line in the expense table. And this is only the beginning.

In 2027, if capex really reaches USD 300 billion to 330 billion, depreciation takes another big step up. Whether the 13.9% operating margin holds then depends on whether AWS revenue growth outruns depreciation growth.

Jassy said on the call that AWS growth will exceed 40% in both 2027 and 2028. If that lands, today's capex is the best money spent; if it does not, the USD 220 billion becomes a rock on the income statement. Depreciation does not wait; revenue does.

One more uncomfortable fact: AWS is no longer the fastest cloud.

In the same month, all three major clouds reported: Google Cloud revenue was RMB 30.7 billion, up 103% year on year; Microsoft Azure rose 48%; AWS rose 39.3%.

AWS is still the largest, at RMB 46 billion a quarter, or RMB 184 billion annualized. But it is no longer the fastest. Six consecutive quarters of acceleration is its own best performance in five years; within the industry, it is losing share.

The reason is not complicated: from 2023 to 2025, cloud growth came from "enterprises moving to the cloud," which was AWS's turf; in 2026, growth comes from AI training and inference, where the model partners matter. Microsoft has OpenAI, Google has its own Gemini and TPU, and Amazon is tied to both — it is a major shareholder in Anthropic and has signed a USD 50 billion partnership with OpenAI.

The strategy is right, but it means part of AWS's growth depends on other companies' product roadmaps.

So how to buy: one-third, wait for three numbers

My answer: buy, in tranches, starting with one third.

Three valuation scenarios: bear case USD 216 (AWS growth falls to 25%, depreciation squeezes profit, 2027 EPS of USD 12, at 18x); base case USD 330 (AWS holds 40%, retail margin 7%, EPS of USD 15, at 22x); bull case USD 438 (AWS above 45%, advertising back to high-teens growth, EPS of USD 17.5, at 25x). Reward-to-risk 1.6 to 1.

For a USD 2.8 trillion company, that payoff is not spectacular, but it is enough. So build one third of the position first and hold the rest back for three numbers: Q1 2027 capex guidance, depreciation data, AWS growth.

If all three numbers hold, fill the position. If AWS growth drops below 30%, or backlog falls sequentially for two consecutive quarters, exit — watch the facts, not the price.

The last sentence.

Amazon is using USD 77.2 billion of today's cash to buy 2028's compute.

If it is right, earnings will catch up to the 20x P/E; if it is wrong, the USD 220 billion becomes depreciation weighing on the income statement.

You do not need to judge whether it is right. You only need to judge which outcome you can bear—and then decide how much to buy.
