Core view

For thirty years Amazon has carried three labels, and all three have now expired: low-margin retailer, slowing growth, capex as a burden. All three held in 2021; all three are wrong in 2026. Until the perception resets, the valuation will never be right.

First impression: not a retailer but "a tech company that acquires customers through retail"

The profit pool is unambiguous: AWS generates USD 45.98 billion of annual revenue and USD 17.78 billion of operating profit, 63.3% of the group total. North America retail turns USD 112.85 billion of revenue into just USD 8.72 billion of profit (7.7%), while international contributes USD 1.58 billion on USD 43.11 billion (3.7%). Retail is the gateway; the profit sits in technology and advertising.

That mix dictates the valuation approach. Amazon cannot be valued at Walmart's 25x, nor at retail's 10x, when 63% of profit comes from a business with a 38.7% gross margin. Equally, a cloud company's 40x does not apply either, because 77% of revenue is still retail.

Impression 2 and 3: growth is back, but this time it was bought with spending

In 2022, Amazon's revenue growth briefly fell to single digits, and the market concluded that "an elephant cannot dance." In the first three quarters of 2026, revenue grew 16% and AWS grew 39.3%—growth is back. But the way it came back is different this time: the last recovery rode pandemic tailwinds; this one rests on USD 220 billion of annual capex.

So the third impression must be reset too—capex is no longer a "mild burden" but the company's most important operating variable. Quarterly capex of USD 77.26 billion, up 120% YoY, pushed TTM free cash flow to negative USD 42.4 billion, while long-term debt rose from USD 65.6 billion to USD 173.1 billion. In other words, its growth has a price, and that price is written on the cash flow statement.

The takeaway from this reset: view Amazon as a company "transitioning from asset-light to asset-heavy." For an asset-light company, watch free cash flow; for an asset-heavy one, watch return on capital and capacity utilisation. Use the wrong yardstick and you will buy at the wrong price.

The overlooked fourth figure: the international segment

All eyes are on AWS; nobody is watching International. Revenue of USD 43.11 billion, operating income of USD 1.58 billion, a 3.7% margin. Five years ago that number was negative. International was once Amazon's profit black hole, with prolonged losses in Europe, Japan and India; it now delivers steady positive profit, albeit thin.

What matters here is not the profit but the headroom. Jassy notes that nearly 80% of global retail still takes place in physical stores, and the main battleground for rising online penetration is Europe, Japan, India and Brazil. A 3.7% margin cuts both ways: there is ample room for improvement (North America went from 4.3% to 7.7% over several years), yet any swing in oil prices or exchange rates would push the segment back into loss.

So Amazon's retail story cannot be read through North America alone. North America is a factory already retooled; International is a factory just breaking ground—the former supplies profit, the latter supplies optionality.

Remember the new profile with three numbers

13.9% operating margin, a record high. 39.3% AWS growth, the fastest in five years. -RMB 42.4 billion of free cash flow, the worst in a decade. The first shows it can make money, the second that it can still grow, the third that it is spending. Only with all three together do you get today's Amazon; any one on its own is a misreading.

Why the market misremembers: anchored to 2021

One psychological factor: most investors formed their view of Amazon in 2021–2022, when operating margin was just 2%–5%, free cash flow was positive but thin, and growth leaned on pandemic tailwinds. The stock fell 50% over that period, and the impression stuck: "low-quality growth."

The 2026 financials tell a different story: operating margin of 13.9%, AWS at 63% of profit, and retail efficiency up from 4.3% to 7.7%.Yet the market's valuation anchor hasn't moved—20.8x, a multiple for a "retailer," not for "cloud + advertising."

That is why opportunity and risk coexist. If the market re-rates Amazon to a "cloud company" multiple, the stock revalues; if it stays anchored to a "retailer" multiple, profit growth can only come through EPS, at just over 20% a year.Neither scenario loses money, but the upside differs by a factor of two.

Conclusion

The reset picture: a high-margin, fast-growing company that is getting heavier. The valuation anchor is no longer "retail PS", nor even "cloud PS", but "segment profit × segment multiple − the depreciation cost of capex". Three lines to remember: retail is the gateway, cloud is the profit, capex is the price.
