② Nature of the business: three revenue streams, three customer types, one infrastructure — Amazon

Break the three business segments down to unit economics, and only then does it become clear why it can earn money three different ways at once.

Core view

Amazon's business is essentially selling one set of infrastructure three times: to consumers (retail), to sellers (marketplace and advertising), and to enterprises (cloud). The same logistics network, the same data centers, the same engineers, three ways to charge for them.

Why it can earn three kinds of money at once: because the customers are not the same group of people

Retail's customers are consumers, who want "cheap + fast + complete". The platform's customers are sellers, who want "traffic + fulfillment". The cloud's customers are enterprises, who want "compute + stability". These three customer types have completely different needs, yet share the same set of assets: the logistics network serves the first two, the data centers serve the third, and advertising sells the first group's traffic to brands.

This is why its moat is "interlocking" rather than single-point. Look at logistics alone, and UPS can do it too; look at cloud alone, and Microsoft can do it too; look at advertising alone, and Google is stronger. But no company can simultaneously use retail's cash flow to fund the cloud, use the cloud's scale to lower technology costs, and use membership stickiness to sell advertising.

Cost: all three types of spending draw on the same balance sheet

The benefit of this structure is synergy; the drawback is no risk isolation. Retail cash flow is diverted to build data centers, and if cloud demand weakens, retail cannot get that money back — it has already become steel and chips. That is why free cash flow swung from +RMB 14.8 billion to -RMB 42.4 billion: not because retail is unprofitable, but because the money retail earns is spent by cloud.

2026Q3 figures: operating cash flow RMB 168.3 billion (TTM), capex RMB 210.7 billion (TTM). Look at retail alone and it is a money printer; look at the group as a whole and it is a factory under expansion. Buyers must accept the latter.

Why advertising is the most profitable piece

The company never discloses advertising profit separately, but its scale can be estimated. Advertising revenue is RMB 19.64 billion per quarter; the marginal cost of this business is only servers and algorithm headcount, and the industry-wide operating margin is 40%–50%. At a rough 45%, advertising contributes about RMB 8.8 billion of operating profit per quarter—more than North America retail (RMB 8.72 billion), and close to half of AWS.

What does this mean? It means group profit actually rests on three pillars: AWS at RMB 17.78 billion, advertising at about RMB 8.8 billion, and retail at RMB 10.3 billion combined. The three pillars have completely different stability—AWS depends on customer capex, advertising depends on retail traffic, and retail depends on fulfillment efficiency. The probability of all three collapsing at once is very low, which is why it is steadier than a pure cloud company.

Note this is a rough estimate, not company data. But even at a conservative 35% margin, advertising still contributes RMB 6.9 billion, still a quarter of group profit. Any analysis that ignores advertising will underestimate this company's earning power.

Three profit pools, three fates

AWS's USD 17.78 billion rests on customer capex, advertising's roughly USD 8.8 billion (rough estimate) on retail traffic, and retail's USD 10.3 billion on fulfillment efficiency. The three are driven by entirely different factors — the fundamental reason it is steadier than single-business companies, and the reason it cannot be simply benchmarked. Valuing it requires valuing each separately.

The other half of retail: RMB 5.7 billion in physical stores

Physical stores (Whole Foods and others) did USD 5.71 billion in the quarter, +2% year over year, or 2.8% of total revenue. That number is small enough to ignore, but it reveals a strategic intent: Amazon does not intend to be online-only; it will use physical stores to solve the "last three miles" of fresh groceries.

Fresh groceries are the hardest e-commerce category: low ticket size, high spoilage, and delivery costs that eat a large share of revenue. So it uses physical stores as forward warehouses, paired with same-day delivery: customers who use same-day fresh grocery buy seven times as many everyday goods as ordinary users. Once this chain works, groceries shift from a "loss-making category" to a "high-frequency entry point."

The test is simple: watch whether physical-store revenue growth can rise from 2% to above 5%. If it cannot, the grocery strategy is still stuck at "subsidies for traffic"; if it can, the fulfillment model has truly taken hold.

Conclusion

Judging the nature of the business: this is a two-way business that "subsidizes cloud with retail traffic and feeds retail back with cloud profits." Its ceiling is set by cloud, its floor by retail. This also explains why its share price will neither be beaten down to 10x like pure retail nor bid up to 40x like pure cloud.
