⑥ Management: one engineer, one engineered approach to capital allocation — Amazon

Jassy is not a professional manager; he is the builder of AWS.
Research date 2026/11/06  ·  Price $259.30 (-0.35%)  ·  Market cap About USD 2.80 trillion  ·  PE(TTM) Approximately 20.8x

Core view

Judging management takes only two questions: does he understand the business, and where is his money. Amazon's answer: he understands it well, but his money is not in it. The first explains why it dares to spend RMB 220 billion; the second explains why it does not care about the short-term share price.

Capability: he talks engineering detail, not vision

Jassy joined Amazon in 1997 in marketing, built AWS from scratch in 2006, and became AWS CEO in 2016. That résumé means every statement he makes on the call has a corresponding engineering implementation: one AI accelerator installed every 14 seconds, Graviton 30%–40% better price-performance than comparable chips, open-source TorchNeuron so customers need not rewrite code, and orders for the prior-generation Graviton instances doubling quarter over quarter.

Whoever can explain clearly that "demand for the prior-generation chips remains strong" is someone who understands depreciation and residual value. That matters enormously for a company converting RMB 22 billion into fixed assets—a CEO who does not understand hardware will stumble on depreciation.

Capital allocation: no buybacks, no dividends, borrowing to build plants

Amazon's capital allocation style has not changed in thirty years: no dividend, no meaningful buybacks, all earnings reinvested. The 2026 version is: RMB 220 billion in capex, long-term debt raised from RMB 65.6 billion to RMB 173.1 billion, and USD 11.5 billion for Globalstar spectrum. This is a "we believe our reinvestment returns exceed shareholders' opportunity cost" approach.

The only yardstick for judging whether this approach is right is the return rate. ROIC is currently about 15% (rough calculation) against a cost of capital of about 8%, a 7-point excess. But the denominator is expanding at RMB 77.2 billion per quarter, and the numerator will take two to three years to catch up—so ROIC will fall first in 2027; that is an accounting certainty, not operational deterioration.

No governance blemishes: no accounting restatements, no SEC enforcement, unqualified audit opinions. The only things to watch are depreciation-life assumptions and Bezos's continued selling (8.2% is already a historical low).

Succession and reduction: two slow variables

Jassy was born in 1968 and is 58; no public succession plan exists. Two internal candidates: AWS CEO Matt Garman (runs the profit pool) and Worldwide Stores CEO Doug Herrington (runs the traffic pool). The upside of this structure is that neither successor would trigger an immediate loss of control; the downside is that there is no "all-round" heir.

More worth watching is Bezos's stake. 884 million shares, 8.2%, continuously sold down under a 10b5-1 plan, already at a historical low. His selling does not change operations, but it changes market psychology—the "founder long-termism" premium is sustained by him sitting in the chairman's seat.

One more detail worth remembering: Jassy's total 2025 compensation was about USD 2.1 million, of which base salary was RMB 365,000, with the rest mainly stock and security. For someone managing a USD 2.8 trillion company and deploying USD 220 billion of capex a year, this pay structure shows his incentive comes from equity appreciation, not cash. That is a good thing, but equity accounts for only 0.02%, and the absolute size of the incentive is still on the small side.

How should we view this management team?

Three criteria: Does he understand hardware (yes, AWS background); will he deliver bad news (yes, volunteered that AI margins are lower); is his money in the company (no, 0.02%). The first two score high, the third scores low — that is where the 7/10 comes from.

Conclusion

Management score 7/10: capability 9, integrity 8, alignment 4. This combination means the company will make decisions that are "right for the long term, ugly for the short term," and it also means shareholders must use their own position discipline to hedge against the fact that "management does not care about the share price."
