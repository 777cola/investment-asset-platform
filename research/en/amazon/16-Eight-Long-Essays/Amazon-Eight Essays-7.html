⑦ Risk checklist: six risks, two of them fatal — Amazon

Split risk into two buckets: "will fluctuate" and "will go to zero"

Core view

Amazon carries no risk of going to zero: USD 168.3 billion of operating cash flow, a USD 578 billion backlog and three cash-generating businesses. Its risks fall into two buckets — those that can halve the stock price (valuation and cycle), and those that can invalidate the thesis (demand and accounting). The first is managed through position sizing, the second through discipline.

Two paper-grade risks: demand and accounting

Demand risk is about timing, not direction. The bear case is not "nobody uses AI" but "adoption runs a year slower than expected." Amazon has already built the capacity, bought the chips and signed the power contracts, and those costs are amortised on a straight-line basis over 5–6 years regardless of demand swings. Jassy guided AWS growth above 40% in both 2027 and 2028, tying the company to a very steep curve—if actual growth comes in at 25% rather than 40%, the depreciation share spirals out of control.

Accounting risk is about depreciation lives. Servers are depreciated over 5–6 years, with no change disclosed this quarter. But this is an assumption where "changing it isn't illegal, and not changing it isn't wrong either." If the company extends the lives in any given quarter, current-period profit is instantly flattered by several billion while cash flow rises not a cent. The real damage to investors: you value the stock on the wrong denominator and still think you bought it cheap.

Two price-level risks determine whether you get shaken out along the way

Valuation compression is the most likely risk and the only one that can be quantified. It has already played out once, in November 2026: Nvidia fell 14% in a single day and CoreWeave retreated more than 70% from its high. As the largest spender on AI capex, Amazon would not be immune on such a day. 15x TTM implies USD 187, or -28%.

Competitive price cuts are a chronic risk. Every 5% cut by AWS costs the group 2.8 percentage points of margin. If Google Cloud's TPU drives down cost per unit of compute, AWS will be forced to match — not within a year, but the pressure on its 38.7% margin will persist over three to five years.

The fix is simple and hard to execute: size the position small enough that a 28% drop does not impair your judgment. That is not conservatism; it is what keeps discipline working when emotion arrives.

The shadow of a sixth risk: if AI demand is a "bubble"

Take the worst case to its limit: assume AI training demand falls off a cliff in 2027 — not just slower growth, but an absolute decline. What happens?

First layer: the AI-related portion of AWS revenue (inference already accounts for more than half of AI revenue) starts to be returned, but the core cloud business (compute, storage, database) will not be returned — that is enterprises' everyday workload. So the floor for revenue is the scale of core cloud, not zero.

Second layer: capacity already built sits idle while depreciation continues to be taken. AWS operating margin falls from 38.7% to 25% or even 20%, and group operating margin falls from 13.9% to below 10%. This triggers a double hit to valuation: EPS cut + multiple cut, and the share price could fall to around USD 150.

Third layer: financing. Long-term debt of RMB 173.1 billion, annualized interest expense of about RMB 6.8 billion, and if cash flow remains negative, more borrowing will be needed. This is the only path that could threaten the company's credit rating, but given operating cash flow of RMB 168.3 billion, the probability is extremely low.

Algorithm for the minimum tolerable position size

Assume a worst case of a 48% decline (USD 135). How much portfolio drawdown can you tolerate? If the answer is 2%, the position cap is 2% ÷ 48% ≈ 4%. Work out this number first, then decide how much to buy—reverse the order and you will be forced to sell into the decline.

Conclusion

Risk conclusion: bearable, not ignorable. Of the six risks, two are thesis-level (demand cadence, depreciation), two are price-level (valuation, price cuts), one is balance-sheet-level (debt) and one is tail (regulation). The response comes down to three points: cut the position to one-third, set the stop-loss on backlog rather than price, and rerun earnings if depreciation schedules change.
