③ Moats dissected: four gears, one mesh — Amazon

A moat is not four separate pools; it is a single transmission chain

Core view

Amazon's moat is often broken down into four parallel pillars: logistics, membership, cloud, chips. That is wrong. They form a single transmission chain—the output of each link is the input of the next, and if any link breaks, the whole chain stalls.

Why the transmission chain: three cross-checks

Intersection one: logistics efficiency underpins membership pricing. Membership fees rose and churn did not spike because the "next-day delivery" promise held — 1.5 billion units delivered same-day or next-day in a single quarter, with Amazon Now covering more than 450 towns and cities. If logistics efficiency slips, membership fees stop rising.

Intersection two: membership traffic underpins the advertising premium. Advertising revenue of RMB 19.64 billion, growing 12%, rests on the transactional intent of "people come here ready to buy." That intent comes from retail, not content. Advertising's ceiling is therefore set by retail traffic.

Intersection three: retail and advertising cash flow fund the cloud. Where does the RMB 220 billion of 2026 capex come from? Operating cash flow of RMB 168.3 billion plus RMB 107.5 billion of debt. Without retail margin improving from 4.3% to 7.7%, that RMB 220 billion could not be borrowed. The three intersections confirm: the value of this moat lies not in any single point but in transmission efficiency.

Four weak points: where the chain could break

Weak point one: the low-price segment. Temu and Shein use price to peel away price-sensitive buyers, and these buyers are part of order density. Once density falls, cost per order rises — this is the most fragile link in the chain.

Weak point two: new incremental customers. Switching costs protect the installed base, not the increment. On a new customer's first workload, AWS has no advantage, while Azure competes on enterprise relationships and GCP on model capability.

Weak point three: power. Data centers can be built; grid connections take three to five years of queuing. On this link it stands in the same queue as every cloud vendor.

Weak point four: chips. Trainium matches Nvidia in inference scenarios, but training remains Nvidia's domain, and external sales do not begin until the end of 2027 — this link is still under construction.

Moat maintenance cost: RMB 220 billion a year

Most moats are built once and then collect rent indefinitely: Coca-Cola's formula, Moutai's fermentation pits, an exchange's licence. Amazon's moat is different — it requires USD 220 billion a year to maintain.

Where does the money go? Data centres (the physical substrate of AI compute), in-house chips (the source of the cost advantage), power contracts (the precondition for bringing capacity online), satellite spectrum (the next entry point). Stop spending for a year and the moat does not collapse immediately, but after two years AWS capacity can no longer keep up with customers, and after three years rivals catch up on unit cost.

This creates a valuation contradiction: a business that needs sustained heavy capital investment just to hold its advantage is inherently low on free cash flow. Value it on DCF and you get a very low present value; value it on EV/EBIT and you ignore capex. Neither method is perfect, which is why its valuation range is so wide (15–25x).

The moat's enemy is not the competitor, but the reversal of efficiency

The biggest risk to a transmission chain is not a single competitor getting stronger, but efficiency starting to decline in one of its own links. The test is specific: once freight cost growth exceeds volume growth for two consecutive quarters, the chain begins to reverse. 2026Q3 was -400bp (freight slower than volume), and that number matters more than any market share data.

Conclusion

Moat score 41/50: four strong, one moderate. Its strength depends on "no single link failing," which is the essential difference from a Coca-Cola-style moat that is "overwhelmingly strong at a single point." The benefit of a transmission chain is synergy; the drawback is compounding fragility. Investment implication: a premium is acceptable, but not a "perpetual" premium.
