⑤ Industry and competition: still the largest, but no longer the fastest — Amazon

Three numbers that changed the AWS narrative

Core view

In Q3 2026, the three major clouds grew as follows: Google Cloud +103%, Azure +48%, AWS +39.3%. AWS remains the largest at USD 46 billion, but its growth ranks last. That single sentence is enough to rewrite the entire industry narrative—AWS has gone from "the definer of cloud" to "the king of cloud's installed base."

Why AWS Has Slowed: The Demand Mix Has Changed

From 2023 to 2025, cloud growth came from "enterprises moving to the cloud," and AWS's customer mix (internet-native companies, SMEs, startups) benefited most. In 2026, growth comes from "AI training and inference," where the decisive factor is the model partner: Microsoft is tied to OpenAI (the exclusivity period ends in April 2026), while Google has its own Gemini and TPU.

Amazon's response is to "embrace both": it is both Anthropic's largest outside shareholder (and the source of USD 68.3 billion in unrealized gains) and, in 2026, struck a USD 50 billion-scale partnership with OpenAI — GPT-6 Astra and Bedrock Managed Agents are already live on AWS, and Fortune 500 spending on Bedrock rose nearly 7x year over year. This is the right strategy, but it means AWS's growth partly depends on someone else's product roadmap.

E-commerce battlefield: share is still rising, but the shape of growth has changed

The retail story is less dramatic but more solid. Paid unit sales rose 12%, close to 15% year to date, on top of a base of over 30 billion units last year; new quarterly sales in the US were roughly twice those of the second-largest competitor. First-party online grew just 2%, with growth coming from third-party sellers (+9%) and advertising (+12%).

Grocery is the new battleground: fresh food and daily essentials grew three times faster than other categories and account for about half of unit sales; Amazon Pharmacy customers rose 60%; same-day grocery customers buy seven times as many daily essentials as ordinary customers. The strategic value of grocery lies not in gross margin but in frequency—it turns "once a week" into "three times a week," raising order density and continuing to lower cost per order, which is precisely the input end of the moat's transmission chain.

The advertising battlefield: it is going after search dollars

The third front is the easiest to overlook: advertising. RMB 19.64 billion per quarter, +12%, approaching RMB 80 billion for the full year. This revenue competes directly with Google search advertising, because the logic for brands is "where can I reach people who are ready to buy."

Amazon's advantage is the closed loop: users search, compare, order and review all on-site, and advertisers can see the full path from impression to sale. Google's advantage is reach: when users have not yet decided what to buy, their search behavior sits with Google. So the two companies' revenue does not fully overlap — Amazon captures the "late decision stage," Google captures the "early decision stage."

This also explains growth rates: when consumption is weak, brands shift budget from "seeding" (early stage) to "harvesting" (late stage), benefiting Amazon; when the new-product cycle is strong, early-stage budgets rise faster, benefiting Google. The 12% growth in Q3 2026 was on the weak side, tied to the Prime Day shift and a high prior-year base, and management guides fourth quarter back to high-teens.

Track just one metric: the growth gap between AWS and Azure

The gap now stands at 9 percentage points (39.3% vs 48%). Narrowing to within 5 points would signal AWS is winning back incremental share; widening beyond 15 points would signal systematic share loss. This spread matters more than AWS's own growth rate because it strips out overall industry swings.

The hidden shift in logistics: it is no longer just its own courier company

A rarely discussed fact: Amazon Logistics is already one of the largest parcel networks in the US, delivering 1.5 billion same-day or next-day packages per quarter. Its unit-cost advantage comes from density—same routes, more parcels.

The impact on the industry is structural: UPS and FedEx have lost some e-commerce parcel volume to it, and what remains is "high-value, low-density" freight (enterprise parcels, cold chain, bulky items). So the logistics battle is no longer decided on price, but on "who has higher density."

This also explains why its retail margin has recovered to 7.7%: not by charging more, but by turning the "last mile" from a cost center into an efficiency center. For rivals to replicate this, they need more than money—they need ten years and a density of tens of millions of orders a day. This is the moat I consider hardest to breach, harder than cloud.

Conclusion

Competitive conclusion: cloud is losing growth, e-commerce is gaining share, advertising is holding the line. The most important of the three trends is the cloud growth gap — it determines how long the 38.7% operating margin can hold. If AWS growth falls below 30% in 2027 while Azure stays above 40%, the profit pool of this business will start to leak.
