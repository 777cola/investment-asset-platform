## This team's edge is its ability to turn heavy assets into engineering

Jassy's résumé explains the company's character. He joined in 1997 in marketing, built AWS from scratch in 2006 and became AWS CEO in 2016—not a professional manager but someone who has personally built infrastructure. So when he said on the call that "one AI accelerator is installed every 14 seconds," that "Graviton offers 30%–40% better price-performance than comparable chips" and that "open-sourcing TorchNeuron lets customers avoid rewriting code," those remarks carried engineering detail, not PR copy.

Olsavsky's role is to translate the story into numbers: growth 400 basis points higher after excluding the Prime Day timing shift, shipping costs up only 8% excluding fuel, AI services margins below the core cloud business, and a significant increase in spending in 2027. A CFO willing to volunteer in the best quarter that "AI margins are lower" is worth more than a hundred lines of "vast prospects."

Garman took over AWS in 2024, just as this capacity race hit, and what he has pushed during his tenure is a combination of "in-house chips + long-term power contracts + regionalized data centers." That route runs opposite to Microsoft's (which relies more on third-party compute) and is management's active choice, not passive herd-following.

## Capital allocation with teeth: no buybacks, no dividends, borrowing to buy compute

Amazon's capital allocation has never endeared it to shareholders. No dividend, no meaningful buybacks, and in 2026 the entire USD 220 billion of cash flow went into data centers, chips, power and satellites — still not enough, so long-term debt rose from USD 65.6 billion to USD 173.1 billion. The signal is unambiguous: management believes the return on compute exceeds its weighted cost of capital. If that judgment is right, shareholders earn compounding; if wrong, they absorb the depreciation and interest.

Orders are the first piece of evidence. AWS backlog stands at USD 578 billion, up triple digits YoY, with capacity locked in by customers through 2027–2028. Payback period is the second: Jassy says Amazon is "continually working to reduce the time it takes for infrastructure investments to break even," while conceding a returns gap between core cloud and AI infrastructure — in plain terms, "AI returns are lower than core cloud," minus the numbers.

The satellite purchase is another telling data point. USD 11.5 billion for Globalstar's spectrum, cleared by the FCC on October 27, committed at a moment when free cash flow is negative, shows management betting on Leo as "a second AWS" (in Jassy's words, two businesses each generating USD 1 trillion in annual revenue within 5–10 years). That kind of bet favors long-term shareholders and weighs on the near-term stock price.

## Governance: no scandals, but two areas to watch

The integrity record is clean: no financial fraud, no major accounting restatements, no SEC enforcement cases. EY is auditor, with unqualified opinions stretching back many years. On antitrust, the FTC lawsuit filed in 2023 is still proceeding—an industry-wide risk rather than a corporate governance event. A record this clean is uncommon in this industry and deserves credit.

The first thing to watch is depreciation lives. Servers are depreciated over 5–6 years—the accounting assumption most easily "optimized": extend it by one year and current-period profit immediately looks better while cash flow is unchanged. Broker estimates put average annual incremental depreciation on new compute assets added in 2026 at about USD 40 billion, so any change in useful life would scale to the billions. See Note 1 to the annual report.

The second is the shareholding structure. Jassy holds 0.02% and 1.05 million unvested RSUs, roughly USD 600 million combined—wholly out of proportion to the scope of his decision-making influence; Bezos holds 8.2% but does not participate in operations. Institutional ownership is about 76% (Vanguard 7.8%, BlackRock 6.9%). Management's incentives therefore come mainly from annual stock awards, not from "putting their own money on the table." That is not a flaw, but it explains why management more readily chooses "long-term expansion" over "short-term cash flow."

## Succession: not a problem now, but a problem in five years

Born in 1968, Jassy turns 58 in 2026, his fifth year as CEO, with no publicly disclosed succession plan. The most likely internal successors are AWS CEO Garman and Worldwide Stores CEO Herrington—one runs the profit pool, the other the traffic pool. The upside of this structure is that neither would cause an immediate loss of control if either took over; the downside is that there is no "all-round" heir.

The real risk is not succession but Bezos's stake. He keeps selling under a 10b5-1 plan, and 8.2% is already a historic low. If he cuts his stake below 5% over the next five years, the company's "founder premium" will disappear and the market's tolerance for long-termism will decline—no small matter for a company burning RMB 220 billion in cash.
